## Findings

### 1. Applied: the contrabassoon no longer doubles the basses under the p lament (107–109); it enters ff on C1 at 110

Bars 107–109. The chromatic lament bass F–E–E♭–D–D♭–C starts p and crescendos to the ff V7 of F at 110. It was scored cellos + Bn 2 (F3), Bn 1 two octaves up (F4, c327), basses F2, and the contrabassoon also on F2, in unison with the basses. Rimsky-Korsakov's rule for a low melody that needs a soft tone (PDF 78–79, Ex. 91, Tsar Saltan: violas + bassoon, cellos + bassoon, basses alone) is that the cellos take the bassoon and the basses stay undoubled; a reed at the unison with the basses at p adds buzz, not line. His crescendo method (PDF 129–130) and the loud entries of the contrabassoon in this range (Ex. 87, Kashtchei, C-fag. entering ff at the climax; Ex. 98, Saltan, C-fag. ff with the tutti) put that instrument at the goal. The contrabassoon now rests 107–109 (it last played at 94) and enters ff on its existing C1 at 110, so the climax gains its 16-foot octave as a new sound while the crescendo starts lighter.

### 2. Holistic comparison with Tchaikovsky 5/iv: the plan holds; no change

The large plan matches the master in the points that matter. Tchaikovsky turns the motto to major in the strings and brass at the start and at the Moderato assai; this finale reserves its major motto for the arrival and chorale (223–234, 255–262), which is the stronger choice for a symphony whose earlier movements did not use the motto in major. Tchaikovsky's B-major half close with a general pause before the coda corresponds to the stop chord B–D–F–A♭ held at 222 and resolved at 223 (owed item). His last stage brings movement I's main theme back in the brass in major over running strings; 247–254 does the same with both of movement I's theme heads, and 263–264 has the running strings. Two differences are deliberate and protected: this finale ends broadening (Largamente 273, the scherzo quotation, fff chord with fermata) where Tchaikovsky ends Presto with four hammered chords; and it is about 7 minutes against Tchaikovsky's 12. In orchestral craft the score now uses every device of this reading range that has a place (table above). A conductor would accept 223–279 as professional late-Romantic writing. The weakest point relative to Tchaikovsky is still melodic: F1 lacks the march-like rhythmic profile of Tchaikovsky's Allegro vivace theme, but F1's head bar is protected and has been reviewed many times; I make no proposal.
