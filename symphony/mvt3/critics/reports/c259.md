## Findings

### 1. Bar 20: the new v6 has no root on its downbeat (applied)

**Bars.** 20, beats 1-2.

**Problem.** Cycle 257 reharmonised F1's consequent so that the bass falls E♭-C | B♭-A♭-F | G (v6 - iv6/5 - iv7 - V7). On 20.1 the only pitches that sound are the tune's B♭ in five octaves (Fl 1 B♭5, Ob B♭4, Vln I·II, Hn 1·2 B♭3, Bn 2 and Vc B♭2, Cb B♭1) and D4 (Cl 1, violas). The root G enters only as Hn 4's staccato off-beat G3, mf, on 20.2, under an f string tune. On the strong beat the listener hears the bare third B♭-D, with the chord's third doubled from top to bottom. In v249 this beat was VII6, a complete triad. This is the one place where the last ten cycles made the sound worse.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 152 (Ex. V-27): in a first-inversion chord the bass (the third) should be doubled "near the bottom", leaving "the 'open' sound so characteristic of this inversion intact". Adler gives the opposite layout as "not good". PDF 257 (Ex. VIII-25) repeats the rule for winds: the third of a first inversion is doubled "toward the bottom rather than the top", and the other layout is marked "bad". Because the tune must have B♭ on top, the open sound has to come from the inner voices, and here they supply no G.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-259/work).** In pOne.ily, pOneClOne bar 20 (the tenth bar of the variable): `d'2( es'2)` becomes `g'2( es'2)`. Cl 1 holds G4, the last eighth of its bar-19 figure, and falls to E♭4, the iv6/5's seventh, on beat 3. The violas keep D4 and Hn 4 keeps its G3, so 20.1 is now B♭-D-G. This adds no new parallels: par8.py over 19-21 reports only the existing octave doublings of the bass in Bn 2 and Vc. Clarinet against bass is a sixth then a fifth (G/B♭ → E♭/A♭), and against the tune it moves by contrary step. Bar 160 is not affected (c257 left it as VII6). No theme note is changed.

### 2. Holistic comparison with Tchaikovsky 4/iv: a conductor would accept the craft (no change)

Tchaikovsky's finale gets its force from three things: (a) a second theme that returns many times, each time scored differently (Adler PDF 238 and 255); (b) unison and octave tuttis that clear the ground before harmonised blocks (Adler PDF 139-140); and (c) a coda over a long tonic pedal. This score has all three. F2 returns as trumpet solo (51), violins in octaves over the harp (67), bassoon in the development (115), muted trumpet with cor anglais (171) and violins with oboe and horn (187). F1 sounds in a C-major octave unison at 265-268 before the harmonised continuation at 269-272. The coda stands on the C pedal from 223. The Tchaikovsky-like cyclic intrusion is the hammered quotation of movement I at 199, the counterpart of the fate fanfare.

Two differences from Tchaikovsky are deliberate and right for this symphony, so I decided against changing them. First, the cymbals sound only at 223, 263 and 278 and there is no triangle: the first crash of the symphony is an owed item, and Tchaikovsky's constant cymbals would cheapen the arrival. Second, the second theme is never given to oboe and bassoon in octaves (Adler PDF 237, Ex. VIII-4, the colour of Tchaikovsky's folk song): F2's statements are solos by design (protected trumpet solo, c004's muted trumpet and cor anglais), and Adler PDF 249-250 warns that a doubled solo loses its rubato.

Where the score still sits below Tchaikovsky, the cause is not orchestration. The finale has sixteen stages of roughly equal finish, and the long coda (223-279, 31% of the movement) carries five quotations in a row (chorale, pulse, S head, horn call, combination). Tchaikovsky's coda carries one idea and drives. That is protected form (c253 kept the proportions) and not a matter for this reading.
