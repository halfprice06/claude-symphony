**Brahms 1/iv against this finale.** A conductor would accept the large plan. Both movements are in C minor ending in C major, both make a C-major chorale the goal, and both reserve full brass on the chorale for the coda. This finale reaches its C major at 223 in the same way Brahms reaches his Allegro: a long dominant (the stop chord held from 135 and again at 222), a strong arrival with timpani, and the chorale in trombones and trumpets. Brahms differs in two ways, and I would not copy either:
- His big tune enters p in the violins. This finale's F1 enters ff, which the cyclic rules require (loud opening after the pause, a first theme that is new).
- He lets the alphorn call return before the coda as a pivot. This finale's horn call in major at 243-246 does the same job, so the score already has this device.

Where Brahms's craft is visibly finer is in small orchestral detail: every doubling plays exactly the same line as the voice it doubles, and soft string answers never sit alone at the top of the range. The two findings below are both of that kind, and both come straight from Rimsky's examples.

## Findings

### 1. Bars 119 and 122: Vln I's pp answers to the bassoon solo sit alone at G6 and D6 (applied)

**Problem.** Over the bassoon solo in G minor (115-122), Vln I answers twice, pp dolce: G6-F6-E♭6-D6-C6-A5 | F♯5 at 119 and D6-C6-B5-A5-G5-F♯5 | G5 at 122. The whole section plays at the top of the E string, three octaves above the bassoon's held C4, and nothing doubles it. The lines are short and soft. In this register a full section at pp sounds thin and edgy, not dolce. It is the only exposed high violin line in the movement that Rimsky's octave rule has not yet been applied to (c109 applied it at 159-164).

**Evidence.**
- Rimsky PDF 58-59: "melodic figures in the very high register of the violins become too isolated from the rest of the ensemble unless doubled in octaves."
- His note to Ex. 24 (PDF 200): "One or two desks of the 1st Violins are sufficient to double the melody in the upper octave, all the other 1st Violins can play the octave below. In this way the piercing quality of the highest notes will be diminished, the melody will acquire a clearer and more pleasant sound."
- Ex. 26 (PDF 202-203) is exactly this layout at pp: "2 Viol. soli" on top, the rest of Vln I an octave lower. PDF 60 adds: "The solo desk will be sufficiently prominent owing to the general pianissimo." Ex. 36 (PDF 214) shows "Viol. I (2 Soli)" again in a soft texture.
- This technique (solo desks on top, tutti an octave below) is not used anywhere in the score. c109 used plain div. octaves at f-ff, not the pp solo-desk layout.

**Fix (applied).** In `devVlnOne` (sections/dev.ily), 119 and 122 become octave chords. The upper note is the existing line, unchanged; the octave below is the doubling:

`r4 <g'' g'''>8(\pp^\markup \italic "dolce, div.: 2 soli upper note" <f'' f'''> <es'' es'''> <d'' d'''> <c'' c'''> <a' a''> | <fis' fis''>4) r4 r2`
`r4 <d'' d'''>8(\pp^\markup \italic "div." <c'' c'''> <b' b''> <a' a''> <g' g''> <fis' fis''> | <g' g''>4) r4 r2`

Add "unis." below the staff at Vln I's next entry, 129: `a''4(\mf\<_\markup \italic "unis."`. Putting it above the staff pushed the score to 17 pages; below the staff it keeps 16. The lower octave stays above the bassoon's C4. The all-pairs parallel list is identical to v209's. On 122 beat 2 it meets Vln II's pizzicato D5 at the unison, and its C♮5 is the seventh of D7, falling to B4. The bassoon solo, Vln II and violas are unchanged.

### 2. Bar 35: Fl 1 doubles F1's head but plays it without the violins' grace glide (applied; regression from c201 + c208)

**Problem.** Since c201, Fl 1 doubles Vln I·II at the unison on F1's head in A♭ (35-38). Since c208, the violins glide into the head's A♭5 with a grace E♭-F-G. The flute was left with a plain A♭5. So at the counterstatement's first new key, two lines meant as one unison attack the same note in two ways: the strings slide in, the flute strikes it cold. The glide is blurred and the doubling no longer blends.

**Evidence.**
- Rimsky's examples show a wood-wind that doubles a violin melody taking the violins' ornaments as well. In Ex. 2 (PDF 176) Fl I and Ob I play Vln I's quick pickup into the note. In Ex. 3 (PDF 177) flutes and oboes play the violins' sixteenth-note pickups. In Ex. 8 (PDF 182) the a2 flutes, oboes and clarinets double the violins, pickups included.
- PDF 76-77: a wood-wind "progressing in unison with a stringed instrument increases the resonance of the latter and amplifies its tone". It can only do that if it plays the same notes.
- c208's own source (ch. IV, PDF 129-130, Ex. 254) allows the glide "in strings or wood-wind".

**Fix (applied).** In `pTwoFluteOne` (sections/pTwo.ily), bar 35 becomes `\grace { es''16( f'' g'' } aes''2->\f es''4. f''8 |`. The slur starts on the grace and ends at 36.1, as in the violins. The f is on the main note, so the shared staff prints one f with Fl 2. In `pTwoFluteTwo`, bar 35 gets matching grace skips: `\grace { s16 s16 s16 } c''1\f |`. F1's notes and rhythm are unchanged. This changes only how F1's head is doubled at 35 (Fl 1 takes the same grace as the violins), so finale.md's line on c208 should read "F1 at 35 has a grace glide in Vln I·II and Fl 1".
