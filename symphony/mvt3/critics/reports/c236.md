## Findings

### 1. 210→211: the timpani stay on the dominant A♭ when the bass resolves to the D♭ tonic. Retune the C drum to D♭ for 211-212 (applied)

- **Bars:** 206 (mark), 211-212 (strokes), 215 (mark back).
- **Problem:** 205-210 is an A♭ pedal under A♭7, and the timpani roll on A♭ f < through 209-210. At 211 the bass (tuba, contrabassoon, cellos, basses) resolves to D♭ ff. The key signature changes to D♭, it is Largamente, and theme B's recall begins: this is the cadence the passage has built toward. The timpani strike A♭ again at 211 and 212. Their roll on the dominant leads to another dominant stroke, and the tonic arrives without them. The movement does the same gesture correctly at 262→263 (G roll → C fff).
- **Evidence:** The timpani's job is "to strengthen the tonic and dominant bass notes and participate in strong tutti passages, especially in consummating climactic cadence points" (Adler ch. 12, PDF 79). Pedal drums "change pitches very quickly and easily", and this "has greatly enhanced the function" of the instrument (PDF 79). Tuning changes are marked over the rests (PDF 81, Ex. XII-51). The C drum is free: its last stroke is at 202, and it is not needed again until the roll at 223. Earlier critics (c006, c036, c056, c146) wrote that no retuning is "needed" because every stroke is a chord tone. That is still true, and the MIDI check still finds no new clash. My point is the cadence: a fifth over the root is not the tonic stroke that PDF 79 asks for. The symphony already retunes in a movement (scherzo "muta G in G♯"), and D♭ is one of movement II's drums, so the passage that recalls movement II's theme B gets movement II's drum pitch. The drum that plays the Neapolitan D♭ at 211 then plays C at 223. So the timpani also move D♭→C, the movement's ♭2–1, without touching the protected G→C under Tpt 1 B→C at 222→223.
- **Fix (applied):**
  - codaOne.ily, bar 206 in the rest: `R1^\markup \small \italic "muta C in D♭"`.
  - codaTwo.ily: `codaTwoTimp = { des4->\ff r4 r2 | des4\f r4 r2 | R1 | aes,4 r4 aes,4 r4 | R1^\markup \small \italic \column { "muta D♭ in C" "medium sticks" } | R1*3 | ...` (214 stays A♭ over the A♭ bass).
  - D♭3 lies inside the 26" drum's range (C3 up a semitone; 25" B♭2-F3, PDF 79). There are five bars at "poco a poco più animato" to make the change, and four Largamente bars (about 6.7 s) to go back.
  - I considered a two-note stroke A♭+D♭ at 211 (PDF 81, Ex. XII-50) and decided against it. The single D♭ answers the A♭ roll the same way 263 answers 262.
- **Note for the composer:** coherence/finale.md, "Timpani: … No retuning inside the movement", becomes "G, A♭, C, E♭; the C drum is D♭ from 206 to 215 (strokes at 211-212)". The bar-1 label, the opening tuning and the quotations are unchanged. This is not a change to F1, F2, the chorale or any quotation.

### 2. 235-238: the softest timpani roll uses the normal beating spot. Play it near the rim (applied)

- **Bars:** 235 (pp roll on C3 for four bars), 243 ("ord." for the p < f roll on G).
- **Problem:** Under movement I's S head (violins sul A, p), harp eighths and clarinet, the timpani hold a four-bar pp roll on C3 with medium sticks. On the 26" drum, a roll at the normal spot, about six inches in, gives a broad low boom under a cantabile line. The roll follows 233-234 (mf >) without a rest, so there is no time to change sticks. A change of beating spot needs no time.
- **Evidence:** "Usually, the player strikes the head about six inches in from the rim. A beautiful pianissimo can be achieved by playing even closer to the rim" (Adler ch. 12, PDF 80). The percussion "does not have to be loud to be effective" (ch. 14, PDF 138). The scherzo's timpani end ppp near the rim (finale.md, opening). So the finale's one pp timpani passage in C major uses the colour the timpani last had before the pause.
- **Fix (applied, sHead.ily):** `sHeadTimp = { c1:32\pp~_\markup \small \italic "near the rim" | c1:32~ | c1:32~ | c1:32 | R1*4 | r2 g,2:32\p\<~_\markup \small \italic "ord." | ...`. Notes, dynamics and MIDI are unchanged. The 235-247 plan is not touched.
