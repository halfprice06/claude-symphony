## Findings

### 1. Bar 9: the sustained entries come in at the level they should grow from (applied)
- **Problem.** The dominant preparation is built by staggered entries (c280, c437): bassoons and cellos mf at 7, violas mf at 8, then at 9.1 Hn 1–4, contrabassoon and basses all enter **mf** on held notes, exactly when the first group (bassoons, violas, cellos) begins its crescendo from mf. Six sustained parts arriving at the level already sounding make a step up at 9.1 before the crescendo has started, so the two bars rise in a terrace and then a ramp.
- **Evidence.** Rimsky-Korsakov, PDF 127, "Amplification": two groups in "sustained notes or chords" make a crescendo; "while the first group effects the crescendo gradually, the second group enters piano or pianissimo, and attains its crescendo more rapidly. The whole process is thereby rendered more tense as the timbre changes." Ex. 248 (*Russian Easter Fête* [D], PDF 413) shows it: the horns, trumpets and trombones enter pp cresc. under the strings' moving figure and reach f with them. The later entries at 9.3 (clarinets, mf) and 10.1 (oboes and flutes, mf) already follow this, because by then the crescendo is above mf; only the 9.1 group enters at the current level.
- **Fix (applied, sections/intro.ily).** `introHnOne` `g'1~\mf\<` → `g'1~\p\<`; `introHnTwo` `d'1~\mf\<` → `d'1~\p\<`; `introHnThree` `b1~\mf\<` → `b1~\p\<`; `introHnFour` `g1~\mf\<` → `g1~\p\<`; `introCbsn` `g,,1\mf\<~` → `g,,1\p\<~`; `introCb` `g,,1\mf\<\upbow~` → `g,,1\p\<\upbow~`. A c439 comment line stands above `introHnOne`. The violins' rising scale at 9.2 stays mf, because it is a melodic line and the book's rule is about sustained notes. The f at 10.3, the timpani (protected 7–10), the entry order and the no-re-attack rule (c189) are unchanged. Both horn pairs change together, so the part-combined staves keep equal dynamics. This keeps cycle 53's "mf at 7–10" level for the passage; it only starts the new held parts below it.
- **Check.** Page 1 shows p under both horn staves, the contrabassoon and the basses at 9. MIDI notes are unchanged, only their velocities.

### 2. Bars 119 and 122: flute doubling of the 4 soli (tested, declined)
- **Candidate.** PDF 124: "2 Vni soli or Violin solo + Fl. (Picc.) are often sufficient to double a melody in the upper register", and a solo string doubled by a wind in unison gains "great purity and abundance of tone". Ex. 231 (*Kitesh* [297], PDF 402–403) doubles two solo violins with piccolo and Fl 1, p cantabile. The 4 soli at 119 (G6 down to F♯5) and 122 (D6 down to G5) are the only high violin lines in the score without a flute.
- **Test.** In /tmp/critic-439/test1, `devFluteOne` gets the soli's notes pp "dolce" at 119–120.1 and 122–123.1. The build gives two warnings ("compressing over-full page by 14.0 staff-spaces", "page 7 has been compressed"), because the flute staff is no longer hidden in the first system of page 7.
- **Why declined.** The answers are string replies to the bassoon solo. Rimsky's own echo rule (PDF 128–129) depends on related timbres, and a flute inside the reply makes it half wind, which blurs the dialogue. The score already uses this technique at the right point: at 129–130, Fl 1 joins Vln I "unis." at pitch as the harp passage crescendos, and that new colour would be anticipated. A pp flute at G6 is also hard to keep soft. 115–130 is protected (bassoon solo, c209/c438 octave layout at pp). Not better, so not applied.

### 3. Short pizz. chords to stress wind chords (Ex. 255): no playable place

### 4. Bar 237: the harp's D2 sits a second above the basses' C2 pedal (checked, kept)
The close-interval scan finds only thirds at G2–B2 (normal) and one cluster at 237: basses C2 (pedal), harp LH D2 (whole note), cellos D3 then F2 on beat 3, so C2–D2–F2 sound together in the bottom octave. PDF 124–125 warns against close harmonic intervals in the lowest range. I kept it. Rimsky's warning concerns the lowest octaves, below this one, and a plucked harp D2 decays under the bowed pedal. The cello line is movement I's quotation, and 235–247 is a protected plan. The alternatives are worse: F1 in the harp goes below the pedal, D3 breaks the harp's octave line with a leap of a ninth, and `d,2 f,2` keeps the C2–D2 second on the downbeat.

### 5. Beethoven 5/iv: would a conductor believe a master wrote this?
- **Shared design.** Like Beethoven, the finale saves timbres for itself: Beethoven's trombones, piccolo and contrabassoon; here the trumpets' melody (51), the cymbals, the string tremolo and the C7 (223). It brings back the scherzo inside the retransition, as Beethoven does at bars 153–206: here motive x is in the timpani at 143–145 under the F1-head stretto, and theme A's head at 275. It also ends with a long tonic close after the first root V–I (263–279, 17 bars, against Beethoven's 29 bars of C).
- **Differences, kept.** Beethoven's scherzo recall is a whole pp section in the scherzo's metre. Here it is a motive inside a crescendo. That is subtler but clearly heard, because it is the only timpani line in 143–146. Beethoven builds his C-major arrival on the 50-bar timpani pedal crescendo from the scherzo. This finale has to open ff after a pause (cyclic rule), so its long crescendo goes to the 205–223 path into the arrival, which does the same job later. Beethoven's opening theme is a tonic triad march; F1 is a minor-key theme that turns major only in the coda, which is the Brahms 1 / Tchaikovsky 5 model the plan names.
- **Verdict.** A conductor would take 1–279 as professional work in the Brahms–Dvořák line. The one place where the orchestral technique was cruder than the rest was the step at 9.1 (finding 1). It was small, but it falls in the first crescendo of the movement.
