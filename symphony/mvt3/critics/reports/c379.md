## Findings

### 1. 259–262: the clarinet doubles the violins' arpeggio in unison; it should break the chords in contrary motion (applied)

**Problem.** Under the last chorale statement Vln II (ff) and Cl 1 play the same eighth-note arpeggio at the same pitch through 259–262 (iiø6/5, iiø6/5, German sixth, V7). The clarinet adds nothing that can be heard. Its A♭4–A♭5 lies inside two violin sections at ff under brass at ff, so it is absorbed. The movement has no place where two parts break the same chord in opposite directions.

**Evidence.**
- Adler PDF 205 and 208, on Ex. XVI-9 (images PDF 206–208, mm. 5–6 and 9–12): "The woodwind arpeggio is most imaginatively scored: while the piccolo and first flute are playing it, the two clarinets 'flutter' in the opposite direction with the implied harmony notes." On the page the flutes rise while the clarinets fall on the same chord.
- PDF 229 (Schoenberg on Op. 25): two like lines at the same pitch "swallow each other up". Unison doubling is the weakest use of a second instrument.
- PDF 213 (Bizet): the runs are dovetailed so that the motion is felt across the whole range.
- Saint-Saëns 3/iv puts running figuration against its C-major hymn; the figuration is a layer in its own right, not a doubling.
- c127 gave Cl 1 the arpeggio "at Vln II's pitch (mixed colour kept, c117), below the line". The reason for the 259–263 protection is that nothing should rise above the chorale top line. Both conditions still hold: the clarinet keeps the same chord tones in F4–G5, below the top line (Fl 1 D6–B5, Tpt 1·2 D5–B4 an octave below that line), and still sounds with the violins in the same range. Only its direction changes.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-379/work/sections/finale.ily, Cl 1 only).**

```lilypond

a'8(\f\< c'' e'' a'') e''( c'' a' e') | a'8( c'' e'' a'') e''( c'' a' e'') |
f''8(\ff\< d'' aes' f') aes'( d'' f'') r8 | f''8( d'' aes' f') aes'( d'' f'' d'') |
fis''8( es'' c'' aes') c''( es'' fis'') r8 | g''8( f'' d'' b') g'( b' d'' g'') | g''1\fff~
```

**Decisions.**
- Each half bar runs opposite to Vln II (Vln II A♭4–D5–F5–A♭5, Cl 1 F5–D5–A♭4–F4; then the reverse). The two parts meet in the middle of each half bar and are a sixth to a tenth apart at its ends.
- 258's last eighth becomes E5 (was C5). C5→F5 made octaves with Tpt 3's C4→F4 at 258→259, and an A♭5 start made octaves with Tbn 2's C→A♭.
- 260 ends on D5 with Vln II. An F5 there made octaves with Tpt 3 and the violas on F→F♯ at 261.
- 261's F♯5 rises to G5 at 262.1 after the breath. 262 ends on G5 into the fff G5 at 263.
- The breaths at the ends of 259 and 261 are kept.
- Vln II, Cl 2, Fl 1·2, the harmony and all dynamics are unchanged. The chorale top line and the cadence 261–263 are unchanged.

**Check.** The scratch build has 0 warnings (build-warnings.txt is empty) and 16 pages. analysis.txt shows 0 outer-voice parallels, as before. par8.py on 255–264 gives the same output as v379 (only the existing bass doublings). allpairs_par.py over the whole score gives output identical to v379. The finale quote record reads 51/51. No theme, quotation or record line is touched.

### 2. 269–271: a new timpani rhythm for the last crescendo (Schoenberg, PDF 230), considered and not applied

From 265 to 271 the timpani repeat `c4-> r8 g,8 c4 r4` while the bass and brass grow from f to ff (269–271). Adler PDF 230 praises Schoenberg's "completely new rhythm in the timpani, which lends great excitement to the crescendo". The full motive-x form `c4 r8 g,8 c4 r8 g,8` at 269–271 would do that. I decided against it. finale.md item 5 records this pulse as movement I 179–180, quoted at 229–230 and reused at 265–271. The crescendo there already gets new layers from the trumpets at 269 and the trombones at 271 (c124/c129). No change.

### 3. Saint-Saëns 3/iv: would a conductor believe a master wrote this?

Yes, in its main lines. Both finales take a C-minor work to a C-major apotheosis built on a transformed earlier motto: Saint-Saëns's organ-and-strings hymn, and this score's brass chorale with the falling major motto at 223–227. Both use a cyclic return, a lyrical wind episode (his pastoral oboe and flute; this score's bassoon solo 115–122 and cor anglais 171–186) and a quickened coda (his Più allegro; this score's 247).

The differences come from the model this score chose. Saint-Saëns opens the finale in major and stays there; this score withholds C major until 223 (the Brahms 1 plan, protected). Saint-Saëns writes a fugato on the hymn theme; here the stretto 139–146 does that job (protected). His closing chord is held by the organ after the orchestra stops; here the harp glissando and the timpani roll at 277–278 give the final chord its ring.

The one Saint-Saëns technique this score handled weakly is the independent running figuration against the hymn. Finding 1 addresses it inside the existing notes. The conductor's likely question is the same one c349 and c359 noted: the coda's six stages (223, 235, 247, 255, 265, 273) against Saint-Saëns's single drive. This plan is protected, so I raise it as an observation only.

### 4. 133–134: Bizet's "whispering" tritone, considered and not applied

The fading V4/2 at 133–134 (f→p, harp ringing) is the one held soft chord in an unprotected place. Bizet's device (PDF 213: a murmuring tritone in the cellos and a timpani roll at ppp) does not fit it. The cellos carry the bass F there. A timpani G2 roll would sit a major second above the basses' F2 at the bottom of the texture. 135 already starts the retransition's pp motion. No change.

### 5. 278: open strings in the final chord (Dvořák, PDF 209), not applied

The cellos' final `<c g>` (C3–G3) uses no open string, while the violas' G3 is open. Open C2–G2 in the cellos would ring as Dvořák's opening chord does. 273–279 is protected, and c376 has already used the cellos' open C for the movement's only open-C moment at 263–264, so I made no change.
