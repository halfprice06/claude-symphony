## Findings

### 1. 68: F2's second bar in E♭ major is a root-position leading-tone triad with a tripled leading tone (applied)

Problem. On the downbeat of 68, the second bar of F2 in the violins, the sounding notes were D2 (Cb), D3 (Bn 1 and Bn 2 in unison), A♭3 (Vc, Vla, Hn 1) and F5 (Vln I·II). That is D–F–A♭, VII5/3 in root position, held for two beats at mf in a soft, thin texture. The root B♭ came only as the harp's third arpeggio eighth (68.2), after the chord had already sounded without it. The leading tone was tripled. Bars 52, 60 (E♭ minor F2), 76 (the next phrase), 172, 180 (C minor, fixed by c263) and 188 (A♭ major) all put V6/5 over the same leading-tone bass, so 68 was the one statement of F2 that did not.

Evidence. A&S PDF 243, sec. 12: "Diminished triads on the leading tone are seldom satisfactory in 5/3 position… V6/5 (which contains all three tones of the diminished VII) creates a far more pleasing sonority and is to be preferred". Review point 10 (PDF 245): "a leading tone in the bass should usually support V6/5". PDF 243 (Ex. 15-18): in the diminished chord Bach "doubles the 3rd… the preferred doubling", not the leading tone. The exception in Ex. 15-19 (PDF 244), a diminished chord heard as V6/5 because it "grows out of the strong V at the beginning of the bar", does not apply here: 67 is I. c331 called 68 a V6/5, but only because it counted the harp. c277 kept the bassoon unison at 67–69 for scoring reasons, not harmonic ones.

Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-353/work/sections/sTwo.ily). Bn 1 holds B♭2 through 68 instead of doubling the bass D3–E♭3:

```
sTwoBsnOne = { es1\p | bes,1 | c1 | c2. r4 | ...
```
(was `es1\p | d2 es2 | c1 | ...`). The downbeat is now D–B♭–A♭–F, a complete V6/5 like the other statements. On 68.3 the B♭ is the fifth of I. Bn 1 moves E♭3–B♭2–C3, a fourth down then a step up, and the leading tone is left in the bass line only (Cb D2, Bn 2 D3). Tune, counter-melody, harp, dynamics and the a2 at 67 and 69 are unchanged. The partcombined bassoon staff shows two voices in 68 only (page 4, checked as an image). par8.py on 64–72: the bassoons' "shared" octave lines at 68.1, 68.3 and 69.1 are gone. What remains is Bn 2 doubling the bass at the octave, which was already there. analysis.txt: 0 outer-voice parallels; the only change is the bassoon note counts.

### 2. Ex. 15-14 (natural VII → V6/5 → i, bass B♭–B♮–C) has no place in this score (considered, not applied)

The chromatic bass B♭–B–C is the one VII technique in the unit that the movement never uses (PDF 241: "the chromatic inflection of 7̂ can produce a particularly expressive effect"). I checked every V6/5–i in C minor:

- 13–15, 21–23, 153–155, 161–163: F1's third bar has B4 in the tune over the bass G, so a B♭ chord cannot stand there.
- 169–171: protected (stopped horns c108/c210, Fr6 at 168).
- 184–186: a deceptive cadence.

The dominant preparation 7–10 and the retransition's B pedal (139–146) have no tonic to reach. I made no change. This is the reason the technique stays unused, not an oversight.

### 3. 69: III as support for the passing 7̂ (Ex. 15-10d) (considered, not applied)

The tune falls E♭6–D6–C6 across 69.3–70.1 over IV6 → IV. Ex. 15-10d (PDF 237–238) would move the bass C–G–A♭ so that D6 sits a fifth above G. I rejected it for three reasons:

- The D6 is a quarter-note passing tone and is not a fault as it stands.
- A G bass on 69.4 would make the violas' and cellos' C4 (c027's counter-melody) a fourth above the bass, and the counter would have to be rewritten.
- The harp's group on beats 3–4 cannot carry a change on beat 4 alone.

The gain is too small for three disturbed parts.

### 4. 26→27 and 198→199: rising thirds into a downbeat (kept)

PDF 231 and 240 warn against a rising third from weak to strong. Both cases in the movement are section joints. At 26 a deceptive cadence is followed by a two-beat silence, then the ff counterstatement starts at 27. At 198→199, c270 has since turned 198 into A♭ minor (C→C♭ in the violas and cellos on beat 3), so A♭ minor → C minor keeps only one common tone. This is the "chromatic inflection [that] removes one of the common tones and adds more contrast" of PDF 231. The joint no longer sounds like one chord. c263's decision stands, now with this further reason.

### Form verdict on the lens

The tonal plan is the one Unit 15 describes for minor:

- III is reached through natural VII as V of III (39–50) and confirmed by root cadences (65–66, 81–82, 91–94).
- It is left by step upward through iv to V (Ex. 15-2b, 15-3).
- It sits at the time midpoint between tonic and dominant, as PDF 231 describes.

The coda grades its goals as PDF 235–236 describes. Inverted dominants reach I at 222→223 and 246→247, V7→I6 ends the combination's phrases (250–251, 254–255), and the root V7–I comes only at 262–263. The coda's four stages last 37, 26, 21 and 50 s. Each later stage is either faster or louder than the one before (fff 223 → p cantabile 235 → ff 247 → the trumpets' ff chorale entry 255 → fff 263), so the coda builds rather than sags. The one fault the reading finds is local (finding 1).
