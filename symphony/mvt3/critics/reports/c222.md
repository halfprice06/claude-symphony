## Findings

**1. Bar 30: the counterstatement's V4/2 → i6 becomes VII°4/3 → i6, with 5–6–5 in the winds' top line. Applied.**

Problem: at 29-30 the bass statement of F1 (cellos, violas, bassoons, Hn 3·4 at F4, the basses an octave lower) climbs to its peak F and falls to E♭: V4/2 → i6. The winds above hold G (Fl 1 G5, Ob 2 G4, tied from 29) through the change. The score uses the °7 often, but only in root position and in the °6/5 position, and never in the °4/3 position that A&S teach as the upper neighbour of I6. Their first example is a C-minor Allegro con brio (Beethoven op. 10/1, bars 10 and 12). There is also a voice-leading fault at the same moment. Hn 2 B3 and Hn 1 F4 form a d5, and they move to C4 and G4 (a P5) in similar motion. A&S mark this "avoid" because it makes obtrusive hidden fifths.

Evidence:
- A&S PDF 375 and 383 (Points for Review 1): VII°4/3 = V4/2, the upper neighbour of I6.
- PDF 381: the 7th of °7 as upper neighbour to 5, the line 5–6–5, "a particularly characteristic and important function". The box on the same page: dissonances involving the bass resolve.
- PDF 375, Ex. 22-3a and 22-3c: the d5 resolves inward; a d5 moving to a P5 in similar motion is marked "avoid".
- Ex. 22-6: °4/3 → I6, with 2 rising to 3 (here Fl 2/Ob 1 D5 → E♭5).

Fix (pTwo.ily):
- `pTwoFluteOne`: `g''1~ | g''1~ | g''1` → `g''1 | aes''2 g''2~ | g''1` (bars 29-31).
- `pTwoOboeTwo`: `g'1~ | g'1~ | g'1` → `g'1 | aes'2 g'2~ | g'1`.
- `pTwoHnOne`: `f'2 g'2~ | g'1` → `f'2 es'2 | g'1` (bars 30-31).

Bar 30 now sounds B (Hn 2, and the countersubject's B4), D (Fl 2, Ob 1), F (bass, Hn 1) and A♭ (Fl 1, Ob 2). It resolves on beat 3: A♭→G, B→C, D→E♭, F→E♭, and the bass F→E♭. This is the movement's first °4/3. The winds' top line G–A♭–G now repeats the G–A♭ step of F1's own head above the bass statement of F1. F1, the countersubject (violins and Cl 1·2), the dynamics and the slurs are unchanged.

c191's protection of 27-34 concerned F1's scoring in the bass, and that scoring is unchanged. c188's ties are kept wherever the pitch stays the same. B–D–F–A♭ resolving to C minor is not the stop chord's event: the same vii°7 → i already occurs at 22-23, 162-163 and 169-171. The stop chord (138, 222) still resolves to C only at 223.

par8.py on 27-34 reports one new line, 30.3 "8ve Hn 1 F4→E♭4". This is Hn 1 doubling the bass statement's own F4–E♭4 at the unison. It had already been sitting on that F4 since 28. It is the regular resolution of the d5 and gives i6 a doubled bass, as in Ex. 22-3a. The analyser reports 0 outer-voice parallels before and after.

**2. 222→223: °6/5 to I in root position, with the bass's d5 D–A♭ going to C–G. Not applied (decided: keep).**

Problem: A&S Ex. 22-4b marks °6/5 → I5/3 "poor". They accept it only as a neighbour chord (8–7–8 in close position, Ex. 22-4d). At 222 the bass D (Vc/Cb) and A♭ (Vln II A♭5, Tpt 2 A♭4, Tbn 1 A♭3) move to C and G.

Decision: keep. The D in the bass is movement I 138's harmony (finale.md, quotation item 3). The tone-for-tone resolution of every voice at pitch (B→C, F→E, D→E, A♭→G) is the owed item, and the passage is protected in every cycle since 160 ("216-223 with 222→223"). Putting B in the bass would satisfy PDF 375 (d7 → P5) but would break the cyclic identity. The bass D→C step also carries the last stage of the chromatic bass D–D♭–C that 135-138 began.

**3. ♭VI in major (Brahms 3/ii, Ex. 23-5; deceptive V–♭VI, Ex. 23-6) is the one mixture device the reading teaches that the score never uses. Not applied (decided: no change).**

Problem: the C-major coda (223-279) carries mixture through iv (233, 274), iiø6/5 with A♭ (259-260), the German sixth (261) and the F♯°7 chain (273). It never uses an A♭-major chord. The only C-major music is 223-279, and every part of it is protected: 223-234, the 235-247 plan, 247-254, 259-263, 265-272 and 273-279.

Decision: no change. The places where a ♭VI could replace vi (239 or 257) are in the protected 235-247 plan and the protected last chorale. A ♭VI there would also anticipate the A♭ that the iiø6/5 and the German sixth introduce at 259-261. I record it so a later critic does not have to search for it again.
