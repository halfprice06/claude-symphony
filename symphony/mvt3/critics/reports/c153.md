## Findings

### 1. 131-132: the G-major hand-over closes with a root-position perfect cadence in G, which halts the development 18 bars before the return. Applied.

Problem. The development's harmonic goals are F minor (113, perfect cadence, ff), G minor (121-122, half cadence), G major (131-132, perfect cadence, f), then G7 (133-134), the diminished-seventh chain to the stop chord (138), V over B (139-146) and V7 on G (147-150). From 115 to 154 the development sits on G, first as a key and then as the dominant: about 40 bars, roughly 57 s. The cadence at 131-132 makes G a closed tonic in the middle of that span. D7 f in every section, then the G-major chord on 132.1 with Vc, Cb and Bn 2 on G, horns on G-B-D, timpani G, and the violins' arpeggio G6-D6-B5-G5. Only after that does the retransition have to turn the same G back into a dominant. Adler PDF 267 (Ex. XVII-10) names this scoring: a held fifth in the horns and the low strings over the bass "emphasizes the solid return to the tonic". At 131-132 the score uses that scoring of a return, but in the dominant key, before the real return. The corpus model does not do this. Dvořák 9/iv has one authentic cadence in its development, about 30% of the way in, and runs from there to a half cadence into the recapitulation (MCTL cadence.csv, beats 588 and 780). Our 113 matches that cadence; 131-132 is a second, later close that Dvořák avoids. Adler PDF 263-264 and 267 (Mozart keeps the solo's colour out of the orchestra) adds a second point. The timpani's G at 132 is the drum's only stroke between 113 and 143, so it takes the freshness away from the scherzo's motive x on G at 143.

Fix (dev.ily). Make 131→132 a deceptive cadence D7 → E minor. The G area, its key plan, F2's notes and 133-134 G7 all stay; E minor goes to G7 over the common tones G and B. With this, the G area has only open cadences (half cadence 122, deceptive 132), and the motion runs unbroken from 114 to the recapitulation.

### 2. 231-234: the plagal close stops every pulse for four bars at the slowest tempo of the movement. Applied.

Problem. In the arrival (223-234, Maestoso 2 = 56) the only rhythmic background is the violas' portato quarters at 224-228, and at 229-230 movement I's timpani pulse. At 231-234 (IV–I–iv–I over C, f > mf) every part plays whole notes and the timpani roll, so nothing is attacked after each downbeat for 8.6 s, and the next stage begins at 235 with the harp's eighths. Adler PDF 259-260 says the background keeps a rhythm of its own that "neither duplicates nor imitates" the foreground. Here background and foreground stop together. Adler PDF 288-289 (Orff) says an accompaniment figure gives "vital ... rhythmic assistance" without obscuring the line. Adler PDF 192 says the seams of a hand-over must not show. The pulse goes from violas (224-228) to timpani (229-230), then disappears for four bars before the harp takes it (235). The chorale's own statement is 5 bars (224-228); its closing, 229-234, is 6 bars, and the last 4 of them are motionless. This is where the coda sags.

Fix (arrival.ily). The violas restart their portato on their own plagal-close notes, with the same pitches, harmony and dynamics:
```
arrivalVla = { … | g2\cbaw bes2 | g1 | a4--(\f a4-- a4-- a4--) | g4--( g4-- g4-- g4--) | aes4--(\> aes4-- aes4-- aes4--) | g2.\mf r4 | }
```
The A♭3 of the iv chord (the mixture) is now restruck four times inside the texture. It does not undo the deliberate dip at 230-238 (c033): the dynamics are unchanged and the violas lie under every other part. It also does not touch the protected plagal close (c042), because the progression and every pitch are kept. Scratch build: clean, 16 pages, no new parallels.
