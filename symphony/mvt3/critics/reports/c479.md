## Findings

### 1. Holistic comparison with the Eroica finale: no change

The movement has the Eroica finale's main structural features:
- **Opening.** Beethoven opens ff on V7 of E♭, reached through a G-minor scale, and stops on a held chord before the theme (mm. 1–11). This finale opens ff on a German sixth to V of C (1–10). Both start off the tonic and save the tonic for the theme.
- **Slow apotheosis.** The Poco Andante (m. 349) turns the lyrical theme into a tutti with the tune in the horns and running strings (m. 380). The Maestoso arrival does the same: the chorale in the brass with string tremolo and the violins' canon (223–234).
- **Return of the opening.** The Presto (m. 431) brings back the opening flourish after the slow section, and the movement ends with hammered tonic chords. Here the Più allegro (247) follows the slow coda, the opening scale figure returns in C major at 263–264 (c79), and 277 hammers the tonic before the held chord.

The order slow apotheosis, then fast coda, then emphatic close is the same. One difference stands. Beethoven's middle section is a fugato (m. 117), so it keeps contrapuntal drive. This development is 36 bars and turns lyrical at 115–134, with the bassoon solo and then the cellos with the harp. That is closer to the Eroica's G-minor flute episode (m. 211) than to its fugato. The passage is protected (115–130) and is the Brahms 1/iv model the brief names, so this is an observation only. A conductor would accept the coda's pacing and its brass writing as a master's.

### 2. Schoenberg's new timpani rhythm at 245–246: tested in the build, not better

**Candidate.** Adler PDF 230 (Op. 25, mm. 874–879): "Schoenberg here introduces a completely new rhythm in the timpani, which lends great excitement to the crescendo." At 243–246 (stringendo, cresc. molto into the Più allegro) the timpani hold a G roll, p < f. I tested this change in /tmp/critic-479/test:

```lilypond
sHeadTimp = { ... | r2 g,2:32\p\<~ | g,1:32~ | g,1:32 | g,4 g,4 g,8 g,8 g,8 g,8\f | }
```

The roll ends after 245, and 246 accelerates into the 247 pattern `c4 r8 c8`. Build: 0 warnings, 16 pages, outer-voice parallels 0, par8.py on 243–247 identical to v479. The page 14 engraving is clean.

**Why it is not better.**
- It is not new. The same accelerating G strokes already lead into the retransition's ff at 143–146 (`g,4 g,8 g,8 g,4 r4`), so a second use would be a repetition, not Schoenberg's "completely new rhythm".
- It doubles a pulse that is already there. At 245–246 the cellos and basses already speed up from halves to quarters (`d2-- d2-- | d4-- d4-- d4-- d4--`), so the strokes would add nothing of their own.
- It spends the 247 entry. The timpani's `c4 r8 c8` at 247 is new with the brass, which is the fresh entry Adler PDF 228 and 236 ask for. Anticipating it in 246 weakens that entry.
- It cuts the roll's swell short. The roll lasts through the whole stringendo, as at the other big arrivals (219–222, 261–262).

Not applied. /tmp/critic-479/work is unchanged.

### 4. 242: harp G♯ against the horn's A♭, kept

Adler PDF 213 says Bizet respelled D♯ to make the diminished seventh clear. That would favour A♭ in the harp at 242, where Hn 4 has A♭3 in a iiø7. But spelling A♭ needs the A pedal changed at the barline from 241, where the A strings are still ringing. That change can make the strings buzz, which is the kind of decay and cessation detail Adler praises Ravel for notating (PDF 224). G♯ uses the G pedal, which is free through 239–241. The harp spelling follows harp practice, and the conductor sees the pedal text at 241. Kept.
