## Findings

### 1. 103–106: the development restates an exposition key at pitch; move the second statement to D♭ major (applied)

Problem. The development's two statements of F1's head are F minor (99–102) and B♭ minor (103–106). The second is the exposition's 37–38 again: the same key, the same pitch (Hn 3·4 B♭4, cellos B♭3: `bes'2->( f'4. ges'8 | aes'4-.) ges'8( f'8 es'4-.)` in pTwo.ily and dev.ily), the same instruments, only the last note differs (D at 38, D♭ at 104). So the first half of the development moves only between F minor and its subdominant and repeats a key area the exposition has already used at the same pitch; the development first leaves C minor's diatonic orbit nowhere (99–134: iv – iv/iv – iv – v – V).

Evidence. Unit 32 says the masters' large-scale chromaticism exploits "the greatest variety of tonal areas" while the diatonic frame keeps the direction (PDF 592). Its development plans move to areas the exposition did not have: the Appassionata's development moves in major thirds, A♭ minor – E major – C minor – A♭ major, to a D♭ goal that enlarges the movement's 5–♭6–5 figure D♭–C (PDF 599–601, Ex. 32-19); the Hammerklavier's development goes to E♭ after the exposition's I–VI♯ (PDF 595); the Waldstein's goes F major → F minor → V (PDF 594). Section 16 (PDF 600–601) adds that a key area is best chosen to enlarge the piece's characteristic interval, as Chopin's D♮ area enlarges his D♮–C♯ figure (Ex. 32-11).

Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-383/work/sections/dev.ily). The second statement is in D♭ major, a major third below the F minor of 99. That is the first step of the equal division F–D♭–A, and it puts D♭ in the bass at 103–106 and again at 109 (the German sixth's bass), falling to C at 110. So the development now does in F minor what the coda does in C: D♭ → C, the Neapolitan thread (♭2–1 of C) that the coda completes at 211–223. It also follows principle 8: ♭VI (of F) reached directly and left through the German sixth to V (109–110).
