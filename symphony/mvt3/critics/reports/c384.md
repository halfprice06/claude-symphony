## Findings

### 1. Bars 278-279: the final chord's strings play tremolo, as at 223 and 273-276 (applied)

Problem. The last chord is the score's third fff tutti. Every other group sustains with full energy: brass fff, a timpani roll, cymbals and bass drum, the harp's glissando landing. The strings, which have played bowed tremolo through 273-276 and struck short double stops at 277, hold 278 as plain whole notes divided three ways in Vln I, Vln II and violas (each note on a third of the section), cellos divided, basses a plain whole note. A slow sustained bow, divided, under fff brass and a cymbal crash is the weakest way the strings can play a loud held chord, and they play it at the movement's last sound. It also makes the last chord a different string sound from the identical fff C-major chord at the arrival (223), where all five string parts are tremolo.

Evidence.
- Appendix of single tutti chords, PDF 505-507: in chords 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 9-12, 17, 19 and 20 the strings of a held f, ff or fff chord carry tremolo strokes, and in every chord marked "div." (4, 18, 19) the divided strings are tremolo. The only held tutti chord with plain strings is chord 5, a bare octave on one pitch. Chords 3, 8 and 18b strike the strings short instead. No chord holds a divided string triad plain at fff.
- Ch. III, PDF 88 (B, "Sustained and tremolando chords"): tremolando is the usual substitute for a sustained string chord, and "forte tremolando on two strings" is the form for loud held chords; the same page says divided upper strings over undivided cellos and basses need balancing, which the tremolo does by giving every divided part more sound per player.
- Ex. 206 (Sadko, PDF 369) and chord 18 (Sadko, the end, PDF 507): the strings at a fff close play tremolo on the held chord and strike only the final short chord.
- c114's report said 278 follows "appendix chords 1 and 17-20" with "div. a3"; those chords are divided *and* tremolo, so the reading was half applied.
