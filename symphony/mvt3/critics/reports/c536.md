## Findings

### F1 (applied). 151–158: Ob 2's unison copy of F1 moves to the cor anglais

Problem. At the recapitulation (151–158, ff) Ob 1 and Ob 2 play F1's head in unison. The line falls to E♭4 at 152.4 and D4 at 153.1 and passes F4 at 157.1. Those notes lie in the oboe's coarse lowest fifth, doubled in unison at ff. The cor anglais has F1's next eight bars anyway: since c446 it holds the inner line at 159–166, and it plays 147–150 ff. It rests only at 151–158.

Evidence. Sebesky PDF 71: "I don't recommend voicing oboes in unison unless, for some reason, an especially harsh effect is required", and on the same page "The oboe may be combined effectively (in unison or octaves) with flutes, piccolos, clarinets, English horns, and bassoons". PDF 70–71: F down to B♭ is "a bit coarse" and "the most difficult to control". PDF 72: the cor anglais "does not become coarse or nasal in the bottom of its range". PDF 80: unison doublings carry more "chance of an intonation conflict". So the swap keeps two double reeds on the tune, which was c187's reason for the a2 in the exposition (Rimsky ch. II). It avoids the unison of two oboes and the doubled coarse low notes. It also makes 147–166 one continuous cor anglais passage instead of an eight-bar gap.

### F3 (checked, no change). 248 and 251: trombone slide distances

At 2 = 100 Tbn 1·2 play C3–B2–C3 slurred into an accented A2 at 248, and D3–C3–B2 | C3–E3 at 251–252. On a straight tenor trombone this means 6th–7th–6th–2nd position in eighths of 0.15 s, the case Sebesky marks "awkward – very difficult if played in fast tempo" (PDF 28, Ex. 27). Orchestral tenor-bass trombones have an F attachment, which puts C3 and B2 in near positions (the effect Sebesky describes for the bass trombone's trigger, PDF 29), so the figure is playable. The passage is protected (247–254, the trombones' line, c071/c091/c177), and F2's head cannot be re-pitched. No change.
