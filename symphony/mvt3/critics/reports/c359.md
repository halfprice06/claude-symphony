## Findings

### 1. 255–262: at the chorale's last statement the first violins hold dead whole notes; give them the chorale's own tremolo (applied)

**Bars.** 255–262, Vln I.

**Problem.** At the last chorale statement the thematic material is in the brass and the lower strings. Tpt 1·2 (later with Ob 1, Cl 2 and Fl 1·2) have the chorale. Violas, cellos, Hn 3·4 and Bn 1 have F2's phrase at 255–258, and the violas' repeated quarters follow at 259–262. Vln II and Cl 1 play eighth-note arpeggios, and the basses play eighths. Vln I doubles the chorale an octave above the trumpets as plain tied whole notes, ff, marked "change bow at will": G5 | G5 | E6 | E6 | D6 | D6 | C6 | B5. At 2 = 88 a bowed whole note at ff under full brass has no attack and does not grow. It is the only string part in these eight bars that does nothing to keep the passage moving. The chorale's first statement had its own string colour: the arrival tremolo continued under its first two bars (223–225, c150). Its last statement drops that colour.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 243 (Ex. VIII-11, Figaro mm. 46–58, viewed): the winds, brass, violas, cellos and basses carry the thematic fragment while "the violins perform what we may call an agitated pedal or an ornamented pedal". Adler PDF 161 (Ex. V-37, *Carmen* prelude): the tune is in the lower instruments and the upper strings play ff tremolo on held chord tones. Adler PDF 261–262 (Ex. VIII-33, VIII-34, Debussy): give a recurring idea the same colour each time it returns, so that the form is clear. The chorale is that recurring idea, and its colour at 223–225 is violin tremolo. Mahler 1/iv's closing chorale over D major is a brass chorale over strings that keep moving, not over held whole notes. The score's own reason for 224–225 (c149, after Bruckner 4/iv's coda and Rimsky-Korsakov Ex. 281: strings in tremolo under the brass at the climax) applies equally to 255–262. c355 rejected tremolo under the horn call at 243–245 because the strings there are mf to leave the horns room. At 255–262 every part is f to ff and Vln I is not under a solo, so that reason does not apply. The cyclic rule only places the first tremolo at 223, and 273–276 already brings it back.

### 2. Holistic comparison with Mahler 1/iv: a conductor would accept this as the work of a capable late-Romantic composer, with one difference in kind (no change)

What the two finales share: each has an outburst for an opening that follows on from the previous movement (Mahler's cymbal crash and dissonance after the funeral march; here the ff German sixth that takes the scherzo's G♯ as A♭). Each has a lyrical second theme in a flat key set against a storming first theme (F2 in E♭ minor/major). Each recalls the first movement (199–204, 224–230, 235–246, 247–254). Each attempts the goal key early and fails: Mahler's premature breakthrough in the development; here the stop chord B–D–F–A♭ at 138, left unresolved until 222→223. Each ends with a brass chorale in the major (223, 255). Mahler's extra standing horns have their counterpart in the trumpets' horn call in major at 243–246 and the horns' a2 S head at 247.

The difference in kind: Mahler's coda is one continuous rise driven by the same string figuration from the breakthrough to the end. This coda has six stages: arrival, chorale, S head, horn call, combination and last chorale, then the F1 close. Each stage starts with a new texture. 235–246 in particular (Poco più mosso, p, harp arpeggios) is a full stop between the arrival and the drive to the end. This form is protected (235–247 plan, c253 proportions, c349 note on Bruckner 4/iv), and c343 measured the coda's stages, so I am not proposing a change to the form. Inside the form, the texture can be made continuous where Mahler's is. Finding 1 does this for 255–262, the only stretch after 247 where a string part had stopped driving.
