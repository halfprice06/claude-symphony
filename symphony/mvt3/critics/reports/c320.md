## Findings

Ranked by how much each would matter if the current choice were wrong.

### 1. The symphony's key plan is the unit's I – VI – (IV) – V – I, and finale 1–10 supply its dominant. Keep.

Unit 26 says the order of key areas should be as purposeful as the order of chords in a phrase (PDF 448 §7). It names I – VI – IV–V – I as a Beethoven sonata plan (PDF 454, after Ex. 26-14). Point 10 (PDF 457) adds that tonicized VI leads down in thirds toward IV.

The four movements follow this plan. C minor (I) moves to A♭ (II, VI), then to E (III, a further major third down). The finale's bar 1 is the German sixth A♭–C–E♭–F♯, a IV-family chord built on the same A♭. It goes to V at 2, V is prolonged with its 7th and ♭9 through 10, and I arrives at 11. Without bars 1–10, the symphony's large progression would have no dominant before the tonic returns.

Section 15 (PDF 456) says that when V returns after a digression, "it is often preceded by a II or IV and often supports a 7th". The finale's opening does exactly that, and the scherzo's last G♯ becomes the A♭ of that IV-type chord.

Decision: bars 1–10 are the hinge of the whole tonal plan. They are already protected, and this unit gives a second reason to protect them.

### 2. C is the only key in the finale that is not confirmed by a II/IV–V–I cadence until 259–263. Keep, and add none.

The unit's rule for every key area is a pivot to introduce it and a cadence with II or IV and V to confirm it (PDF 443; Points 2, PDF 456). Every other key in the finale gets that cadence (rows 4 and 13). C minor never does: 25–26 is deceptive, 165–166 is V–i6, 199 is entered without a dominant, and 147–151 lands on i6/4. C major is confirmed once, at 259–263, by iiø6/5 – Ger6 – V7 – I with Tpt 1 B4→C5. That is the unit's cadence formula in its fullest form.

The cyclic rule (no root-position V–I in C before 261–263) therefore works as a large-scale suspension of the unit's normal procedure. Each key the music passes through is confirmed while the home key is not, so the tension ends only at the last cadence. A master's finale does the same; Brahms 1/iv withholds a full C-major cadence until its coda.

Decision: do not "strengthen" 165–166, 151 or 199 with a cadence in C.

### 3. 243: III♯ in major as a chord, not a tonicized key. Keep. This answers row 14 and repeats c220's reason, now with the unit's own terms.

Ex. 26-9 (PDF 450, Brahms Op. 117/1) shows I – tonicized III – V in major, with III expanded by its own IV–V–I. The finale's 241–243 has III♯ (E major) for half a bar between ii7 and the cadential 6/4. PDF 443 adds a cyclic point: in C *major*, E minor (iii) is a closely related key. In C minor it is remote. The finale's turn to major is what makes the scherzo's key a diatonic relative of the home key.

I worked out the Brahms form: 239 A minor (iv of E), 241–242 F♯ø7 – B7, 243 E. Two reasons rule it out:
- It needs D♯ as B major's leading tone. The finale keeps D♯→E for one event only: violas and Hn 3 at 219–220, the answer to the scherzo horn call's unresolved D♯4 (scherzo 5–6).
- It replaces the protected 235–247 bass plan.

E minor (iii) without D♯ cannot be tonicized; only plagal motion is left, A minor → E, which is just vi → iii in C. Section 5 (PDF 446) allows the weaker form of tonicization by figuration. But c310's Vln I G♯5→G5 already puts the scherzo's last pitch on the chord, and adding F♯ or D♯ figures would turn a recall into a key.

Decision: keep 243 as it is. This is c220's position, now with the reason in the unit's terms.

### 4. Row 10, the only technique the score does not use: a sequence of tonicized chords. Tested, not applied.

Ex. 26-6 (PDF 447) expands a descending-thirds sequence by giving each chord its own IV6/5–V6/5. Point 10 (PDF 457) names "tonicized VI leading down in 3rds from I to IV" as frequent in major. The finale has the bare chord progression in C major at 235–240: I (235–236) – V7 over the pedal (238) – vi (239) – IV (240). To tonicize vi at 239, 238's second half would need V6/5 of A minor, G♯–B–D–E, with the bass G♯2 in the cellos and harp.

I did not apply it, for three reasons:
- That chord is the E-major sound that c310 made "the finale's only sound in the scherzo's key" at 243. Using it five bars earlier as an ordinary applied dominant takes the cyclic weight off 243.
- 235–247 is protected (c220).
- 238 is the S head's C6 appoggiatura over V7, whose voicing c082 and c122 settled.

The other free places do not suit a sequence of tonicized chords:
- 87–90 is already a descending 6/3 bridge (c053, c143), and tonicizing each chord would end its contrapuntal function (A&S Unit 18).
- 99–106 is a sequence by rising fourths, f to b♭, which the unit also allows (PDF 445).

Decision: leave row 10 unused. The unit presents it as one option, not a requirement, and no bar here gains from it.
