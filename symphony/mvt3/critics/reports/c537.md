## Findings

1. **209–210, timpani crescendo into theme B: tested, not applied.** The reading's candidate is Adler PDF 230: in Schoenberg's Op. 25 transcription, "a completely new rhythm in the timpani … lends great excitement to the crescendo." Every timpani crescendo in this movement is a roll (149, 167–168, 209–210, 246, 254). In /tmp/critic-537/work I replaced the A♭ roll `aes,1:32\fp\< | aes,2.:32 r4\!` with the coda's own hammered rhythm from 199 (`aes,4\p\< r8 aes,8 aes,4 r8 aes,8 | aes,4 r8 aes,8 aes,4-> r4\!`). The build had 0 warnings, 0 outer-voice parallels and 16 pages, and par8 found nothing at 207–212. It engraves cleanly. I declined it for these reasons:
   - Schoenberg's timpani rhythm works because the figuration around it does not already have that rhythm. Here the basses already hammer A♭ in eighths through 209–210 (codaOne Cb) and the violins and cellos run in eighths. The strokes fall on the same pulse and the same pitch, so they would thicken the basses rather than add a layer.
   - The roll is the only sustained, unpulsed layer under the woodwinds' and horns' held fp < chord. c494 started it at fp for exactly that reason (Adler part 2 PDF 184).
   - The roll is a dominant A♭ that resolves to the D♭ stroke at 211, which is the classical roll-to-arrival shape.
   
   The scratch copy is reverted to v537.
2. **209–210, string scale dovetailed from bottom to top (Bizet, PDF 213, 215–216): worked out on paper, not applied.** The cellos, Vln II and Vln I play the A♭ scale in three octaves together. Passing it Vc → Vla → Vln II → Vln I would cover about 3¾ octaves in 16 eighths, which needs sixteenths or an incomplete line. It would also take the violas off their held E♭/G♭, which is the chord's seventh. Bizet's dovetail is a soft device. A crescendo grows by adding octaves (Adler ch. 5, PDF 137, as used in c524), and the three-octave scale does that.
3. **89–90, contrary-motion clarinet against the woodwind run (Brahms, Hungarian Dance 1 m. 5, PDF 205, 208): worked out on paper, not applied.** Cl 2 is free at 89–90. However, Fl 1, Ob 1 and Cl 1 already fill F4–F6 in octaves. Any rising figure in Cl 2 between them meets Ob 1 at the unison on D5 (89.4) or crosses Cl 1. The bars also carry c502's voice leading (V4/3 of V → V7 → I). The figure would cost clarity without adding a new layer.
4. **205–208, countertheme passed between instruments (Schoenberg, PDF 230–231; named and not applied by c337): checked, no change.** These bars already hold four ranked layers: F1's head (horns, trombones, Bn, Vc at 205; winds and Vln I at 207), the answering scale (Vln I·II, then Vln II), the violas' repeated chords, and the A♭ pedal in eighths. A fifth line would break Adler's rule on PDF 203 (rank the layers; too much colour confuses the form). The movement's countertheme of this kind is the Vln II line at 159–166, which follows the reading.
5. **115–118, sustained "damper-pedal" note under the bassoon solo (Ravel, PDF 217; Dvořák's sustained harmonic counterpoint, PDF 209): checked, no change.** The solo has only pizzicato support. A held clarinet or horn G3/D4 would sit in the bassoon's own register (G3–A4), which goes against Adler part 2 PDF 264 and 274 as applied in c500 and c527. The pizzicato texture also matches 51–66 by design (the pizz./arco timing is protected).
6. **11–26 and 151–166, F1 doublings checked against PDF 229 and 239: follows.** The violins are in unison at C5 with violas and horns an octave lower (Brahms's own layout in Hungarian Dance 1). The repeat raises Vln I to the flutes' octave (159), which is the high–low exchange of PDF 239. No change.
