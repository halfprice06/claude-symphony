## What else I studied

**Saint-Saëns 3/iv (Maestoso – Allegro).** I compared from the full score (IMSLP, https://imslp.org/wiki/Symphony_No.3,_Op.78_(Saint-Saëns,_Camille)). The two finales share a plan: C minor turns to C major, a colour is held back for the apotheosis (Saint-Saëns's organ; here the C7 tremolo, the first cymbal crash and the fff full brass at 223), a cyclic theme becomes a brass hymn, the soft hymn statement is haloed by an arpeggiating plucked or struck instrument (his piano four hands; here the harp at 235–242), and the close is a plagal tonic prolongation with timpani before the last held chord (273–279). They differ in two ways. Saint-Saëns builds the whole finale from one transformed motto, while this finale moves through several recalled themes in its coda. That is an observation only, because the coda's stages are protected. Saint-Saëns also runs continuous figuration against the hymn; c379 already addressed that at 258–262. Would a conductor believe a master wrote this? Yes, for the arrival, the S head and the close. The one weak spot I found against Saint-Saëns's handling is local: his crescendos into a structural downbeat are built by adding layers from the bottom up. Here the stringendo into R started every run part at once, under the horn call's resolution. Finding 1 fixes that.

## Findings

**1. 245–246: the stringendo run into R should enter from the bottom up (applied in /tmp/critic-579/work).**
- Problem: violas (G3), Vln II (G4), Vln I and Fl 1 (G5) all started the same one-octave run on 245.3. The four-bar crescendo was therefore left to the players' hairpins, the way Adler says a transcriber must not leave it. Vln I and Fl 1 also rose G5–A5–B5 just under Tpt 1's held C6 on the very beat where the horn call resolves B→C (245.3), an owed item in its own register.
- Evidence: Adler2 PDF 213. Bizet "orchestrated a diminuendo in the beginning, [and] did the same for the crescendo", and his run is "dovetailed for absolute smoothness from the bottom to the top" (Ex. XVI-11, PDF 216, m. 31: cellos, then violas, then violins). PDF 224: Ravel's orchestrated crescendo gains fullness through added at-pitch doublings. PDF 205: the full sound is not struck all at once. The Saint-Saëns comparison above points the same way.
- Fix (sHead.ily), applied:

**2. 245–246 alternative: a true relay sweep G3→G6 in sixteenths. Declined.**
- This is Bizet's literal device (PDF 213). It doubles the run's speed under a stringendo that already reaches 2 = 88, it drops the octave weight into the ff at 247, and it is the same reason c537 declined a dovetailed scale at 209–210. Finding 1 takes the useful part of the technique, the entries in order from the bottom up, and keeps the score's rhythm.

**3. 110–113: Bn 1 rests so that its solo at 115 is fresh. Declined.**
- Basis: Stravinsky refreshes a solo entry by changing the colour before it (PDF 228).
- Against: c094 and c062 put Bn 1 on the violas' scale figure so that it is not covered by the ff brass (header of dev.ily). The bar of rest at 114 (about 3 seconds at 2 = 84) is the usual preparation for a solo after a tutti. Removing it would weaken the counter-line it was placed there to support.

**4. 121–122: Cl 1's pp D6 an octave lower, a controllable range (PDF 213). Declined.**
- Dvořák's point concerns a ff→p diminuendo in the piccolo.
- Here c323's D6 is a 7–6 suspension that hands its D6 to Vln I's soli at 122. Written E6 pp is within a good player's control. At D5 it would sit between Vln II's pizzicato D5–G5 and the violas.
