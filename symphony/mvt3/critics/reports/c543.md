## Findings

### 1. 133–134: resolve the V4/2's seventh down to E♭ on 134.4. Tested in the scratch build and not applied.
- What the reading says (A&S PDF 219–220, Ex. 14-1 bar 11): after a long stay on V, its seventh (4̂ of the main key) turns the temporary tonic back into a dominant, and in Schubert the V4/2's bass F resolves to I6. Here the cellos and basses hold F under G4/D4 from 133 to 134.3, then rest. The bass at 135 is D, so the bass seventh does not step down.
- The test: in dev.ily, Vc `f2.\p r4` became `f2.\p es4` and Cb `f,2.\p r4` became `f,2.\p es,4`. The bass then runs F–E♭–D–D♭–C–B from 133 to the stop chord at 138. The build was clean: 0 warnings, 16 pages, 0 outer-voice parallels. par8 flagged only the cellos doubling the basses an octave above at 134.4 and 135.1.
- Why I rejected it:
  - The E♭ sounds alone on 134.4, because Vln I, the violas and the harp all stop at 134.3. A lone E♭ after V7 is heard as i6 of C, sixteen bars before the recapitulation. That is the objection c520 raised against i6 at 114, and here it is stronger because the bass carries it alone.
  - The seventh is not dropped. F carries into the diminished seventh at 135 (Vln II F5, violas F4/B4), so the dominant is prolonged rather than resolved, which is the job of a retransition. Schubert resolves at once only because his tonic returns at once (A&S PDF 219: "after a brief sojourn … simply introduce the I chord").
  - The quarter rest before J is the break that A&S PDF 216 lists among the means that set off a new section. The E♭ fills it.
  - 131–134 is on the do-not-touch list (c133, c183).
- Decision: no change. The scratch copy was reverted and rebuilt.

### 2. 238→239: an applied V of vi (E7) on 238.3–4 before the S head's forte restatement over A. Rejected on paper.
- What the reading says (PDF 213, section 4): any triad may be preceded by its own V. The restatement at 239 starts on A minor after V7 over the C pedal, a deceptive move.
- Why I rejected it: the change would need G♯ in an inner voice, E in the cellos and basses, and an end to the timpani's C roll and the basses' C pedal on 238.3. That rewrites the harmony of the movement I quotation, whose bass at 235–238 is movement I's bassoon line at pitch (finale.md, item 6). It also sounds G♯ four bars before the A♭ = G♯ link at 242–243 (c510), which should be the only G♯ in this passage. Its sole gain is to make the deceptive move to A predictable, and the reading values that move as a 5–6 push (PDF 217). No change.

### 3. Proportions and the coda's stages. Checked, no change.
- What the reading says (PDF 220, section 11): form comes from departure and return, and a key area marks a large division.
- How the movement does it:
  - The departure keys are clearly marked: E♭ (51–98) and F minor (99–113), each closed by a cadence with a rest after it. The dominant G (115–150) is the longest single area: G minor, then G major, a PAC at 132, V4/2, then the dominant pedal and the stop chord. That makes the return at 151 a true return in the reading's sense.
  - The recapitulation (16.3%) is half the exposition because P2 and the transition are cut. That suits a finale whose tonic resolution is saved for the coda.
  - The coda (31.2%) rises in stages: C-minor tutti 199 (f–ff), D♭ ♭II area 211, C-major arrival 223 (fff), S head 235 (p, molto cantabile), Più allegro 247, chorale 255, last cadence 263. The one soft stage (235–238) works as a 5–6 springboard (finding 2), and from 239 to 263 the dynamics only rise.
  - After the last cadence, 263–279 repeat the final cadence with variation (the timpani's G–C pulse at 265–271 and 276–277, and the basses' G1 at 277.2), as the reading describes for a coda (PDF 220).
- Decision: no change.
