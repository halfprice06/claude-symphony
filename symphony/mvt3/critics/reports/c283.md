## Findings

Ranked by how much each would matter if the current choice were wrong. None calls for a note change.

### 1. 113–114: no pivot chord between F minor and the G area. Tested, not applied.

The unit's main rule is that a pivot chord usually introduces the new key (PDF 443, Points 2). At 113 the development's F-minor cadence stops ff on a half note. A half-bar rest follows, and at 114 the strings alone play D7 p >, V7 of G, straight after the F-minor triad. They share only C. I worked out the textbook alternative: on 114 beats 1–2 C minor in first inversion, which is v6 of F minor and iv6 of G minor, so a true pivot, going to D7 on beats 3–4. In LilyPond: Vln I `g''2(\p\> fis''2)`, Vla `g2(\p\> a4) r4\!`, Vc `es2(\p\>\upbow d4) r4\!`, Cb `es,2(\p\>\upbow d,4) r4\!`, Vln II unchanged. It would be clean (no parallels, no cross relation).

I do not apply it, for three reasons:
- The unit itself allows the direct move. "A change of key is not always brought about by a pivot chord" (PDF 445, referring to Unit 14, p. 219). A new area in a sectional form "frequently—and typically—enters right at the beginning of the new section without a transition" (PDF 452). Here the rest and the change of colour and dynamic (ff tutti, then p strings, then a pizzicato bassoon solo) mark a section seam. c228 and c243 kept the break-off for that reason.
- The pivot would be a Phrygian approach: bass E♭–D, iv6 to V. The G-minor phrase already ends on exactly that bass at 121–122 (E♭ with the Italian sixth, then D). Using it at 114 as well would spend the phrase's cadence seven bars early.
- The F-minor-to-D7 shift is a chromatic third relation, a Romantic colour that a pivot would make plain. c132 protected it so that each chromatic step (F→F♯, A♭→A) stays inside one part.

Decision: keep 113–114 as it is.

### 2. 77–78: vi is not tonicized inside F2's E♭-major climax. Tested, not applied.

The one technique in the unit that the score does not use at all is the tonicized chord within a phrase or sequence (Ex. 26-5 and 26-6, PDF 446–447; Points 6). The only unprotected phrase where it could go is F2's second half in E♭ major (75–82; 187–198 is protected). The candidate is V6/5 of vi on 77 beats 3–4 (bass B♮), moving to C minor at 78. That fails here. The tune holds E♭6 through 77 beat 3, which is an unprepared ♭13 over a G-major chord. At 78 the tune's peak is B♭6, a beat after the bass's B♮, so B♮ and B♭ form a cross relation between the outer voices. That is the case A&S treats as faulty (PDF 394–395, cited by c132). Changing the tune to avoid it would alter F2, which is not justified for a colour chord. The first half of the theme already has the device: 70–71 tonicize ii with V4/3 of ii (c232, c239).

Decision: keep. The technique appears once in F2, at 70–71, and a second use at 77–78 would damage the climax.

### 3. 186→187 is prepared the way the unit's Mozart example prepares its transient key (keep)

Ex. 26-7 (PDF 447–448) shows a small V6/5–VI progression, stated twice, that foreshadows the larger area that follows. Here the exposition's deceptive cadence V–VI at 25–26, and F1's head on A♭ at 35, foreshadow the recapitulation's A♭ statement of F2, opened by the same deceptive move at 186→187. The recapitulation's own F1 consequent avoids it (165–166 is V–i6 since c134), so the deceptive cadence that opens A♭ is heard once in the recapitulation, at the point where it leads somewhere. This is the technique in the unit closest to a master's handling of large-scale form, and the score already uses it. Do not add another deceptive cadence between 151 and 186.

### 4. The G area 115–150 prolongs V for 36 bars without a tonicized VI (keep)

Section 15 (PDF 456, Ex. 26-17) says that a prolonged dominant often tonicizes VI as an upper neighbour, and that development sections show this on a larger scale. In C minor that VI is A♭. Inside 115–150, A♭ appears only as the ♭9 of the stop chord (138–146) and in Hn 4's A♭3→G3 at 139, which is the neighbour figure inside one chord. The large VI-to-V motion comes later, in the coda: A♭ at 187–210, then D♭, then G at 220–222. That is row 18's plan, with ♭II standing in for IV.

A tonicized A♭ inside the retransition would anticipate the recapitulation's A♭ area and weaken the C-minor recapitulation that follows. It would also fall in protected bars (135–150, c197). Instead of subordinate keys, the dominant area changes by mode (G minor → G major), harmony (G → V4/2 → diminished-seventh chain → stop chord over B → V7) and texture. Section 15 accepts the 36-bar length. The development (12.2%) and the retransition (5.5%) together come to 17.7%, which is within the range of Dvořák 9/iv's proportions (c193).

### 5. Key plan and section joints (keep)

The movement's plan is the unit's typical minor-mode plan, row 13: i – III – (passing IV) – V. It also has the K. 457 details: C7 at 97–98 acts as V of F and leads to F1's head in F minor at 99, and the B♮ bass at 135–146 under V6/5 brings back the leading tone after G minor. In the coda, Beethoven's I – VI – (IV) – V – I (row 18) appears as A♭ – D♭ – German sixth – V – I. Each new key gets a cadence with a predominant (row 4), and each arrival has 1 or 3 on top on a strong beat (row 7). I found no seam where the unit's rules are broken.
