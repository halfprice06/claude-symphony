## Findings

### 1. 263–264: the only root V–I in C has no open-string resonance in the cellos; give them open C2 with C3 (applied)
**Problem.** The cellos never play their open C string in this C-minor/C-major finale. Their lowest note in the whole movement is D2 (175). At 263, the symphony's only root-position V–I (protected since v14), the cellos rise G2→C3. The bass octave C2 is left to the basses and the tuba, and C1 to the contrabassoon. The cellos' strongest note in C, the open C2, is missing exactly where it would count most.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 99: the cello's C string "is a true sonorous bass of rich quality". PDF 73–74: open strings "have a greater vibrating potential" and "lend themselves to great exploitation separately" (Ex. III-4, Brahms 1/iv, the open G). PDF 76: orchestral double stops are kept for "especially sonorous passages" and must be easy. Ex. III-56 (PDF 97–98) lists the cello's open-string double stops; open C2 with C3 (fourth finger on the G string) is the easiest octave on the instrument.

**Fix (applied).** In finale.ily, `finaleVc`: `c1\fff\dnup~ | c1\>` → `<c, c>1\fff\dnup^\markup \italic "non div."~ | <c, c>1\>`. The bowing, dynamics, the progression ♭VI–Ger6–V7–I, the trumpets' B→C and the timpani are unchanged. The F1 head at 265 starts from single notes as before. par8.py reports one new line, 263.1 cellos G2→C2 with the bass G1→C1. That is the cellos doubling the bass line at the octave, as the bass clarinet, Bn 2, bass trombone and cellos already do at 259–262. It is not a voice-leading parallel. analysis.txt still shows 0 outer-voice parallels.

### 2. 255–257: Vln II's octave of F2 under the ff brass chorale lies on the weakest string; mark it sul G (applied)
**Problem.** Since c371, Vln II doubles F2's last statement an octave above the violas, cellos, Hn 3·4 and Bn 1 (G4–E4–D4–C4–E4–G4–C5–D5), ff espress., under the brass chorale's last statement. With no marking, a violinist plays G4–E4–D4 and E4–G4 on the D string, and C5–D5 on the A string. The D string is the one Adler calls the least powerful. So the doubling meant to lift F2 out of the middle register is played on the string with the least body, against trombones and horns at ff. Before this change, the score used the G string only once, at 81.3–82, for a soft diminuendo colour, never for its power.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 74–75: the G string is "the thickest and most sonorous of the four strings. As one goes into its higher positions, the sound becomes very intense and stringent" (Ex. III-5, Tchaikovsky 5/ii, a passionate sul G line in high positions, f–sf). The D string "is possibly the least powerful of the four". PDF 76: "If one wishes to have a passage performed on one string exclusively, the passage should be marked … sul G". PDF 73: the only reason to prescribe a string is the timbre wanted, and here the timbre is the point.

**Fix (applied).** In finale.ily, `finaleVlnTwo` at 255: `\once \override TextSpanner.bound-details.left.text = \markup \italic "sul G" g'2.->(\ff^\markup \italic "espress."\startTextSpan e'4) | d'4( c'4 e'4 g'4) | c''2.( d''4\stopTextSpan) | g''4. …`. It is a dashed spanner like the one at 81.3, so no "ord." is needed. The line stays on the G string up to D5 (seventh to ninth position, the range of Adler's Tchaikovsky example). The player crosses for 258's dotted G5 peak (c351), which brightens on the D/A string where the phrase peaks. No note, rhythm, slur or dynamic changed, and F2 is not changed as a theme, so no other movement thread needs telling.

### 3. 36→37: the violins' C5→F6 leap (checked, no change)
Both violin sections leap an eleventh, from the staccato C5 that ends 36 to F6 on 37.1, at f. Adler PDF 82–83 (Ex. III-22 to III-24) warns that wide skips are less sure in a section than in a soloist. Here the C5 is a separated quarter at 2 = 84, which leaves about a third of a second for a one-string shift to the seventh–eighth position on the E string. The F6 then starts a descending run that confirms it. This is ordinary section writing, not the extreme low-to-high jumps of Adler's examples. Dropping Vln II an octave at 37–38 would thin the unison that 39 deliberately splits. I keep it. The other large leaps (Vla 131→132, Vc 258→259 D4→F2) come after a released note and are downward or onto a double stop. They are fine.
