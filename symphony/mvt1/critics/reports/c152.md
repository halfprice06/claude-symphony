## What I studied

Besides the assigned reading I built a one-track-per-instrument MIDI of v171 (all 30 parts) and listed every sounding pitch by beat. I flagged each complete diminished seventh (27 half-bar hits) and checked how every voice enters and leaves each one. I also scanned for ♭6/♮6 and ♭3/♮3 in the two major-key areas, outer-voice cross relations, and melodic A♭–B augmented seconds. I checked every flagged spot against HISTORY.md and cyclic-notes.md before raising it. No web or corpus sources were needed; the book's own examples (Beethoven Op. 10/1, Bach WTC I/2, Brahms 3, Mozart) are the comparisons.

Overall the movement's diminished-seventh and mixture usage is already textbook-correct in most places. Examples are 63→64 (vii°7/vi, every tone resolves), 70→71 (vii°7/V with C♭), the coda's i–iv–vii°7–i over the tonic pedal (Ex. 22-10), the B°7 over the G pedal (the Appassionata case on PDF 378), and the F♭ minor-iv6 at 61 (Ex. 23-2b). The three findings below are the only places where the score goes against a rule the reading states outright and where a fix leaves protected material alone.

## Findings

### 1. Bar 30: the brass move a diminished fifth to a perfect fifth in parallel (applied)

**Problem.** Bar 30 is vii°7 of iv in root position (E–G–B♭–D♭ over E). The trumpets play it as E4–G4–B♭4 and the trombones as E3–G3–B♭3, and both groups rise by step to F–A♭–C at 31. Tpt 3/Tpt 1 (E4–B♭4 → F4–C5) and B.tbn/Tbn 1 (E3–B♭3 → F3–C4) therefore move a d5 into a P5 in similar motion. This happens ff, in the two most penetrating groups, with the trombones an octave below the trumpets.

**Evidence.** A&S PDF 375, §4 and Ex. 22-3c: "if the upper dissonance forms a diminished 5th, the similar motion that follows creates an obtrusive set of hidden 5ths; avoid this voice leading." The box on PDF 377 gives the rule: the d7 resolves inward to a P5. PDF 377 §7 names the most characteristic use of the chord: it harmonises 6̂ in the top-voice neighbour 5–6–5.

**Fix.** Give Tpt 1 and Tbn 1 the chord's seventh, D♭, in place of the doubled B♭. Then E–D♭ is a d7 that resolves inward to F–C (Ex. 22-2), and each part plays the neighbour figure C–D♭–C across 29–31 (5–6–5 in F minor). The rhythm, the dynamics, the trumpets' timpani rhythm and the chromatic bass are unchanged. B♭ is still sounded by Hn 4, Cl 2, Fl 2 and the strings.
```
tOneTptOne bar 30:  des''4 r8 des''8 des''4 r8 des''8 |   (was bes')
tOneTbnOne bar 30:  des'1 |                              (was bes1)
```
This is a pitch change inside the protected 27–32 tutti. It does not touch what the protections cover: the layout by instrument and register, the brass grading, the trumpets' rhythm and the bass.

### 2. Bar 26: vii°6/5 falls to root-position i (applied)

**Problem.** The ff stop chord at 26 is vii°6/5 over D (Cb, Cbsn, Tuba, B.tbn, Vc). After the rest the bass falls D→C into the tonic tutti at 27, while A♭ falls to G in Fl 2 and Hn 1. The diminished fifth D–A♭ involving the bass therefore moves to the fifth C–G. The top voice (Fl 1, Vln I B5) goes to G5 for the theme's pickup, so the book's exception (soprano 8–7–8 in close position) does not apply.

**Evidence.** A&S PDF 376, Ex. 22-4: °6/5 normally goes to I6 because the bass 2̂ must rise to 3̂. Ex. 22-4b, °6/5→I5/3 with D→C in the bass, is labelled "poor". Box PDF 377: "All dissonances involving the bass must resolve." The book's opening example, Beethoven Op. 10/1 (PDF 373–374), shows the alternative: the root-position °7 (bass B♮, bar 17) leads into the tonic. Exercise 1b (PDF 384) is I–IV–VII°7–I, which is exactly 25–27.

**Fix.** Put the stop chord's bass on B♮, so the chord is root-position vii°7 and the bass leading tone rises to C at 27. D stays in Bsn 2, Hn 3, Tpt 3, Ob 2 and Vln II. In the basses, the approach A♭ (25, beat 4) → B♮ is the A2 that PDF 380 §11 allows "in the course of a motion from II, IV, or VI to VII°7".
```
tOneCb 26:    b,,2.->\ff r4      tOneCbsn 26:  b,,2.->\ff r4
tOneTuba 26:  b,,2.->\ff r4      tOneBassTbn 26: b,2.->\ff r4
tOneVc 26:    <b, aes>2.->\ff r4
```
The stop, the silence, the timpani G and the entries of the low brass and timpani are kept, as the stop-chord protection requires. I did not change 138. There the same chord goes up from D to F (iv) at 139, which is not the poor case. The coda's 170 is over the C pedal (row 10), so 26, 138 and 170 still share the chord and its accent. The cyclic "unresolved B♮s" (14, 170, 174, 177; cyclic-notes.md) do not include 26.

### 3. Bar 164: Horn 3 turns ♭6 back into ♮6 (applied)

**Problem.** At 163→164 the music inflects ♮6 to ♭6 (A→A♭; IV → borrowed iiø7), which the book calls good. Hn 3 then plays `aes2 a2`: its A♭3 rises to A♮3 over the G bass on beat 3 and goes on to B3 at 165. ♭6 is replaced by ♮6 before it reaches 5. The harp's A♭5→G5 in the same bar resolves correctly, so the two parts contradict each other.

**Evidence.** A&S PDF 388: "Once introduced, it tends to move to 5; it will not normally be replaced by ♮6 before 5 is reached." Points for Review, PDF 395: "♮6–♭6–5 is good; ♭6–♮6–5 is poor." The score itself does this correctly at 61→62, 76→77 and 156→158.

**Fix.**
```
recTwoHnThree bar 164:  aes2\p g2 |   (was aes2\p a2); 165 b1 and 166 g1 unchanged
```
The progression iiø7–V7–I (the protected climax harmony 158–166), Hn 4's D at 164, the dynamics and the brass fade are unchanged. The only loss is the passing major ninth A3 on beats 3–4. That ninth is the note the rule objects to.
