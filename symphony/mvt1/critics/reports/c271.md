## Method

Besides the reading, I read every section file of v271 and looked at score pages 2, 6, 8, 15 and 16 at 80 dpi. On v271/full.midi I ran three scripts: (a) stretches of two beats or more with no new onset in any part, bars 15–170; (b) holds of six beats or more in the top sounding line, and which parts move during them; (c) runs of parallel 3rds/6ths between the tune and every other part. For comparison I extracted the second themes of Tchaikovsky 6/i (Violino I, bars 89–100, `musicxml-converted/xml/Tchaikovsky_Sym6_mvt1.musicxml`) and Dvořák 9/i (Flauto I, bars 149–156, `Dvorak_Sym9_mvt1.musicxml`).

## Findings (ranked)

None of these findings calls for a change to the score. I list them in order of how much they would matter if they were wrong.

### 1. The four themes do not need new versions (no change)

Sebesky's question 6 (PDF 220) is whether the song is weak in its melody and what to do about it. I asked it of each theme.

- **Motto** (G–E♭ rising minor sixth, then D–C–D♭–C). It is stated low at 1–4, a fifth higher in the CA at 5–8, on the flute at 73–75, by the brass at 117–121, in stretto at 127–130, as D–D♭–C at 156–157, and in the CA, the trombones and Vln I at 171–182. A listener hears it nine times in clearly different colours, which is principle 20 at work.
- **P.** The head rhythm (pickup quaver, accented dotted crotchet, falling chromatic quavers) is the movement's most repeated figure. Its second half 19–22 has one peak (D♭6 sf at 20) and a stepwise fall to the tonic. The sequence at 23–25 is the motto's E♭–D–C cell regrouped in threes over the bar line, so even the transitional bars carry a theme.
- **S.** The head has the motto's own rhythm: dotted minim tied to a quaver, then a quaver (bar 2 `es2.~ es8 d8` against bar 47 `c'2.~ c'8 bes8`). It also has the rising sixth, three times in five bars (E♭–C at 46–47, A♭–F at 48, E♭–C at 51). The first statement's peak (F5 at 53) comes about three-quarters of the way through the phrase. The violins' restatement peaks at D♭6 at 63, about 60% through. The S head is planted before the theme in exactly the way Sebesky describes (PDF 206: an introduction "based solely on the initial two-note motive"). The horn call E♭–C–B at 13–14, the four horns' E♭–C–B♭ at 41–42 and Hn 1's echo E♭4–C5–B♭4 at 43–44 give the first three notes of S, first in minor and then in the S key.
- **Horn call.** It has three notes, and it is the S incipit with B♮ in place of B♭. That makes it a signal, not a tune, and it is placed as one.

Comparison with the models. In Tchaikovsky 6/i the second theme repeats its two-bar cell (F♯–E–D, then B–A–F♯–A–D–B, then A) four times in twelve bars (89–100). Dvořák 9/i states an eight-bar tune made of a two-bar head and its variants (149–156). Both rely on immediate repetition. Here S makes a nine-bar statement (47–55) and a thirteen-bar restatement (56–68), and the sixth-plus-descent shape returns inside each. This is the Brahms and Elgar way of building a long line, and it gives the listener less immediate repetition than Tchaikovsky. The exposition makes up for it: the head returns at f at 69 and is planted three times beforehand (13, 41, 43). I would not trade the long line for a more repetitive one. Every pitch and rhythm of these themes is protected (c091, c101), and I found no reason to reopen that protection.

### 2. c161's open note on bar 150, and the onset scan (close the note; no change)

c161 left open "no moving fill under held notes at 150", citing Sebesky PDF 222. The fill Sebesky describes takes up slack while the voice holds one note for "two beats +" (Ex. 286, bar 13). At 150 the tune does not hold a note. Hn 1, the violas and the cellos play C5–B4 in two minims, the 4–3 that ends the antecedent. The same two beats are still at the exposition's bar 50, the matching cadence. Sebesky also says a line moves only when "tempo, rhythmic complexity, and harmonic motion" call for it (PDF 212), and a half cadence is the place for a breath. Where the recapitulated tune does hold a note (147, 3½ beats), the CA fills it on beat 3½, as in Ex. 286.

The onset scan agrees. In 15–170, every stretch of three beats or more with no new onset in any part is a stop or a join: 26, 41, 76→77, 84, 122–124 (fff held tremolo), 126, 138. The only exception is the flute's held A♭5 at 74 (3½ beats, pp, inside the protected flute motto, where the held note is the motto's own long note). Recommendation: record c161's bar-150 note as closed.

### 3. Codetta 70–71: does the P-head counter shadow the S head? (no change)

At 69–72 the P head (Cl, Hn 1·2, Vln II) runs against the S head (Fl, Ob 1, Tpt 1, Vln I). At 70 both lines reach F a beat apart, an octave apart, and both fall to E♭. At 71 the counter's quavers D♭–C–B♭ run an octave under the tune's dotted D♭–C–B♭. Sebesky's Ex. 279 shows violins that "shadow the melody too closely" and blur the focus (PDF 211). I measured all parts against the tune and found no run of parallel 3rds or 6ths anywhere in 15–32, 47–68, 147–166 or 171–182, and only these brief octave touches at 70–71. They come from combining two themes that have their own rhythms. This is Ex. 280's case, where the background "touches down briefly on the solo melody notes" (PDF 212), and not Ex. 279's. The passage is protected (69–72) and stays.

### 4. Coda 171: the minor S starts without its rising-sixth pickup (considered, no change)

Every other complete statement of S begins with a rising sixth on the upbeat: E♭3–C4 at 46–47, E♭4–C5 at 55–56, F♯–D at 96–97, G2–E3 at 146–147, and the violins' F5–D6 at 155–156. The solo cello at 171 starts on E♭4 on the downbeat. In C minor its pickup would be G3–E♭4, the motto's own interval, so I tested the idea on paper. I reject it for two reasons. First, beat 4 of 170 is the fermata rest of the stop chord, and a pickup there would have to sound inside the fermata. Second, the CA already plays G4 on beat 4 of 171 and E♭5 at 172, so the motto's sixth sounds one bar later as the duet's second voice. Sebesky's point that the end of one idea can supply the start of another (PDF 207) is met by the pairing as written. The coda notes are protected (c228, c270), and nothing in the reading answers that protection.

### 5. The violins' A♭6 at 31, 72, 122 and 160 (considered, not reopened)

Sebesky's Ex. 275 saves a fuller sound "for emphasis in a later bar" (PDF 209), and Ex. 285 holds instruments back so that an effect lands (PDF 222). A♭6 is the violins' ceiling at four points. This was settled at cycle 194, when Vln I left A♭6 at 140, and confirmed at c264. The climax hierarchy does not rest on the top pitch. It rests on fff only at 122 and 160, on the A♭ bass at 82/122/160, and on the full brass and cymbal at 160. The ceiling is fixed by the finale's reserved C7. I found nothing in the reading that answers the stated reasons, so I do not reopen it.

### 6. Soft pads above the tune at 47–49 and 147–150 (no change)

In both places the top sounding string line is a pad (Vln II A♭4 metà pp for three bars at 47–49; the Vln II sul tasto halo, mostly A5, at 147–150), and the tune lies below it. Sebesky warns against anything that "distorts the aural focus" (PDF 211) and wants a soft lead line to have character (PDF 210). Here the tune is still the only line with frequent onsets and swells (after the downbeat of 47, Hn 1 and the cellos each have 9 onsets in 47–49 against 2 in Vln II; at 148–150 Hn 1, the violas and the cellos each have 9 against Vln II's 6). The pads are pp, divided or sul tasto, and the halo's lower voice moves C–C♯–D. The focus stays on the tune. Both passages are protected (c247, c091).
