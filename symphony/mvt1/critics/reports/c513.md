## Findings

I made no edit. Under this reading nothing in v513 stands between the score and a master's work strongly enough to justify touching protected bars. Every passage the reading bears on is protected, and for each one I checked whether the stated reason still holds. Findings 1 and 2 are new readings that support the current text. Findings 3 and 4 are the two places where I looked for a weakness and tested it.

### 1. The pivot into A♭ is ii of the new key, the reading's preferred kind (34–36). Keep.

PDF 217 says the best pivots are chords that "would normally lead into the V chord of the new key — II and IV of the dominant being preeminent", and that the pivot is likely "the chord just before the first dominant in the new key". Earlier passes named 34–36 as the pivot area without saying which chord.

The reduction shows:
- 33: C minor, i.
- 34: A♭ bass under the C of the tune: the 5–6 with a skip down to the root, as in Haydn's Ex. 14-7.
- 35.1–35.3: B♭ minor (Vc B♭2, Bsn 1 F3, Cl 2 F4, Vln I D♭5). This is VII of C minor and ii of A♭.
- 35.4: B♮–D–F–A♭ over the B♭ bass, the movement's stop-chord sound, as a passing chord.
- 36: E♭, the first dominant of A♭.

So the chord just before the first new dominant is ii of the new key, which is the textbook pivot. The movement also uses B♭ minor ten bars later as ii6/5 at 46, right before the V7 that brings in the S head. The pivot and the last pre-dominant before the arrival are the same chord. That gives the transition a consistent harmonic plan, which a listener hears as purpose. Protected (c163, c236, c503). Keep.

### 2. The flute motto's F♭ is a 5–6 over the A♭ tonic that sets up the development (73–78). Keep.

c423 showed that the G♭ at 75 is mixture, not §10's neutralizing seventh. This reading adds §8 (PDF 217–218): "The 5–6 technique is important for modulation precisely because it allows the tonic, itself normally a goal, to become an active element."

In the score:
- Over the A♭ pedal, Vln II holds E♭4 (73–74) and moves to G♭4 (75) and F♭4 (76). The F♭ is the 6 above A♭ and takes the place of the fifth E♭.
- At 76 the chord is D♭ minor 6/4 over A♭ (A♭–D♭–F♭). The tonic of the second key is no longer at rest.
- At 77–78 the same pitch, written E4, becomes the root of E major (Vln II E4, Cb E2 at 78.3).
- At 79→80 Vln II goes E4→E♭4 over the bass E→C (c430's seed).

This is the Mozart K. 333 model (Ex. 14-6b) used to leave a key: the sixth over the old tonic becomes the root of the next one. The exposition's last tonic therefore leads into the development and does not stop. That is why 73–76, the softest bars of the exposition (20 attacks in four bars, analysis.txt), do not lose momentum. Protected (c333: Vln II 73–80, Fl 1 F♭5 at 75). Keep.

### 3. The C-major confirmation at 166 lasts about 1.4 seconds. Tested against §9; keep.

§9 (PDF 219) says most modulations "confirm the new tonic by means of an authentic cadence", and the Schubert model goes deceptive, then authentic. The recapitulation does exactly that: 160 V7→♭VI fff, 162 →vi, 165–166 cadential 6/4–V7–I, D–C in Vln I. But the root-position C-major triad at 166 lasts two beats under rit. (crotchet ≈ 81–87): 368.7 s to about 370.1 s on the MIDI map. Then the violas', Cl 1's and Hn 1's E♭4 turns it to minor (166.3), and the forte coda starts at 167.

I checked whether this is too short to count as a confirmation. The answer is no, for three reasons:
- The 20-bar C-major area has already sounded its tonic: the whole of bar 147, and the IAC at 155. The cadence at 166 closes an area that the listener already knows; it does not create one. Under §10 (PDF 219), the short stay still holds the area in the ear.
- The PAC carries the melody to 1̂ (D–C), the harp's C chord and the horns' C4/E4, which is a full cadence at p.
- Withdrawing the major after two beats is the dramatic point. The finale needs the first sustained, strong C-major arrival (coherence/mvt1.md, "Do not reuse"; c240, c293, c331). Lengthening 166 would also move the tempo change at 167 (c468) and the bar numbers that other movements quote (170, 175–182).

Keep.

### 4. Where the movement is smaller than a master's: the loud plateau 113–144 and the short return. No change.

From 113 to 144, about 70 s, the music is loud except for 125–128 and the mf hemiola at 135–137. A master might give the return of the tonic more room after the fff at 122. But the recapitulation already lands on a fresh colour. The low P head (trombones, clarinets, bassoons, cellos) at 131 has a different register and layout from the violins' P at 15 and 27, so it is not a repeat at the same weight. The dip at 125–128 is enough to make 131 an arrival: the bass G1 holds and the motto stretto rises at 127–130, with Cl 1's crescendo into 131 (c498).

Making the return land harder would mean either lengthening 125–130 or thinning 131–134. Both are protected:
- 117–130 and the stretto 127–130 (c233, c263, c343, c473, c493).
- Graded brass at 131 (c264, c308).
- Section lengths (c243, c329, c433, c493, c503).

None of these reasons is weakened by anything in Unit 14. The unit treats the return of the tonic as a matter of preparation (V7, 130.3), and the score has that. The proportion question (recapitulation 0.61 of the exposition, coda 0.14 of the whole) is unchanged since v373 and was weighed against Beethoven 9/i in c493 and c503. I do not reopen it.
