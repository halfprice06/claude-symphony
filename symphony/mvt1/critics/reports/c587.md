## Findings

v587 follows every principle in the chapter that applies to an orchestra. The string changes since c487 are inner-voice and bowing details: the Cb at 164-166 (c583, c586), the cello C under the coda pulse (c568), and the Hn 1 call at 125-126 (c581), which does not touch the strings. None of them breaks a rule of this reading.

My scans agree:
- The only string second above E5 is the passing quaver at 96.
- No inner string sounds above Vln I.
- Every downbeat with no chordal third is one of these: a unison or octave line (1-5, 84-88, 96, 129), a 4-3 suspension over V (14, 32, 114, 126, 142, 150, 165), a fugato entry before the harmony fills in (91, 94), or bar 77 (Finding 1).

I then asked where, inside this lens, the music falls short of a master's. I found no change that the reading supports and that I could show to be a clear gain, so I recommend no change. The five points below are the places where the reading comes closest to asking for something else. None repeats c487's five. I made no change in the scratch copy.

### 1. 77: the development opens on a bare fifth; the third is in the solo (keep)

The reading: in Ex. 194, bars 33-35, the cellos hold two notes and the wordless vocal line supplies the chordal third. Sebesky says the illusion of chordal fullness "is made possible by the presence of the chordal third somewhere in the ensemble" (PDF 144-145).

The score: at 77 the double basses hold A♭1, the violas tremolo E♭3 and Vln II tremolo E♭4, all p. The A♭-minor third C♭ comes only in the bass clarinet's solo head, on beat 2: E♭2 on 1.5, then C♭3. From 78 the violas take C♭4.

Why it stays: this is Sebesky's own procedure. The strings hold an open pad, and the solo supplies the third and becomes part of the chord. If the violas held C♭4 from the downbeat, they would sound the head's accented C♭ before the solo reaches it. c127 moved them off that note for that reason, when it was a unison with the solo (Adler PDF 274). The bare fifth lasts a dotted crotchet, and the head's minor third arrives at once.

### 2. Bar 5: the double basses hold C2 alone (keep)

The reading: the footnote to rule 3 says the string bass can sustain a line "only in a recording studio, never in a live performance" (PDF 139).

The score: at bar 5 the double basses hold C2 pp for one bar with nothing else, and the cor anglais enters on beat 4. This is the only place in the movement where the basses sound alone.

Why it stays: a single held note between the motto (1-4) and the cor anglais entry is a pedal, not a line, and Sebesky's warning concerns a line's sonority. The bar is a deliberate thinning after the motto's low unison (B.cl, Vc, Cb). It also gives the cor anglais a clear floor for its entry, which Sebesky approves when the arco bass supplies the roots "all by himself" in the last bars of Ex. 194 (PDF 146).

### 3. Cello/bass octaves on about half the beats (keep)

The reading: rule 7 says not to double the bass notes with the lowest cello "by habit", and to keep bottom-rich doubling for special moments "used sparingly" (PDF 140; Ex. 204, PDF 157).

The score: the cellos' lowest note matches the double basses' pitch class on 294 of the 549 beats where both play. The bars fall into three groups:
- Soft passages: only the motto (1-4), the intro chords (6, 13-14), the pedals (73-76, 123-130) and the chorale and ending (175-182). These are exactly Sebesky's special moments.
- 56-68: the cello arpeggios start on the bass note. c387 protected them.
- Everything else is f-ff tutti.

Why it stays: Sebesky's reason is a studio one. A recorded bass is resonant, and the strings should "float over the foundation laid down by the rhythm section". An orchestra has no rhythm section. In the score's f-ff tuttis the bass needs the cellos, as in every Brahms tutti. Where the music is soft and floating (47-55, 107-110, 147-155, 171-174), the cellos already stay away from the bass: they carry the tune or rest, and the basses play pizz. or alone.

### 4. 147-155: the first violins rest for eight and a half bars of the recapitulated S (keep)

The reading: Ex. 201 and 202 (PDF 149-151) put the violins high on a unison counter-melody over the lower strings' pad, and Ex. 214 adds a counterline where the lead holds (PDF 168). A master might add a high violin counter to the recapitulated second theme in the major.

The score: the tune is in the violas, the cellos an octave below, and Hn 1. Vln II holds a div. sul tasto halo above them, and the cor anglais answers in two-bar units. Vln I is silent from 147 until the pickup to 156.

Why it stays:
- A violin counter-melody to the S head in C major is on the finale's reserved list in coherence/mvt1.md: "the violins' C-major statement of the S head with its chromatic counter over a C pedal". Any high Vln I line here would anticipate it.
- The violins' entry at 155.4 (the mf pickup F5, slurred into the rising sixth to D6 at 156) is the start of the climb to the fff at 160. It has force because Vln I has been silent since its pp G4 at 145-146.
- The passage is protected (c387: 147-155 pad; c427: Vln II tremolo).

### 5. 109-110: Fl 1 is marked mp over violas at p (keep)

The reading: a flute on the lead string line is marked a level softer than the strings to make up for its weight (PDF 164).

The score: at 109-110 Fl 1 joins the violas' S head marked "mp espr.", and the violas are p.

Why it stays:
- c167, an earlier pass of this same chapter, applied Sebesky's rule and set Fl 1 to pp. c178 reversed that to mp on register grounds, and HISTORY records the point as settled. It says not to change the dynamic again, and the only alternative it allows is for Fl 1 to rest.
- Sebesky's rule is for a flute on top of a violin section at mf or louder, where the flute's weight is the problem. Here the flute doubles violas at p in a register where a flute is weak, so c178's reason still holds.
- Making the flute rest would remove the colour that c141 ("violas + flute 107-110") and c307 ("Fl 1 with violas 109-110") protect.
