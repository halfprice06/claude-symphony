## What I studied

Besides the reading, I ran LilyPond's event listener on a scratch copy of v162 (one staff per player) to list every entry, dynamic and sounding bar, and counted per bar how many of the 28 non-harp players sound and how many are marked f or louder. I compared this with the MusicXML of Tchaikovsky 6/i, Dvořák 9/i and Beethoven 9/i in the local corpus (reference-corpus/musicxml-converted/xml): trombones sound in 36% of bars here, against 40% (Tchaikovsky) and 30% (Dvořák); full tutti (78% or more of the players) covers 23% of bars here, against 24% and 28%. The tutti runs here are 2–9 bars long, Tchaikovsky's and Dvořák's mostly 8–14. I viewed score pages 3–14 at 55 dpi. I did not use the web.

Overall verdict: the movement's builds and releases follow chapter IV closely. Its long crescendos add strings, woodwind and brass in that order (9–12, 19–26, 85–97, 110–117), its diminuendos drop brass first (102→107, 123–127, 162–166), and the heavy brass are used about as often as in Tchaikovsky 6/i. Critic 108 applied the same chapter with the expression lens; I did not repeat its points. Two places still miss what the chapter teaches.

## Findings (ranked)

### 1. Bars 135–137: the timpani keep playing when the low brass drop out (applied)

**Problem.** From 117 to 146 the timpani play in 29 of 30 bars: the G roll 117–130, the hammered eighths 131–134, accents on C at 135–137, then 138 and 140–146. They are the only group with no rest anywhere in this span, the movement's longest stretch at high level. At 135 the theme falls from ff to mf for the hemiola. Cycle 129 shaped that drop by removing the trombones, bass trombone and tuba, "as at 23–25, where low brass are silent". At 23–25 the timpani are silent too, but in the recapitulation they stay and accent the hemiola at mf. So the drop is made by elimination for the brass only, and the timpani's f return at 138 (set by cycle 108) and their crescendo roll into the A♭6 peak at 140 come straight after bars of their own playing, not after a rest.

**Evidence.** PDF 136: "Neither musical feeling nor the ear itself can stand, for long, the full resources of the orchestra combined together ... A group of instruments which has been silent for some time gains fresh interest upon its reappearance." PDF 130: a diminuendo is made by removing instruments in the order brass, woodwind, strings, and PDF 127 calls removing a group "essentially the diminuendo"; percussion of little sustaining power goes with the brass here. The exposition's own counterpart, 23–25, has no timpani.

**Fix (recOne.ily, applied).** `recOneTimp` bars 135–137: `R1*3 | g,2.->\f r4 |` (was `c4->\mf r4 r2 | r2 c4-> r4 | r4 c4-> r2 | g,2.->\f r4 |`). The hemiola accents stay in the contrabassoon, bassoons and basses. The timpani now rest for three bars, as the low brass do, and re-enter f on the 138 stop chord with the trumpets. The protected G roll 117–130, the stretto 126–130 and the dominant roll 144–146 are unchanged.

### 2. Bars 113–114: the brass join the wedge at mp, the level the strings have already passed (applied)

**Problem.** The wedge 111–117 is the longest crescendo in the movement. Strings, clarinets, bassoons, horns and tuba start it at mp/p cresc. at 111. Cycle 108 moved the trumpets to 113, so the brass come in after the strings and woodwind. But the trumpets, trombones (113) and bass trombone (114) still enter at mp cresc., when the strings have been crescendoing for two bars. So they enter at about the same level as everyone else and rise at the same rate, adding volume without making the crescendo steeper.

**Evidence.** PDF 127 ("Amplification and elimination of tone qualities", text missing from the OCR, read from the image): "While the first group effects the crescendo gradually, the second group enters piano or pianissimo, and attains its crescendo more rapidly. The whole process is thereby rendered more tense as the timbre changes." PDF 136: re-introducing these instruments mezzo-piano "produces a colourless and common-place effect". PDF 130: brass are the group best able to turn "crescendo chords into the most brilliant sforzando climaxes". The horns and tuba in the same passage already enter at p.

**Fix (dev.ily, applied).** Change `\mp\cresc` to `\p\cresc` in Tpt 1 `c''2.~(\p\cresc c''8 b'8)`, Tpt 2 `g'1~(\p\cresc`, Tpt 3 `es'1\p\cresc` (113), Tbn 1 `g1~(\p\cresc`, Tbn 2 `c1\p\cresc` (113) and B.tbn `d,1\p\cresc` (114). Tpt 1·2 and Tbn 1·2 share staves, and each pair carries the same mark. The ff arrivals at 116 (trumpets) and 117 (trombones), the notes, and the wedge's entry level at 111 (cycle 73) are unchanged. The brass now rise from p to ff in three to four bars against the strings' six, as PDF 127 describes.

## Considered, no change

- **77–79, development opening at mf.** The bass clarinet (77), horns and cellos (78), and oboe and violins (79) all state the head at mf, then 80–81 are f and 82 is ff. PDF 136 calls mf re-entries colourless, but that sentence is about horns, trombones and tuba after a *long* rest. Here the horns have rested five bars, and the bass clarinet is a solo over p strings, which PDF 123 asks for. The chain mf–f–ff is graded. Leave it.
- **156–158, two-bar approach to the main climax.** About 14 players join on the downbeat of 158. PDF 130 says short crescendi come from the players' own dynamics, and the strings, horns, bassoons and harp crescendo through 156–157. Cycle 83's rule against raising 147–157 stands, and the reading gives no reason to break it.
- **82–83 vs 122.** The development's opening crash uses the full brass ff, as its climax does. PDF 116 asks whether a passage is a climax or a step. It is a two-bar interruption, followed by 29 bars without trumpets, so the trumpets' return at 113/122 is still fresh (PDF 136). No change.
- **12 and 69, trombones mf.** Critic 108 weighed PDF 136 against PDF 133 and kept them. I agree.
- **171–174, cello section rather than a solo cello.** PDF 123 allows a solo "simply to obtain that singular difference in colour", but the coda is protected (cycle 130). Raise it for movement II instead.
