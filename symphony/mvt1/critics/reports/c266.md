## Findings

### 1. Tuba 120–122: one breath for a tied G1 from ff into the fff climax (applied)

**Bars and problem.** The tuba plays bars 111–126 with only four quarter rests (114, 119, 122, 125). At 120–122 it holds G1 tied for eleven beats (`g,,1~ | g,,1\<~ | g,,2.\fff r4`) through the allargando (♩=120, 114, 106). That is about 5.7 s on the lowest, most air-hungry note of the passage, crescendoing into fff. Because of the tie, the tuba has no fresh attack on the fff downbeat at 122. Trombones 1·2 and the bass trombone already breathe on the last eighth of 121 (c087, `c'2..\< r8`) and strike 122 fresh.

**Evidence.** Adler says the tuba "takes a great deal of breath... special care must be exercised not to tire out the performer. Frequent rests are strongly recommended" (adler2 PDF 8). He also says that "in a loud passage one should not phrase too many notes on one breath in a slow tempo, for it does take more breath to play loudly than softly" (adler1 PDF 292). His tuba example of strong attack, Ex. X-87 (adler2 PDF 8, `sff>p<ff` re-struck), shows the tuba's power in a fresh attack, not in a held crescendo. c087's own precedent is Tchaikovsky 6/i bar 134, a tuba breath before the arrival.

**Fix.** dev.ily, `devTuba`:
```
g,,1~ | g,,2..\< r8\! | g,,2.\fff r4 |
```
(was `g,,1~ | g,,1\<~ | g,,2.\fff r4 |`). The bass G does not stop: the contrabassoon and double basses hold G through 121–122 and the timpani roll continues (checked in full.midi). Rule c087/c107 ("the same line keeps sounding in another player") is met. The tuba now breathes with the other low brass and attacks the fff with them. c156's "tuba rests 117–125" protection keeps all its existing rests; this adds one.

### 2. Horn 3, 121–126: fourteen seconds unbroken from ff< through fff to p (applied)

**Bars and problem.** Hn 3 breathes on beat 4 of 120 and then plays without a break through 126: `g'1\< | d'1\fff | d'1 | b1\> | c'1 | c'4\p( b2.)`. This is about 13.9 s at ♩=114–100 (allargando, sostenuto), with three bars at fff. Hn 4 below it breathes on beat 4 of 123, and Hn 1·2 stop after 123. Hn 3 is the one horn left without air at the loudest point of the movement.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 292 (above), and PDF 303 (horn: "periodic rests are most welcome"). Adler PDF 309: a horn plays everything under one slur, or one unbroken phrase, on one breath. c186 gave Hn 1·2 a breath at 119 for a similar 14 s span ("Hn 1-2 breathe before the D at 119 (were unbroken 117-123, ff-fff, about 14 s)"). Hn 3 needs the same.

**Fix.** dev.ily, `devHnThree`:
```
d'1\fff | d'2. r4 | b1\> | c'1 |
```
(was `d'1\fff | d'1 | b1\> | c'1 |`). Hn 3 breathes together with Hn 4, so the 3·4 staff shows one shared quarter rest (checked on score page 12). D4 keeps sounding on that beat in Tpt 2 (D4 at 123), and Hn 1·2 hold G4/F4 through it. The re-entry at 124 is the new pitch B3, so the restrike is musical, not just a breath. Protections: c156's "brass/horn layout 117–123 (apart from breaths)" and c239's "117–130 chain" allow breaths. No pitch changes.

### 3. Hn 1 at 43: p entry on written G5 two beats after the ff low call (no change)

Hn 1 ends the four-horn call on written F4 (sounding B♭3) at ff in 42. Two beats later at 43 it enters p on written B♭4 → G5 (sounding E♭4 → C5). Adler warns about soft high attacks (PDF 284) and about leaps (PDF 304). Two things make it playable. The entry is in the bright, heroic register, not the extreme one (X-5). And the change is from loud-low to soft-middle-high with a half bar of rest, which is ordinary first-horn work: compare Tchaikovsky 5/ii in Ex. X-24 (PDF 309), where the solo enters p on written E5 and rises to A5. Moving the line to Hn 3 would gain nothing, since Hn 3 also plays the call. Keep it.
