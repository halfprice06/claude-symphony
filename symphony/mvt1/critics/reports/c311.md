## Findings

The reading is about how figuration turns a simple framework into a theme with its own character. I checked the four themes, and the places where they are restated or developed, against the 27 points above. None is violated. c221 found the same in its first pass and added one figure, the turn at 56. In this pass I looked at bars c221 did not cover and at the examples it called unused (Ex. 20-12, 20-13c–d, 20-21b, 20-24). Each of them is already in the score. I recommend no change to the score. The findings below give the evidence, ranked by how close each point came to needing a change.

### F1. P, bars 16 and 18: single and double neighbour (no change)

P's second bar circles C with its lower neighbour (C–B–C at 16). The fourth bar has the double neighbour, C–B–D–C at 17.4–18. Ex. 20-12 (PDF 334) shows the double neighbour as idiomatic outside chorale style, and PDF 333–334 says the neighbour figure is usually treated as a motive. So P varies its own motive inside four bars: single neighbour, then double neighbour, then the long held G. A listener can follow this, and it makes P easy to remember: C–B–C answered by C–B–D–C is the kind of small contrast that fixes a tune in memory. It is how the openings of Brahms's Third, first movement, and Dvořák's Seventh, first movement, work as well. The double neighbour comes back at 134, when the recapitulation states the head in full (131–134). The shortened returns at 143–144 and 167–169 keep only the single neighbour, so the full figure belongs to the full statements. The finale quotes the P rhythm (c301 protected 15–19). Keep.

### F2. Fugato subject, bars 89, 92, 95: the turn is already a motive (no change)

c221 said the turn of Ex. 20-21 was unused and added one at 56. That is not the whole picture. The fifth bar of the fugato subject is the five-note nineteenth-century turn that A&S describe (main note, upper neighbour, main note, lower neighbour, main note; PDF 338, Ex. 20-21b, Mahler). It is E♭–F–E♭–D–E♭ in the cellos at 89, A♭–B♭–A♭–G–A♭ in the violas at 92, and D♭–E♭–D♭–C–D♭ in Vln II and the cor anglais at 95. So the turn at 56 is not a one-off ornament. It is the first sound of a figure that the development takes up at every entry. This is the motivic use of the neighbour figure the reading asks for (PDF 333–334, Ex. 20-11). I considered putting the turn back into S at 64 (A♭5) or at 147 (E4). I reject both. Bars 63–64 are protected (c221), and the plain recapitulation of S at 147 must stay plain so that the major-mode G–E–D–C stays clear (mvt1.md, "Do not reuse"). Keep.

### F3. Introduction 9–12 and P 24–26: implied tones (no change)

The violins' rising sixths at 9–12 (G4–E♭5, A♭4–F5, B4–A♭5) make a polyphonic line with two strands (PDF 330, Ex. 20-3). The lower strand G4–A♭4–B4 needs a C. The violin does not play it: it leaps from B4 to A♭5. The viola supplies the C at 12, after its own C–B at 11 (c132). A&S say that a tone the context supplies may be left out of the line "without creating a loose end" (PDF 330, Ex. 20-3b). The horn call's B3 at 14 works the same way: C arrives in the bass at 15. In 24–26 the upper strand F5–G5–A♭5–B5 rises over the repeated E♭–D–C. Its A♭→B is an augmented second in the implied line, but it sits inside the vii°7 harmony, which is the exception at PDF 340 (Ex. 20-24). The cyclic loose ends (horn call 14, Tpt B4 at 170, Cl 1 B4 at 177) are deliberate and kept for the finale. They are not faults in this sense. Keep.

### F4. Run at 32.4, F–G–A♭–B→C (no change)

This is the one place where the letter of the reading could point the other way. PDF 340 says a rising figure in minor normally takes the melodic minor (A♮–B). The run F5–G5–A♭5–B5 rises to C6 at 33 and uses A♭. It sounds alone on beat 4, after the V7 strokes C–B over G on beats 1–2. A&S go on: "what goes on in the other parts and the larger harmonic context sometimes leads to the use of lowered 6̂ … ascending" (PDF 340). Here the context is the hammered tutti 27–32, whose last melodic bar (31) falls A♭6–G6–F6–D6. The A♭ is the ♭9 of the dominant that 26 and 31 have stamped on the ear, and the run repeats the tutti's own A♭ before the transition. With A♮ the run would sound like a scale exercise, and it would lose the ♭6̂ that runs through the movement (motto, A♭ climaxes at 82, 122, 160). Bar 32 is also inside the passage the finale quotes at its 199–204 (mvt1.md). Keep A♭; c221 reached the same verdict.

### F5. Sevenths in the theme cadences (no change)

Ex. 20-18c (PDF 337) is the reading's test for a figurated melody: the 7th of V7 must resolve. I checked every cadence of P and S. At 54→55 the tune's D♭4 falls to C4. At 63→64 and 67→68 the violins leave D♭ by leap (D♭6–B♭5, D♭5–B♭4), and the C comes in the violas (D♭4→C4 at 68), Hn 2 and Cl 1. At 154→155 Hn 1 and the violas take F4→E4. In the coda at 173–174 the N6 goes to the cadential 6/4 with C5→B4 in the solo cello. Each resolution is present. Where it is moved out of the tune, the leap draws attention to the soprano dissonance, as PDF 336 recommends. Keep.

### F6. Spelling of chromatic passing tones (no change)

I parsed every section file for three-note chromatic runs and compared each spelling with the rule of PDF 339 (Ex. 20-22: the chromatic half step comes first, raised going up, lowered going down). The motto-derived P head (E♭–D–D♭–C), the transition (C–C♭–B♭ at 34), S's mixture turn (B♭–B♭♭–A♭ at 60), the flute motto (G–G♭–F at 74), the fugato subject (E♭–E♮–F) and the recapitulation's D–D♭–C (156) all follow it. The script flagged only bar 96 (B♭–A♮–A♭). There A♮ is the leading note of B♭ minor inside the P-head shape, so the rule does not apply (F4 reasoning; PDF 340). Nothing to change.

## Would a listener hum them afterwards?

Yes, for three of the four. The motto (a minor sixth up, then falling chromatically) can be sung after one hearing, and the movement sounds it at least eight times in different colours. P has the profile of Tchaikovsky's Fifth, first movement, and Brahms's First: a wide leap, two short neighbour cells and a peak at D♭6. S is a long-breathed Dvořák-type tune whose head grows out of the horn call's E♭–C. The horn call is remembered as a colour more than as a tune, which is its job. The reading gives no reason to rewrite any of them. Its main lesson, that figuration should be the substance of a melody and not a coat of paint on it, is already the method of this movement.
