## Findings

### 1. Bar 55: "molto cantabile" runs into the c321 "sul D" and in Vln II sits over the wrong beat. Applied.

What is wrong. c321 (v322) added a "sul D" text spanner on the violins' pickup E♭4 at 55.4. The existing "molto cantabile" is right-aligned to the same note, so on page 6 it ends exactly where "sul D" begins and the two read as one phrase, "molto cantabile sul D". In Vln II the right-aligned column "molto cantabile / unis." reaches back over beat 1, above the "tutti, div." chord, so the expression mark looks as if it belongs to the held chord, and "unis." touches "sul D" ("unis.sul D"). The collision with "sul D" is new since v319 (the only thing in v319→v329 that got worse); the Vln II text over beat 1 is older but is fixed by the same change.

Evidence. The reading's own model for this passage is Ex. 12 (PDF 186: "Viol. I e II unis.", then "sul G"), where the string indication stands alone over its note. Rimsky-Korsakov's point at PDF 57 is that the unison and the string choice are the colour of the phrase; the mark must be legible as a separate instruction. A conductor reading "unis.sul D" over a pickup in an otherwise clean page sees a proofing slip.

Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-329/work/sections/tTwo.ily). Left-align the expression text at the pickup so it stacks above the spanner, and make the Vln II column left-aligned:

```
% Vln I, bar 55
r2. \once \override TextScript.self-alignment-X = #LEFT \once \override TextSpanner.bound-details.left.text = \markup \italic \small "sul D" es'4(\p^\markup \italic "molto cantabile"^\startTextSpan | ...
% Vln II, bar 55
<es' aes'>2^\markup \italic "tutti, div." r4 \once \override TextScript.self-alignment-X = #LEFT ... es'4(\p^\markup \left-column { \italic "molto cantabile" "unis." }^\startTextSpan | ...
```

Result on page 6: "molto cantabile" (and "unis.") start at the pickup and sit above "sul D - - -"; "tutti, div." stands alone over beat 1. The text ends at the system's right edge (55 is the last bar of the system), so nothing moves; 18 pages, same parts page counts. No notes change.

### 2. Holistic comparison with Bruckner 7/i: what the score shares with it. Keep.

Bruckner opens with the main theme in the cellos with violas and a horn, in unison, over a pp violin tremolo on the tonic; the tune is a long rising arch in the cellos' tenor register. That is Rimsky-Korsakov's unison of a tenor string line with a wind of the same register (PDF 57–58, Ex. 15 and 17: the cello tone prevails, the wind is absorbed). This score's second theme at 47–55 is the same sound (Hn 1 + cellos in unison, pp pads), and its recapitulation at 147–155 is Bruckner's texture almost exactly: violas and cellos with Hn 1 on the tune, Vln II div. sul tasto tremolo as the halo (PDF 60 (d): violas and cellos in octaves while the violins are busy). A conductor who knows Bruckner 7 would accept both.

Bruckner's second group is a wind-led song (oboe and clarinet) over a moving string accompaniment, and his development and recapitulation keep the choirs separate in blocks. This score does the same at 103–106 (Ob 2 + Cl 2 on the P head, the Ex. 54 unison, against horn and cellos on the S head) and 117–121 (brass motto over woodwind and string triplets). Bruckner builds his climaxes on long pedals; the G pedal 117–130 and the C pedal 167–170 do the same. Bruckner separates groups with general pauses; the stops at 14, 26, 138 and 170 do the same job. None of this needs a change.

### 3. Holistic: where Bruckner 7/i differs, and why the score should stay as it is.

(a) Breath of the themes. Bruckner's main theme runs about 20 bars before it cadences; this movement's P is an 8-bar sentence (15–22) and its S a 9-bar period (47–55). In a 7½-minute movement with a cyclic motto and three theme statements in each group, longer themes would break the proportions that cycles 215, 243 and 283 measured and protected. The S period with its extension to 68 already gives a 22-bar lyrical span. Keep.

(b) Inversion. Bruckner's development turns the main theme upside down and his recapitulation combines it with its inversion. This score never inverts a theme; its development instead combines the two heads (103–110) and runs a fugato (85–96). Adding an inversion would mean new bars in the protected development (c215, c243) and would duplicate work the fugato already does. Keep.

(c) Coda. Bruckner's coda is a long crescendo over a tonic pedal with timpani to a full-brass E major. This score's coda (171–182) fades over the motto to ppp and goes attacca into movement II. The major full-brass apotheosis is on the "Do not reuse" list for the finale, and the soft attacca is protected (c239, c603). The score's coda has Bruckner's tonic pedal and timpani pulse (179–180), without his crescendo, which is the right choice for the cycle. Keep.

### 5. Woodwind order inside the tutti chords. Keep.

The scan finds Ob 2 below Cl 1 in 66 places, almost all at 27–32, 69–71, 82 and 139. In each, Ob 2 holds a harmony note while Cl 1 doubles the tune an octave below the flutes and Ob 1. Rimsky-Korsakov's warning against inverted order (PDF 65, 67) concerns a melody given to two woodwinds in octaves or thirds (Ex. 56–59). Here the inversion is a held chord tone under a doubling, the "overlaying" that his chapter III allows in tutti (PDF 106–114). 27–32 is also quoted at pitch by the finale. Keep.

### 6. Double bass alone at bar 5. Keep.

PDF 56 says the double bass is never given a phrase of importance without cellos or bassoons. At bar 5 the basses hold C2 pp alone for one bar while the cor anglais enters. It is a held tonic, not a phrase, and since c328 it is the end of the motto's C3 handed down an octave. The examples show the same (Ex. 40, PDF 218: soli basses holding under a wind solo). Keep.
