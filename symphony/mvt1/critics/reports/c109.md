## Holistic judgement: would a conductor believe a master wrote this?
- **Overall:** yes, on the evidence of this reading. I checked every melodic statement against Chapter II and its first 60 examples.
- **Lyrical themes:** each sits in one of Rimsky's recommended combinations:
  - horn + cello (47–55);
  - violins in unison that open into octaves (56–63);
  - violas + cellos + horn as a heavier third hearing (147–155);
  - cello cantabile against a solo cor anglais (171–174).
- **Woodwind solos:** they are accompanied the way Rimsky accompanies his: sustained pp strings, pizzicato basses, harp.
- **Tuttis and brass:** the loud tuttis double the tune in winds and strings, which Rimsky allows for loud passages (PDF 66–67). The brass carry the theme only where it is vigorous: four horns at 41, the trombone motto at 117, the brass head at 167.
- **Against Tchaikovsky 2/i:**
  - Both frame the movement with the introduction's melody in its original colour. Tchaikovsky uses the horn. This score uses the bass-register motto and the cor anglais, which returns at 171.
  - Both shorten the first group in the recapitulation. Tchaikovsky goes further and keeps his first subject for the coda.
  - This score's 16-bar recapitulated first group plus the brass head at 167 is within that practice.
- **Weakness:** the one weakness this reading exposes is small. A low flute doubles a viola section at p (Finding 1).
- **Unresolved:** the larger questions about this movement (melodic memorability, proportion) are outside what Chapter II measures.

## Findings

### 1. Bars 107–108: the low flute doubling the violas adds nothing (applied)
- **Problem.**
  - Since cycle 74, Fl 1 doubles the violas at 107–110 on the second-theme head, p/mp espr.
  - At 107–108 the line lies at G4–C5 (g'2.~ g'8 fis' | e'4 c''2 b'4). That is the bottom of the flute's range, its weakest register.
  - A whole viola section plays the same line there in the same register.
- **Evidence.**
  - Rimsky PDF 76: when a wind and a string instrument of equal power play in unison, the string predominates. "If several wind instruments play in unison with one group of strings, the latter will be overpowered." The reverse also holds: one weak wind under a section is absorbed.
  - PDF 76–77 lists the natural unisons by matching register. For violas the list is Ob/CA, Cl and Bsn. The flute is listed only with violins.
  - PDF 66–67: in soft passages, doubling costs expression without adding colour.
  - Every low-register flute in the examples is a solo over a very thin pp background. Ex. 37 (p. 215) is 2 Fl in the low register over Vln II/Vla pp. Ex. 39 (p. 217) is the alto flute solo over divided violas.
  - At 109–110 the line rises to D♭5–A♭5. There the flute speaks and the doubling does add colour.
- **Answer to cycle 74's reason (a fresh colour at 107):** the colour change at 107 is the violas themselves, alone and espressivo after the f winds. The flute's colour then arrives at 109, where it can be heard.
- **Fix (dev.ily, devFluteOne, bars 107–110):**

### 2. No other conflicts with this reading (no change proposed)
I checked every melodic statement in rows 1–20 of the table. Two places look as if they break Rimsky's rules, but they do not:
- **131–134 trombones unslurred under slurred bassoons and cellos.** Rimsky PDF 75: "the addition of a wind to a brass instrument yields a finer legato effect than when the latter instrument plays alone". The bassoons and cellos supply the legato. This is the deliberate exception from cycle 8, and the book supports it.
- **147–155 three-group unison at p.** Rimsky PDF 79 names "a heavy piano effect" as a proper use. The exposition (Hn + Vc) is the light version and the recapitulation the heavier one, which is the intended difference between the two hearings.

I am not inventing further findings: the reading gives no grounds for them.
