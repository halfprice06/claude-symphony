## Findings

This pass applies no change to the score. I checked the four themes against the reading bar by bar, and also against my lens: would a listener hum them, and do they hold their own beside Tchaikovsky, Dvořák and Brahms? The reading confirms what is there. The three points below are the only places where a principle could be read against the score. For each I give the reason to keep the bars as they are, so later critics need not reopen them.

### Finding 1. Bars 131–134: the low P head is audible. Keep it.

**Question.** PDF 54 says a melody in the low register stands out "to a less degree" than one at the top. At 131–134 the P head returns in Cl 1·2 (ff), Bsn 1·2, Tbn 1·2 (f) and Vc (ff), all in unison around G3–F4. Above it Fl, Ob and Vln I·II play E♭6–D♭6–C6–B5 / A♭5–F5–D5 at ff, and the trumpets and timpani beat the pulse.

**Assessment.** The same page gives the remedy for a melody that is not on top: "strengthening of resonance by doubling, tripling". The head here is quadrupled across three families, and the trombones give it a colour nothing else in the texture has (I looked at page 13 of the score). The upper line is sustained and moves on the off-beats, so it does not sit on the head's attacks. Ex. 33 (PDF 212) shows the low register used for a "rough and severe" phrase. That is the character this recapitulation needs after the dominant pedal. Rimsky's caveat is about prominence, not about audibility. The head is the only moving line in its register, so it is heard. c84, c127 and c427 protect the layout. Nothing in the reading answers their reason, so I keep it.

### Finding 2. Bars 97–102 and 158–161: Vln I over Vln II + Vc is Rimsky's "rarely found" arrangement. Keep it.

**Question.** PDF 61, Ex. 30, lists two ways of putting violins and cellos in octaves: "Vln I / Vln II + Vc" and "Vln I + II / Vc", and says "the first arrangement is rarely found". The score uses the first one in both of its big S statements: B minor ff at 97–102 (c231 added the cellos) and the fff climax at 158–161.

**Assessment.** The arrangement is rare because Vln I alone on top is thinner than the two sections below it. In both places the score corrects that balance with wind. At 97–102 Fl 1 and Ob 1 double Vln I's top. At 158–161 both flutes double it, and Ob 2 and Hn 1·2 add to the lower octaves, which makes it the double octave of PDF 62 (Ex. 32). The note on PDF 63 also says that uneven numbers between octaves "is not of any great importance, for … the partial harmonics of one octave support the tone of the other". The arrangement also gives the lower octave the cello colour that the reading values (PDF 58: "the 'cello tone prevails"), on the tune the cellos introduced at 47. Keep it.

### Finding 3. Bars 94–96: Fl 1 and Vln I reach D♭6 in unison with no lower octave. Keep it.

**Question.** Note I (PDF 62) says melodies above the middle of the fifth octave are generally doubled an octave below. A scan of full.midi for notes of A5 or higher lasting a crotchet or more, with no octave below at the onset, found only flute and oboe notes. The only melodic case is the last fugato entry: Fl 1 with Vln I on F at 94, D♭6 at 94 and 96.

**Assessment.** This is a fugato voice, not a cantabile melody. A lower octave would double a line that already sounds in the other three voices and blur the counterpoint. c133 and c177 protect the entries. Note I is about melodies in octave doubling, and the flute gives this entry the firmness that PDF 59 says an isolated high line needs. The other cases are held chord notes (Fl 1 F6 at 169–170, Fl 2 F6 at 140) and Ob 2 inside the 103–106 combination. None of them is a melody. Keep them.

### Melody and memorability: the themes

- **Motto (1–4, 177–182).** It has six notes, one leap and a chromatic fall, and it is coloured differently at each return (B.cl + Vc sul G; C.a.; Fl over the A♭ pedal; trombones; Vln I sul D). In this respect it is close to Tchaikovsky 5. It is the movement's most memorable idea. Keep it.
- **P (15–22).** It is a sentence: the motto head, its sequence a step higher, then a climb to the Neapolitan D♭6 and a stepwise fall. Its rhythm is busier than the others, so it is the least singable theme. But its head is the motto, so the listener already knows it, and the finale quotes 27–32 at pitch. Vln I alone at f over mf syncopations is the relief by position and dynamics that PDF 54–55 describe. Keep it.
- **Horn call (13–14).** E♭3–C4–B3 with the B left hanging. It has three notes and a clear contour, and the scherzo and movement II answer it. Keep it.
- **S (46.4–68).** It is a period of long notes on strong beats with one sixth per phrase, in the line of Dvořák and of Brahms 2. Each of its settings is one the reading describes: the cello on the A string (PDF 56), then the violins an octave higher (Ex. 7), violins and cellos in octaves at ff (PDF 60–61), violas and cellos in octaves while the violins are held back (PDF 60), and double octaves at the climax (PDF 62). At the climax the rising sixths G5–E6 and C6–A♭6 complete the motto's leap in major. Keep it.

No theme change is warranted from this reading. The themes are quoted by movements II and IV, and the reading supports every setting of them.
