## Findings (ranked)

No finding calls for a score change. They are ranked by how much each would matter if my verdict were wrong.

### 1. Would a listener hum the themes, and is any of them weak? (PDF 220 Q6; no change)

Question 6 (PDF 220) asks whether a tune is weak in melody or harmony and what can make up for it. I asked it of each theme, and asked separately where each falls short of the masters named in the brief.

- **Motto** (1–4). A rising minor sixth and a chromatic fall that turns back up a semitone: two features a listener keeps. It returns in fixed colours, as Tchaikovsky's motto does in his Fifth. No weakness.
- **P** (15–22). The head (pickup, accented E♭5, chromatic fall D–D♭–C) is the most memorable cell in the movement and returns at 17, 27, 29, 69, 77–83, 103, 131 and 167. The consequent climbs by a D♭-major arpeggio to one peak (D♭6 sf at 20) and falls by step to the cadence at 22. Its weak point, measured against Brahms 1/i or Tchaikovsky 4/i, is that the descent 21–22 is a plain scale. c221 and c421 kept it on purpose, as a contrast to the leaps at 31 and 141–142, and c421 protected it. The reading offers nothing that answers that reason, so I do not reopen it.
- **S** (46.4–55). The head has the motto's rhythm and rising sixth and is planted by 13, 41 and 43. It grows each time it returns (D♭6 at 63, f at 64; G5–E6, then C6–A♭6 fff at 158–160). In Dvořák 8/i and Elgar 1/i the second subject also earns its memorability by return and growth, not by a second hook. No weakness that a note change would fix.
- **Horn call** (13–14). A signal built from the S incipit with B♮. It does its job.

Sebesky's question 4 (how to keep a known tune fresh) is answered by the colour plan and the reharmonised returns. Any change to P or S would also break the finale's quotations (I 15–16 at IV 247–248, I 27–32 at IV 199–204, I 146–150 at IV 247–250).

### 2. Can every part be sung? Leap scan of all parts (PDF 213, 216 note E, 217; no change)

Sebesky: "Giving each individual part in your ensemble as meaningful and interesting a melodic line as possible" (PDF 213); "Sing or play each one to yourself and see if they feel natural" (note E, PDF 216); "Some arrangers ... sing a lot" (PDF 217); and Ex. 283 drops a note to avoid an awkward line. No earlier pass of any lens scanned every part for awkward leaps. sing.py lists every leap of 6, 10 or 11 semitones or more than 12, between notes no more than one beat apart, in the top and bottom voice of each staff. It finds 235. I sorted them:

- **Inside a theme or its quotation (most of them).** The P head's rising seventh G–F (the second half of bar 17's shape) in every statement: 29.2, 133.2, 139.2, 169.2 in Fl 1, Ob 1, Cl 1·2, Bsn 1·2, Hn 1·2, Tpt 1·2, Tbn 1·2, Vln II, Vla. These are the theme, not a voice-leading choice.
- **Arpeggios and string figures.** 78 in the cellos and 29 in the violas, nearly all in the arpeggiated accompaniments 56–68 and 158–166 or the double stops at 26 and 138. These are string idioms (Ex. 284 itself has "big leaps" in violas and cellos, PDF 217–218).
- **Stop chords and phrase joins.** Fl 1 and Cl 1 F→B at 26.1 and 138.1, Hn 3 A♭3→D4 at 26.1 and 138.1, Cl 1 E♭4→G5 at 167.2 (new phrase). Each starts a new chord or phrase, which the reading accepts when a change of dynamic or colour covers it (PDF 218).
- **Protected passages.** The bass tritone A♭→D at 160→161 in Bsn 1·2, Cbsn, B.tbn and tuba (c172); the brass counter C♯4→G3 at 100 in Hn 1·2, Tbn 1, Bsn 1 (B-minor block, c117, c424); Cl 2 and violas F4→B3 alternating at 117–119 (the motto's inner alternation, c133, c292); Hn 4 B♭3→F♭3 at 61 (c312); Cl 1/Hn 2 G4→D♭4 at 67.4 (counterline 56–68, c441); Tpt 1 B4→F4 at 114 (wedge, c252, c382); Fl 1·2 D6→B4 at 117.1½ (the flute pickups crossing Ob 1, c429).

What remains are single leaps that a player meets once and that sit inside a chord change: Vln II A♭4→D4 at 21.3½ (after a quaver rest, back to its syncopation), Bsn 2 D3→A♭2 at 12.1 (German sixth), Hn 3 A♭3→G4 at 158.1 (new phrase, ff), B.cl C2→B♭2 and back at 92–94 (fugato bass). None is a line that "feels unnatural" in Sebesky's sense: none is a run of awkward intervals, and none is repeated. Two consecutive tritones in one part occur only at 117–119 (Cl 2, Vla), where they are the point. Verdict: follows.

### 3. Rapid repeated notes in one player (Ex. 282–283, notes F and I, PDF 216–217; no change)

Sebesky crosses voices or drops a note so that no player strikes the same pitch twice in quick succession (note F), because "those repeated notes are very awkward to play" in his idiom (Ex. 282). sing.py (rep) lists every restruck pitch at a quaver or faster outside the strings: 18 places in winds and brass.

- At 27–30, 131–134 and 168 the trumpets' repeated notes are the timpani rhythm of the hammered tutti (c164, protected; the finale quotes 27–32 at pitch).
- The others are single pairs (one quaver followed by the same pitch): Cl 1/Hn 2 at 59.1, Cl 1 at 78.3, B.cl at 78.1, Hn 1·2 at 79.3, Bsn 1/Hn 1·2/Tbn 1 at 97.1, Ob 2/Cl 1·2 at 104.1, C.a. at 92.1, Ob 1/Cl 1/Hn 3·4 at 161.1. Each is a restrike across a beat or a slur break, not a run.

Sebesky's concern is a stream of repeated notes in a relaxed swing style at fast tempo. Orchestral winds and brass restrike a quaver at crotchet = 112–126 without difficulty, and here each restrike marks an articulation the line needs. Not a fault.

### 4. Giving an entering voice its pitch (Ex. 285 bar 10, PDF 222; no change, one record correction)

In Ex. 285 the last horn note (F♮) gives the singer the first note of the next phrase, because complex harmony can hide it. I checked the fourteen exposed theme entries (cue.py, at.py):

- Cue present just before the entry: Vln I G4 at 9.4 (C.a. G4, Rimsky-Korsakov's transfer on a common note, c160); Vln I G4 at 15.1½ (G held in Bsn 2, Vc and Cb through beat 3 of 14); Hn 1/Vc E♭3 at 46.4 (Cl 1 E♭4, Cb E♭2); violins E♭4 at 55.4 (harp E♭5); Hn 1/Vla G3 at 146.4 (Vln I G4 to 146.3, then a beat's silence, c159); Tbn 1 G3 at 175 (Bsn 1 G3, timpani G2); Cl 1 E♭4 at 175.4 (Tbn 2 E♭3).
- No cue: C.a. D4 at 5.4 (over Cb C2 alone, the motto a fifth higher); solo Vc E♭4 at 171 (after the fermata stop chord over C); Vln I G4 at 177.4.

**Record correction.** c471 (Reading notes row 26, Finding 5) wrote that "Vln II holds G4 under Vln I's last motto at 177.4". That has not been true since v512: c512 changed Vln II at 177 to A♭4–F4 to remove octaves with Tbn 2. At 177.4 the G now comes at the same instant in the violas (A♭3→G3, c262), making V4/3 under the pickup, not before it.

The three entries without a cue do not need one. Sebesky's device serves a singer who has to find a pitch by ear in complex harmony. An orchestral player reads the pitch, and each of these entries is either over a bare octave (5.4), after a general pause on a clear chord (171), or on a chord tone of a plain V4/3 (177.4). Restoring Vln II G4 at 177 would undo c512. No change.
