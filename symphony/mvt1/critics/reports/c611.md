### 1. The changes since v521 keep the reading's rules. No change.

The notes have changed in six places since c521 (v521): c522 (violas E4–F4 at 159), c568 (coda ties 179–181, Vln I and Vc slur D♭–C), c581 (Hn 1 horn-call recall 125–126), c583 (Cb G1 on 165.3), c604 (violas B3/F4 through 161) and c607 (Cl 2 on the climax counter 160–161). I checked each one against the table.

- c522 and c604 do not touch a melody. The violas' F4 on 159.2 meets the cellos' F4 for one beat, which is the viola–cello unison of PDF 57, where the cellos predominate. B3/F4 is a tritone double stop. PDF 62 Note II forbids octaves inside a divided section, and there are none.
- c583 puts the basses an octave below the cellos at a cadence. That is the normal bass layout of PDF 61 f.
- c568 leaves the last motto in Vln I alone, on top, pp, sul D (rows 3, 4 and 9). Taking out the re-strikes makes the inner parts more of a background, which PDF 54 asks for.
- c607 changes only the woodwinds. The string layout of 158–161 is still the double octave of PDF 62 with the wind support of PDF 63.
- c581 is discussed in finding 2.

### 2. The horn-call recall at 125–126. Close the open item and keep it as written.

**Bars 125–126.** Since c581, Hn 1 plays E♭3 on 125.2, C4 on 125.3–126.1 and B3 on 126.2–3, mp espr. > p. From the C4 it is at unison with Cl 2, Hn 3, the violas and the cellos' upper note, all of which hold C4 from 125.1 (full.midi). c594, c604 and c610 carried "the recall is heard as an inner voice" as an open item. I answer it here so it can be closed.

**What the reading says.** PDF 54 says that a melody in the middle register "is not so prominent", and that timbre, doubling or crossing must then make it stand out. Measured by that rule, the call's C4 does not stand out: it lands on a pitch that four parts are already holding, one of them a horn.

**Why it should stay.**
- At 125–126 the melody is the Ob 1/Vln I line E♭5–D5–C5–D5, on top. It stands out by position (PDF 54), as the rule asks. The call is a second voice, a reminiscence.
- The part of the call that carries its identity can be heard. E♭3 on 125.2 is a fresh attack. No other part has an E♭ in that octave on that beat (only Cl 1 and Vln II, an octave higher). The rising sixth starts from it.
- The only way to let the C4 come through as a new attack is to take C4 out of all four holding parts. Taking it out of Hn 3 alone, which c589 tested, still leaves C4 in Cl 2, the violas and the cellos. Taking it out of all four would remove the six-four's fourth from the held chord and the 4–3 C–B from the inner voices (c418). Hn 1 alone would then carry the 4–3 at mp, undoubled, which is exactly what 13–14 does. 13–14 would no longer be the only undoubled horn melody, and that distinction is in coherence/mvt1.md and is answered by the scherzo and movement II.
- c594 placed the five statements of the call at different distances: near at 13, ff at 41, inside the texture at 125, farthest in Cl 1 pp at 175–177. That plan is like Tchaikovsky's Fifth, where the motto's middle returns are partly hidden in the texture before the finale brings it out. This movement leaves the call's apotheosis to the finale (the major call and the trumpets' B→C), so a half-hidden recall at 125 is the right weight.

Recommendation: record the item as closed ("125–126 recall is a reminiscence inside the texture by design; E♭3 pickup and rising sixth exposed"). Change nothing.

### 3. The themes against the masters, through this reading. No change.

I looked for a phrase that could sing more, or a climax that could land harder, where the reading or a master's practice gives a reason to change it.

- **S (46.4–68 and its returns).** This is the tune a listener would hum: long notes on strong beats, one rising sixth per phrase, and an appoggiatura swell at each half cadence. Its scoring follows the reading's examples. The cellos with the horn on the A string come first (PDF 56; Tchaikovsky and Brahms both use horn with cellos for a second-theme colour). The violins then take it an octave higher (Ex. 7). They play in unison, sul D then sul A, as in Ex. 12, and go into octaves at the mf, as in Ex. 22. The S is double-octave and fff at 158–161 (Ex. 32). Dvořák's Seventh, first movement, plans the same growth: its second theme is first soft in the winds, then broad in the violins and full at the recapitulation. I tested no alternative doubling, for two reasons. Every one I could name was tested in earlier passes (c521: strings at 52–53, Fl 1 over the violas at 151–155). And the exposition's only doubling choice, Fl 1 at 64 and not at 62–63, is supported by Ex. 22 and 23, where the woodwinds join the violins' cantabile late.
- **Motto.** It changes colour at every return (B.cl + Vc, C.a., Fl 1, brass, trombone chorale, Vln I), as in Tchaikovsky's Fifth. Its two string statements use the colours this chapter gives: low cellos sul G with basses an octave below (PDF 58, 61), and Vln I alone, high and soft (PDF 54–55). Keep.
- **P (15–22).** Vln I alone, f, reaches D♭6 at 20 with Vln II joining an octave below on the sf (Note I, PDF 62). Its head is the motto, so the listener knows it. Of the four themes it is the least singable, because its rhythm is the busiest. But the finale quotes 27–32 at pitch, and nothing in the reading asks for more relief than position and dynamics already give. Keep.
- **Horn call (13–14, 41–42).** Three notes, the rising sixth and an unresolved semitone. It is the movement's signal, and movements II and III answer it at pitch. Keep.
- **The one weak spot in this lens** is still the two-bar climb at 156–157 into the S climax. Glazunov or Bruckner would build over eight or more bars. It is protected for form and proportion (the bar count is quoted by the other movements), and this reading says nothing about phrase length. I do not reopen it.
