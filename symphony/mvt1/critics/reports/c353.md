## Findings

The first pass (c263) checked every 6/4 and the main arrivals and recommended no change. This pass tested the places c263 did not treat: the approach to the second climax, the seventh of the retransition dominant, the cadences that articulate the form, the end of the exposition, and the edits made since v263. I again recommend no change to the score. Each finding gives the reason a passage should stay, so that later cycles do not reopen it without answering the reading.

### 1. The approach to the climax, 155–158, is the minor ascending-fifths segment VII–IV–I–V; it should stay two bars (no change)

What the reading says. PDF 250: ascending 5ths "are not strongly goal-directed"; the usable segment in minor is III–VII–IV–I–V, and Ex. 16-2 (PDF 251) shows that the I before the cadential V "is a I in name only, not in behavior". PDF 260 (Points for Review 2) reduces such a series to a rising stepwise line.

What the score does. After the soft C-major cadence at 155, the bass falls C–B♭ (156, ♭VII) – F (157, iv) – G (158). The chords are ♭VII–iv–I6/4–V: exactly the VII–IV–I–V tail of the reading's minor series, with the "I" appearing as the cadential 6/4 of 158, a tonic in name only. The step F→G at 157→158 is what gives the progression its direction; B♭→F, the ascending 5th, is the weak link.

Why it stays short. Earlier critics asked for a longer approach to 158 (c083, c143, c243, c283) and were refused on proportion grounds. The reading gives a second reason: a longer approach would stretch the non-directional part of the progression (♭VII–iv), which the book says cannot establish its goal, while the part that drives to the goal is already a single bass step. In the exposition the equivalent rise is carried by the stepwise bass G♭–F♭–E♭ at 60–62 (c273), also short. The two-bar approach is the right length for this harmony.

### 2. The seventh of the retransition dominant resolves at the recapitulation, in a real voice (no change; add Hn 2 F4 at 130.3 to the protected notes)

What the reading says. PDF 312–313 (Ex. 19-14, Mozart "Mi tradì"): a passing 6/4 over a sustained dominant connects V7 with V, but "the motion to the 6/4 does not resolve the 7th of V7 … Normally, a resolution of the 7th will follow the dominant", in Mozart several bars later and in another voice.

What the score does. Over the G pedal, F is the seventh of V7 at 117, 119, 122–124. At 124→125 the upper parts move F→E♭ into the i6/4 (Vln I F5→E♭5, Vln II F4→E♭4, Ob 1 F5→E♭5): by the reading this is passing motion, not the resolution. From 125 to 130.2 no F sounds. Hn 2 then re-enters on F4 at 130.3, under the motto stretto, and moves to E♭4 on the downbeat of 131 with the tonic. The seventh of the fourteen-bar dominant is therefore resolved at the recapitulation itself, in one voice, as in the Mozart example. c263 explained why the leading tone may be absent in 128–130 (PDF 322–323); this is the other half of the same arrival: the dissonance that the pedal has carried since 117 is resolved exactly where the form needs it.

Proportion, compared with the corpus. The pedal 117–130 is 29.2 s of the development's 105.3 s (28%). In Tchaikovsky 6/i the last dominant region before the recapitulation (MCTL form_analysis.csv 1062–1214, harmony.csv: V, V7 and vii°7/V with VI6 neighbours at 1146 and 1190) is 152 of the development's 576 crotchets (26%). The share matches. Keep Hn 2 F4 at 130.3–131 as it is: removing it would leave the seventh unresolved at the recapitulation.

### 3. Every cadence that marks a section boundary uses major V, and the one minor V is a key area (no change)

What the reading says. PDF 259 §11: V without a leading tone "does not tend very actively toward I", which makes it ineffective in V–I and "in half and deceptive cadences". §12: minor V "often expands into a key area".

What the score does. I checked the dominant at each boundary or cadence of the form: end of the introduction (14), end of P (32), end of the transition (46, G of E♭7 in A♭), the deceptive cadences of S (63→64, 67→68, G♮ present), the development's half cadence before the fugato (83, B♮ in Ob 2, Hn 4, Tpt 3, Vln I, violas), V of F minor (110, E♮), the lead into the recapitulated S (146, B♮), and the deceptive moves of the climax (159→160, 161→162). Each has its leading tone. The only dominant without one, 128–130, is the elided V of row 30, which c263 accounted for.

At 83→85 the score uses §12 directly. Bar 83 is V♭9 of C, ff; bar 84 is the bare G octave in cellos and basses; the fugato then begins in G minor (85). The bare octave lets the dominant of C become the tonic of a minor-V key area without a B♮/B♭ cross-relation, and the subject's G minor is heard as a new key, not as a weak V. Keep 83–85.

### 4. The exposition closes on a prolonged tonic, and the A♭ 6/4 at 72 is the consonant kind (no change)

What the reading says. PDF 304–305 (Ex. 19-1): the same 6/4 can be consonant (from an arpeggiated bass, approached and left by leap) or dissonant (cadential); context decides. PDF 308–309: the neighbouring 6/4 over a held bass is a sustained-bass IV inside I.

What the score does. The A♭ 6/4 over E♭ appears twice in the exposition. At 62 it is cadential (sub. p, resolving to V7 at 63). At 72.2 the bass moves A♭1–E♭2–A♭1 under a root-position A♭ chord in the full tutti: an arpeggio 6/4, consonant, as the first chord of Ex. 19-1. So bar 72 is a closed tonic, not a renewed cadential delay. The flute motto 73–76 then prolongs I over the A♭ pedal, with the D♭-minor iv6/4 at 76 as a neighbouring 6/4 (sustained-bass iv), and the development starts at 77 on the A♭-minor chord over the same bass. The seam 76→77 is a change of mode on a held chord, not a new start. This is why the development can begin softly without sounding disconnected. Keep 69–80 (c240, c333).

The climax 158 follows the same logic from the other side. There the tune's 6th, E, rises E–F–G instead of falling to D; its resolution goes to Cl 2 (E5→D5) and Tbn 1 (C4→D4) (transferred resolution, PDF 315–316), and Tbn 1 is the doubled 4th that rises (PDF 316). The top line therefore does not close at 158, and the ♭VI at 160 comes as an interruption of an unresolved line. Keep 158–159 (c281, c283).

### 5. The edits made since v263 added no 6/4 and gave one existing 6/4 a real voice (no change)

What the reading says. PDF 323: never use a 6/4 without a clear idea of its function. PDF 309: the neighbouring 6/4 arises from 5–6–5 or 3–4–3 over a held bass.

What the score does. The beat search on v353 finds the same 6/4 sonorities c263 found on v263. Of the ninety cycles since, the edit that touches a 6/4 is c284/v285: Hn 3 now holds B3 at 122–124 and moves to C4 at 125, then back to B3 on beat 2 of 126. That makes the i6/4 at 125 a 3–4–3 neighbouring figure in one horn, doubled by violas and cellos (the vl.txt doublings B3→C4 at 125), instead of a chord that appears without a voice leading into it. The other changes since v263 near 6/4s (bassoon octaves at 158–161, c334; Cl 1 octave at 156–157, c337) double the bass of the 6/4 or sit on the ♭VII and iv chords before it, as PDF 307 asks. Nothing needs correcting.
