## Findings

### 1. The themes under this lens: no change

The reading's string tunes share a few traits. Brahms 1/iv (Ex. III-4), Tchaikovsky 5/ii (Ex. III-5), Brahms 3/iii (Ex. III-7) and Tristan (Ex. III-59) each:
- have one contour with a single high point, reached by one or two characteristic leaps and left by step;
- repeat a short rhythmic cell, so that the second phrase is recognised as an answer;
- give the return of the tune a new string colour, not new notes.

The four themes of this movement meet each point:
- **Motto (1–4).** A rising minor sixth and a chromatic fall. It is restated in a new colour each time (B.cl/Vc sul G, C.a., Fl 1, Hn/Tbn, chorale, Vln I sul D).
- **P (15–22).** The motto's sixth, then thirds rising to D♭6 at 20, then a stepwise fall to C5. The repeated cell is the dotted crotchet and three quavers.
- **S (46.4–55).** The same rising sixth as the pickup, a tenuto peak in every second bar (C, F, C, F), and the cell D♭.C B♭ D♭F | A♭ G. That cell comes back at 58–59 and 65–66, and as the closing figure, so a listener has heard it six times by 68.
- **Horn call (13–14).** E♭3–C4–B3, the sixth again, left unresolved.

The returns already follow Adler's practice of a single string per statement: sul D then sul A at 55–59 (Ex. III-6, III-7), sul G at 98–100 (Ex. III-5), and sul D for the last motto at 177–182. The climactic return 158–161 rises G5→E6, then sequences the sixth up to C6→A♭6 on the E string at fff. That is Adler's E-string brilliance (PDF 75, Ex. III-8), and every note is doubled at the octave. A listener will leave humming the motto's sixth and fall and the S head. This is the same kind of memorability as Tchaikovsky 5's motto and Andante tune, which use the same means: one interval that is heard in every theme. I found no phrase where a change of notes would sing more without touching something the finale quotes (P head 15–19, S head, 27–32, coda).

### 2. Coda solo cello 171–174: considered sul D, rejected (no change)

Adler calls the cello's D string "the most musically captivating … warm and lyrical", and the A string "the most brilliant and piercing" (PDF 99). The coda's minor S head in the solo cello (E♭4–D4–C4–A♭4–G4 … C5–B4, mp espr.) lies on the A string. A "sul D" mark would give it the darker Tristan colour (Ex. III-59) that suits a valedictory statement.

I rejected it for three reasons:
- On the D string, E♭4 is in seventh position and C5 at 174 needs the thumb, so the whole phrase would sit in the high positions of the weakest-carrying upper strings.
- The cello here is one solo player beside the cor anglais, at a Più lento mp. Adler's warning on carrying power for solo strings (PDF 102 on the solo cello, PDF 105 on the bass) argues for the A string's carry.
- c251 chose the A string for this phrase on the same pages (PDF 72, 99). I cannot answer that reason better than it stands.

It should stay as it is.

### 3. Violins' re-entry at 156–157: considered sul A, rejected (no change)

At 156 the violins enter mf with the S head's rhythm a step lower (pickup F5 → D6, then D♭6–C6–B♭5–A♭5–G5) and crescendo into the ff climax at 158. By PDF 75 (Ex. III-7), soft lyrical high notes are better on the A string. But this entry is mf with a crescendo into ff, not soft, and it leads into the E-string climax at 158–160. The E string's carrying power (PDF 75, Ex. III-8) is what this bar needs. A sul A from 155.4 would also force D6 into seventh position just before the leap to E6. No change.

### 4. Double-bass doublings and string leaps: checked, no change

Adler's Beethoven 4/iv example (PDF 105, Ex. III-68/69) warns against fast cello–bass doublings, and his skip examples (PDF 82–83) warn against wide fast skips for a section. The full.midi scan (all string leaps of a tenth or more, rows 12 and 22 above) shows that the score avoids both. The basses never play semiquavers. The wide leaps fall at phrase joins or in cello arpeggio returns. The two leaps inside the S climax (158, 160) are slow, doubled and protected (c341).
