# Critic 584: orchestration (series 4)

Version reviewed: v584. Lens 4: orchestration, with the three A♭ climaxes. Reading: Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, ch. 5 "Scoring for Strings" (adler1 PDF 125–167), fourth pass.

Method. I read every text page from PDF 125 to 167 in full. I rendered every page in the range at 110 dpi and looked at each music example: V-1 to V-6 (Haydn op. 76/3, PDF 126–131), V-7 to V-11 (Brahms 3/iii and the four rescorings, 132–134), V-12 and V-13 (Mozart, 136–137), V-14 (Leonore 3, 137), V-15 (Beethoven PC 4/ii, 138), V-16 (Haydn 103, 139), V-17 (Hindemith, 140), V-18 and V-19 (Schumann 2/iii, Shostakovich 6/iii, 141), V-20 (Beethoven 7/ii, 142–143), V-21 to V-24 (Vivaldi, Bach, Weber, Bartók, 144–149), V-25 to V-28 (150–152), V-29 (Tchaikovsky Serenade, 153), V-30 (Grieg, 155), V-31 (Penderecki, 156–157), V-32 to V-38 (accompaniment formulas, 158–162) and V-39 to V-43 (piano to strings, 163–166). I looked at all 17 music pages of score.pdf at 75 dpi, read the string parts of recTwo.ily, and dumped full.midi bar by bar with a script of my own (/tmp/critic-584/scripts/dump.py).

Critics 294, 384 and 494 made the first three passes over this chapter, and 227, 327, 427 and 527 used its second half for texture. Their scans (unisons between strings, spacing, first-inversion doubling, Vln I register in loud chords, pickups) are not repeated here. I ran two new checks instead:
- a map of the double bass against the cellos in every half-beat of the movement (/tmp/critic-584/scripts/cbvc.py): rest, octave below, two octaves below, unison, or independent (Adler PDF 130–131, 151);
- an inventory of string techniques in the section files (pizzicato, tremolo, sul tasto, single-string playing, mutes, harmonics) against Adler's listening list (PDF 167).

I also asked, at each climax and each change of texture, whether a master would have scored the strings differently.

## Findings

No finding meets the bar for a change. I applied nothing. The findings below record what I checked, the candidates I considered, and why each stays. They are ranked by how close each came to a change.

### 1. The three A♭ climaxes in the strings (keep)

I dumped the string notes at each climax.
- **82, ff.** The P head is in three string octaves: Vln I G5–E♭6, Vln II G4–E♭5, violas G3–E♭4. The cellos hold A♭2 and the basses repeat A♭1 in quavers. The flute doubles the top, and the oboe, Cl 1 and Tpt 1 double the middle.
- **122, fff.** All strings play tremolo: Vln I A♭6, Vln II D5/A♭5, violas B3/D4, cellos A♭3. Only the basses hold G1 without tremolo.
- **160, fff.** The tune is in Vln I A♭6, Vln II A♭5 and the cellos A♭4. The violas hammer C4/E♭4 in quavers, and the basses alone hold A♭1.

Each layout uses a different device from the reading:
- 82 is the 8' + 4' octave texture (PDF 155).
- 122 is the full-string fff tremolo after the Hindemith-type unison at 117–121 (PDF 139–140).
- 160 is Adler's sixth way to strengthen a melody, "distributed over four or five octaves and played by all five strings" (PDF 152), with Vln II doubling at the octave instead of adding harmony, as Grieg does at the climax (PDF 155).

The strings grow in weight from one climax to the next. The one departure is Haydn's rule to keep the extreme octave for the last build-up (PDF 129): Vln I reaches A♭6 at 31, 72, 122 and 160. The finale quotes bar 31, and earlier critics protected the climax order. The A♭ goal also rules out a higher note at 160, because B♭6 or C7 would not belong to the ♭VI chord, and C7 is reserved for the finale. Bar 160 gets its extra weight from the cellos joining the tune two octaves below and from the largamente. Keep.

### 2. Double bass against the cellos, whole movement (new scan; keep)

The basses play in 172 of the 182 bars.
- They rest at 85–89, where the fugato subject is heard alone (PDF 145), and at 171–174, where the solo cello and the cor anglais play their duet.
- They play pizzicato under the three lightest statements of S: 47–55, 103–109 and 147–155.
- They play alone at 5.
- They return arco for every pedal and cadence: 56, 110, 117, 156 and 175.

This is Haydn's plan as Adler describes it (PDF 130–131). In most other bars the basses play their own line, usually an octave or two below the cellos, which is the 19th-century practice Adler mentions on the same page.

The scan found one long stretch with the basses at the cellos' pitch: 59–68, under the violins' S, on E♭2, G♭2, F♭2, E♭2, F2 and D♭2. I checked whether this was a slip. Adler's standard place for the bass is an octave below the cellos (PDF 151, V-26). Here that octave is out of reach, because E♭1 lies below the bass's E1. Adler also lists cellos with the basses at pitch as "very intense" (PDF 151, item 5), and that suits the crescendo to the peak at 64.

I considered F1 for the basses on 64.1 to give the S peak a 16' bass. I rejected it: D2 follows on 64.3, so the bass would leap back up a sixth, and the recapitulation at 164 stays in unison for the same reason (c583 has just set the G1 drop at 165.3). Keep.

### 3. String techniques (keep)

The section files use tremolo, sul tasto (Vln II at 147), sul G, sul D and sul A, cello pizzicato at 62–63, and bass pizzicato. They use no mutes (decided at c76), no upper-string pizzicato and no harmonics. Adler's list for further study names pizzicato in the foreground and the background (PDF 167). I asked whether a master would add upper-string pizzicato somewhere for colour. I looked at three places where it might fit: 84 (sub. p after the first climax), 13–14 (under the horn call) and 145–146 (before the recapitulation of S).

Each of these places already has a colour of its own that other critics protected: the held cello and bass G at 84, the strings' p > pp under the solo horn at 13–14, and the Ob 1 seed over held strings at 145–146. A pizzicato used only once in a movement this short would be decoration, not a colour the form uses again (PDF 166: "orchestration must serve the structure"). Keep.

### 4. 147–155: the Vln II halo and Brahms's register rule (keep)

Adler's Brahms analysis keeps the background out of the register the violins will need for the restatement (PDF 135). The Vln II halo at 147–155 lies at E5–C6, and Vln I takes the tune at 156–161 on D6, C6 and B♭5, so the halo is in the register that comes next. The rule has two parts, though: register and attention. The halo is pp, divided, sul tasto and tremolo, a different colour from Vln I's cantabile ordinario at 156. Vln I itself rests from 147 to 155 (c107), so the bright tone of the first violins is still new at 156. Critics 194, 257, 384 and 402 have cleared every unison between the halo and the tune. I have no answer to c107's reason. Keep.

### 5. 97–102: Vln I's register in the ff S (keep)

Vln I starts the B-minor S on D5, in Adler's weak register (PDF 133). Ob 1 plays it in unison, Fl 1 an octave higher, and Vln II sul G, the cellos and the cor anglais play an octave lower. This combines Adler's ways 2, 3c and 4 (PDF 152). The weak start is covered, and the line climbs to D6 at 102. This passage is protected (Fl/Ob with Vln I at 97–102; the octave layout). Keep.

### 6. 111–116: the wedge and Beethoven's octave crescendo (keep)

In the strings, the wedge's rising line A♭5–B♭5–C6–C♯6–D6 is in Vln I alone. Fl 1·2, Ob 1 and Tpt 1 join it at 115, in two octaves. Beethoven builds by adding octaves (PDF 137, V-14). I considered moving Vln II onto the line an octave below from 115. I rejected it, for three reasons:
- That octave is already played by Ob 1 and Tpt 1.
- Vln II's tremolo dyads (F4/B♭4, F4/A♭4, F♯4/A♭4) are the strings' share of the chromatic inner voices. These are protected (c382, lament wedge).
- The strings add their octave at 117, where Vln II takes B3 under Vln I's B4 in the ff motto. That gives the Hindemith unison into the tutti (PDF 139–140) its full effect.

Keep.

### 7. The ending: Fauré's sombre background, then Haydn's pedal (keep)

At 171–174 the only background is violas pp and Bsn 2, with no cello section and no basses. This is a darker version of Fauré's lower-strings background (PDF 162), and it leaves the cello colour to the soloist. Tchaikovsky ends with the bass dropping out at the tonic, its last note bowed (PDF 154). The coda takes Haydn's other ending instead, a held cello and bass pedal for resolution (PDF 129), with C held from 179 to 182. Both are in the reading. The coda and its last chord are quoted by movement II. Keep.
