# Critic 286 — Playability, instrument by instrument (v286)

Assigned reading: Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, Appendix A (adler2 PDF 302–312): 1. Quick reference to ranges (professional and nonprofessional), PDF 302–307; 2. Names of instruments in four languages, PDF 308–311; 3. Frequently used orchestral terms in four languages, PDF 312. All eleven text pages were read in full. The range charts (PDF 302–307) were rendered at 110–300 dpi and read as images, because the OCR of the notation is unusable. PDF 302 and 307 are printed sideways. Pitches read from the charts are given in scientific pitch (C4 = middle C). Where a ledger-line count was hard to read at the extreme of a chart, I give the value as "about".

Method: per-player pitch extremes and the bars where they occur were taken from `full.midi` (one staff per player), and unbroken playing spans in seconds from the same file with the `\mt` tempo map. Then I checked the section files, the timpani part, the string double stops, the harp part and the built parts (`build-parts.sh` in the scratch copy, with `PARTS_AUDIT=1`).

## Findings

None of the checks below needs a change to the score or the parts. I did not apply anything in the scratch copy. This is a "no change" cycle for the playability lens, as c263 and c283 were for their lenses. In ranked order, these are the places closest to a limit, with the reason each one stays.

1. **Bass clarinet low C, 92–93 and 105–106** (sounding C2, B♭1, B1; written D3, C3, C♯3). Adler's chart puts written D3 and C3 in parentheses (adler2 PDF 304), and his text says writing to low C "is still risky, for not all symphony orchestras have instruments with such extensions" (adler1 PDF 216). This is the only place in the score where the reading's own caution applies. It is protected (c146, c256), and the stated reason was that low-C bass clarinets are now standard in professional orchestras. I do not answer that reason, so I do not reopen it. The score already gives the warning in all three places Adler's caution requires: the part title "Bass clarinet in B♭ (with low C)", the staff name "(low C)", and the "(low C instr.)" label at 92. Bsn 2 doubles the same notes, so nothing is lost if the player has to take the octave. Keep.

2. **Ob 1 B3 at 117 (ff, an eighth pickup to A♭4).** This is one semitone above the oboe's professional bottom, B♭3 (adler2 PDF 304), and below the amateur limit (about C4). At ff the oboe's lowest notes speak easily; the problem is soft low notes. This note is the only oboe note below C4 and it is loud and short. Keep.

3. **Hn 1 E5 sounding at 106 (written B5).** This is one semitone below the chart's professional top, written C6 (adler2 PDF 305). It is mf, slurred in a stepwise line, and the part prints an "ossia" an octave lower (dev.ily, c185). Keep.

4. **Violin I and flutes at A♭6 (31, 72, 122, 160; Fl 1 also at 140).** This is well inside the violin top of about E7 and the flute top of C7 (adler2 PDF 302–303). The note always comes at f–fff, and in the violins under an 8va line in the score (c245). Keep.

5. **Cl 1 C6 sounding at 158 and 160 (written D6).** This is the first altissimo note, far below the chart's written C7 (adler2 PDF 304). It comes at fff with the oboes and is protected (c229, c241). Keep.

6. **Cello F5 at 52.** This is inside the professional top of about A5 (adler2 PDF 302). It is the peak of the protected horn-plus-cello second theme (47–55), on the A string in the cello's singing register. Keep.

7. **Timpani tuning and retuning.** The four pitches G2, A♭2, C3 and E♭3 lie one per drum within the drum ranges in Adler's chart (adler2 PDF 307). The B♭2 at 40 and the B♮2 at 97 are retunes of the A♭ drum, up a second and a minor third, which are well inside a 28–29" drum. Each retune has 6–13 bars of rest at Allegro tempo, and the "muta ... in ..." wording is Adler's (PDF 312). The stick changes (soft at 1, medium at 15, soft at 176) fall in rests. Keep (protected c276).

8. **String double and triple stops, divisi, parts.** I checked every string double stop (98 chord events, about 60 distinct chords) for string and finger position. The hardest are Vla G3–B♭3 (tTwo: 4th finger on C, 2nd on G in first position), Vc B2–A♭3 (tOne: third position, 1–4 across G and D) and Vc G3–B3 (dev: 4–1 across D and A, the normal cello major-third frame). All are standard. All the ff chords at 33 use open strings (c226). The coda's Vln I double-stop tremolo is marked "div.". In the parts, all 30 build with 0 warnings. The 2-page parts open as a spread. The harp, Hn 3, Hn 4 and timpani parts have covers. The double-bass page turn falls in the 4-bar rest at 171–174. Every part that crosses into movement II ends on an even page (strings 2, Cb 4, Hn 3·4 2, timpani 2, harp 3 with cover, ending on its étouffez). The parts audit lists 33 "entries without a dynamic". I checked each one, and all are the first notes of printed cues (bars 53, 54, 60, 62, 66, 67, 70, 80, 89, 101, 110, 111, 113, 145, 154, 171) or the harp's right hand where the left hand carries the dynamic (162). They are not real entries. Keep.

Breathing and endurance were not part of this reading, but I measured them because the lens names them. The longest unbroken wind spans are Ob 1 121–126 (about 14.6 s, fff to p under allargando–sostenuto, with the slur re-articulated at 125, c198), Hn 3 61–68 (has a breath mark at 64, c156), Tbn 1 175–181 (breath after 179, c156) and Bsn 1 9–13 (breath after 10, c146). None is new or longer than what earlier playability cycles accepted (c256, c266).
