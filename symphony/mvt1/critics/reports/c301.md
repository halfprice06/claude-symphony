## Findings

I found nothing in the melodic material that stands between this score and a master's work under this reading. Every principle in the table is followed in every theme and in every statement of it. I made no change, so I have no fix to rank. Below are the six questions I tested, ranked by how much each could have mattered, with the evidence and the reason each passage should stay.

### 1. The rising sixth has a consistent rhythmic code (kept)

**Checked.** Every theme in the movement opens with, or turns on, the motto's rising sixth. If the sixth fell on the beat differently each time, the listener would hear several related shapes, not one tune.

**Found.** Two placements, used consistently:
- In plain statements the sixth's top note is a long note on the downbeat, after an upbeat. This is the "simplest and most natural" rhythm (A&S PDF 51, Ex. 3-1). It appears in the motto 1–2, the C.a. answer 5–6, the chain 9–10, S at 47, 56, 98 and 147, the flute motto 74, the trombone motto 117–118 and the coda mottos 172, 176 and 178.
- In the urgent forms the top note falls on beat 2 and is held through beat 3, a syncopation (PDF 51, Ex. 3-6). This is the P head (15, 17, 19, 27, 131, 167), the second phrase of S (51), and the climax line (158 G5–E6, 160 C6–A♭6, tenutos since c268).
- The chain at 9–12 moves from the first placement to the second. The third sixth B4–A♭5 starts on beat 4 of 11 and is held into the *f* of 12 (Ex. 3-17a: a tone held so that it takes part of the next tone's time).

So the introduction teaches the listener both placements before the Allegro uses them. This is how a motto becomes something a listener hums. Brahms does the same with F–A♭–F in his third symphony: one interval, a few fixed rhythmic placements. **Keep.**

### 2. The horn call grows into the S head before S begins (kept)

**Checked.** Is the horn call (13–14) a separate three-note idea, or does it feed a theme?

**Found.** It is the S head, heard four times with a changing rhythm:
- 13–14: Hn 1 E♭3–C4–B3. The C is held over the bar line as a suspension and resolves on beat 2 (PDF 55, Ex. 3-16/3-17a).
- 41–42: four horns E♭3–C4–B♭3. The C is a crotchet on the downbeat over E♭, an appoggiatura, and the B♭ is long (PDF 54–55, Ex. 3-15c).
- 43–44: Hn 1 E♭4–C5, with the C tied into the V4/2 of A♭ and resolving to B♭4 on beat 3, a suspension again.
- 46.4–47: Hn 1 and cellos E♭3–C4 (dotted minim)–B♭3. This is the theme itself, with the long note now on the downbeat (PDF 51).

The listener has heard the head three times before it becomes a theme. This preparation is why S is likely to stay with a listener. **Keep.** It is protected by c221 (13–14), c245 (head) and c271 (S head prepared by 13–14 and 41–44).

### 3. The P head has no note on the downbeats of 15, 17 and 19 (kept)

**Checked.** A&S PDF 51–52 warns that a steady pattern against the metre can sound like a new metre (Brahms, Ex. 3-7). The P head starts `r8 8 4.` in three of its first five bars. The long E♭5, F5 and A♭5 fall on beat 2, and the downbeat is a rest in the tune.

**Found.** The metre does not shift.
- The tune's even bars put their weight on beat 1: tenuto C5 at 16, B4 at 18.
- The basses play minims on beats 1 and 3 (Vc/Cb `c2 d2 | es2 d2 | …`).
- Vln II and violas tie their notes from 2-and, so the beat-2 accent is answered and not doubled.

The pattern is like Ex. 3-6 (Beethoven, Cello Sonata op. 69/ii), where the syncopations sit inside a clear 3/4 marked by the bass. The head's urgency comes from this displacement, and the finale quotes the rhythm (themes-mvt1.md, `r8 8 4. 8 8 8 | 4 8 8 4. 8`). **Keep.**

### 4. The peak of the S restatement, D♭6 at 63, is short and on a weak beat (kept; c221 stands)

**Checked.** By PDF 50 a high note carries a built-in accent, and by PDF 53 bar 64 (the *f*) is the strong first bar of its group. The highest note of the violins' restatement, D♭6, is a crotchet on beat 2 of 63. The *f* arrives at 64 on the lower A♭5, so the pitch peak and the dynamic peak are a bar apart.

**Found.** The split is right, for three reasons:
- The long-note weight is already on the tenuto C6 at 62 (c268), which is held from beat 2 across the bar line, a syncopation (PDF 51).
- The D♭6 is the échappée (escape tone) that c221 kept with the reason "PDF 334–335".
- A long or downbeat D♭6 here would repeat the peak of P exactly: D♭6, syncopated and *sf*, at 20. The two themes would then share their climax pitch and its rhythm. As written, P's D♭6 is the long, struck one and S's D♭6 passes, so the two themes stay distinct.

**Keep** (protected 63–64, c221, c226).

### 5. Phrase goals and hypermeter (kept)

**Checked.** The goal of each phrase against PDF 53–54.
- P reaches i at 22.3, at the end of eight bars, as in Ex. 3-13.
- S's antecedent reaches V at 50, the fourth bar. The consequent reaches I at 55 as a one-bar suffix, protected since c163.
- The restatement 56–68 is stretched to 13 bars (4+2+2+2+2+1) and ends on the deceptive cadence at 68. The S paragraph therefore ends with an open question that the codetta answers at 69 (f, A♭).
- After the climax, 162–166 is five bars to the soft C at 166, an asymmetrical group of the kind the reading names (Ex. 3-14).

None of these groups leaves a theme's goal on a weak bar without a reason. **Keep.**

### 6. The coda restores the motto's first rhythm (kept)

**Checked.** Does the last melodic idea of the movement have the clearest rhythm, so that it stays with the listener into movement II?

**Found.** In 171–182 the motto sounds three times: C.a. from 171.4, trombones from 175, Vln I from 177.4. Each time the sixth arrives on a downbeat, the D♭ falls on beat 3 and the C on the next downbeat. This is the rhythm of 1–4, the simplest placement (PDF 51). Cl 1's horn call at 175.4–177 is the suspension form of 13–14 (PDF 55). The urgent beat-2 sixth of the climaxes is not used again. **Keep** (coda 171–182 protected; the finale's chorale is built on it).
