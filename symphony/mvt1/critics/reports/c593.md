## Findings

No finding meets the bar for a change. Nothing is applied in the scratch copy. The findings are ranked by how much each would matter if it were wrong.

### 1. The large-scale bass is I–III–(VI)–IV–V–I. The F minor wedge is the "passing IV" of Ex. 15-2, written large. Keep.

A&S say III "leads from an opening tonic to a dominant" and that "in moving from III to V, composers most often use a passing tone in the bass supporting IV or II6" (PDF 231). The examples in 15-2b and 15-2e use IV.

This movement's tonal pillars are:
- C (15)
- E♭ (36–43, III, confirmed at 41)
- A♭ (47–76, VI, a falling fifth from III as in the III–VI link of Ex. 15-9)
- the development
- F minor at 111 (iv), which starts the chromatic descent F–E–E♭–D–D♭–C (111–116)
- G (117–130, V)
- C (131)

Earlier passes did not record this. The development's own key changes are elaborations between VI and IV. The arrival on V is made by the bass step F→G expanded through the lament wedge, which is the bass of Ex. 15-2b/e enlarged.

A&S say the top voice in III–IV–V "usually descends by step", and add that "other options exist" (PDF 231). Here the soprano rises A♭5–B♭5–C6–C6–D♭6–D6 (111–116) against the falling bass. This is a wedge, so the outer voices expand into the V. That is the right option for a retransition, where the music has to gather force rather than relax into V. The c503 point also still stands: A&S's I–III–V in minor puts III and VI in the departure and V in the return, and the score follows that order. The wedge is protected (c333, c389, c452). Keep.

### 2. The two-bar climb 156–157: the reading supports the sudden approach. Keep. A no-bar-count alternative was considered and rejected.

Form critics since c443 have named 156–157 as the movement's one weakness. The C-major S ends p at 155 and reaches ff at 158 in 4.1 s. Lengthening the climb is blocked: bars 160–182 are quoted by bar number in movements II and IV, and proportions are protected (c013, c043, c243, c433). I asked whether the climb could be made stronger without adding bars, and whether Unit 15 supports the present design.

**What the score does.**
- At 155.1 the S statement closes on I (PAC).
- At 155.3 the bass stops (Cb rest, Bsn 2 rest). Only D3/D4 sound, in Vc, Hn 1 and violas, for two beats.
- At 155.4 the violins enter mf with the pickup F5.
- At 156 the full ♭VII arrives: harp, horns p<, Cl 1 counter.
- Then iv (157), the cadential 6/4 ff (158), V7 (159) and ♭VI fff (160).

**What the reading says.** A&S describe exactly this shape of approach. In Ex. 15-6 Brahms reaches V7 "by almost imperceptible degrees", and then "the sforzando and the rush of quick notes bring the V7 forcefully to our attention" (PDF 234). In Ex. 15-16 (image, PDF 242), Handel's p strings at m. 53 give way to a tutti f on the next harmonic link at m. 54.

The 155.3 caesura is the score's version of "imperceptible": the cadence dissolves into a bare octave D, the note that becomes the third of ♭VII. The violins' rising sixth F5–D6 on ♭VII, the four horns' entry and the harp then mark the turn, and the ff arrives two bars later. PDF 241 also says natural VII moving to V7 is "unexpected and, in retrospect, logical", with "a particularly expressive" inflection of 7̂. The score delivers that inflection in one line: Cl 1 has B♭5 at 156.4.5, A♭5 at 157 and B♮5 at 159.1, where it doubles Ob 1. The 4̂ that prepares the 7th of V7 (PDF 242) is Vln I's F6 at 156.1, and it returns as the 7th at 159.3.

**Alternative considered.** Start the crescendo at 155.1 in the tune (Vla, Vc and Hn 1 E4–D4 p<), and hold the bass C through beats 3–4 so there is no caesura. I rejected it for three reasons:
- It blurs the PAC at 155, which ends S as at 55, and the next phrase would start before this one has closed.
- It removes the one-beat break that makes the ♭VII entry an event. Unit 15's two models of a forceful approach to the dominant both depend on that kind of contrast (Ex. 15-6, 15-16).
- A held C under 155.3–4 is a common tone to nothing in the ♭VII chord. Moving the bass to B♭ early would anticipate 156.

I did not apply it. The climb stays short in seconds, but its harmonic route is the one the reading recommends (VII → iv → 6/4 → V7, PDF 241–242). The earlier tests at 156–157 also stay rejected: the iv6 bass at 157 (c553: octaves with Cl 1, and the ♭6 bass spent before 160) and the timpani roll at 157 (c533). Keep.

### 3. Scans re-run on v593: no new diminished triad and no new soprano/bass cross-relation. Keep.

**Diminished triads.** dim.py scanned every half-beat (harp and timpani excluded) for a three-note diminished triad over its root. The only hit is still 16.4: D3–F–A♭ for one quaver, while the low G rests between quavers. It is the case of Ex. 15-19 (PDF 244): it "grows out of the strong V at the beginning of the bar" and is heard as V6/5. 23–24 and 135–136 remain V6/5 with Hn 4 on G3 (c393).

**Cross-relations.** xrel.py lists every semitone pair split between the outer voices on successive half-beats. Real chromatic inflections of one letter name split between soprano and bass occur only at:
- 79→80 (E→E♭)
- 96→97 (B♭→B)
- 102→103 (F♯→F)
- 108→109 (B→B♭)

c393 and c503 dealt with all four as key changes between development stations, and none has changed. Three other hits are not cross-relations:
- 115→116: the soprano itself goes D♭6→D6, so the step stays in one voice (PDF 236).
- 109.4: the B♮2 is the bass clarinet's own chromatic figure C3–B2–B♭2–C3.
- 35.4: the B4 is C♭5 passing in the B♭-minor-7 sonority.

The changes since v503 (125–126 horn call over 6/4–V, Cb G1 at 165.3, Vln II A♭4–F4 at 177) add no hits. Keep.

### 4. The recapitulation without a III stopover is right. Keep.

c503 argued this from PDF 232: III is a stopover when the music leaves the tonic, and the recapitulation does not leave it. The classical minor-key model agrees. In Mozart's G-minor Symphony K. 183/i (reference-corpus/mutopia/MozartWA_KV183), the second group stands in III (B♭) in the exposition and in the tonic minor in the recapitulation, and no III area is interposed before it.

This movement does the same with a different mode for S: the second group is in VI in the exposition and in I (major) in the recapitulation. The approach replaces III with iv–♭II–iiø–V (139–142), the second route the unit names from the tonic area to V (Ex. 15-2e/f), with the top line A♭6–G–F–D–C–B. After the PAC at 143, three bars of V (144–146) hold the dominant before C major. PDF 234 says this is enough ("the force of dominant harmony is so great that it does not always require special emphasis"). The E♮5 that touches 146.1 announces the mode. Keep.

### 5. Coda 175–178: E♭ in the bass carries i6, not III. Keep.

PDF 239: III can replace I6 over the same bass when "the stepwise descent 8–7–6" is wanted, but "in most situations ... I6 is preferable", because it "can help to define the tonality".

At 176 the chorale's bass is E♭ (the motto's E♭), and the chord is E♭–G–C. That is i6, with no B♭. Cl 1 holds C5 over it into 177, where over the D bass it is the seventh of iiø7 and falls to B4 on beat 3 (vii°6/5). That gives 8–7 in an inner line without making the chord III.

In a coda whose job is to fix C as tonic before the attacca, I6 is the correct choice by the reading's own rule. The move to i at 178 runs through an inverted dominant (vii°6/5), which is the contrapuntal connection of PDF 235–236 (Ex. 15-7, 15-8). Coda harmony is protected (c400, c410). Keep.

### 6. Where the movement is smaller than a master's: unchanged, and outside what this unit can fix.

The measurable gaps remain:
- The recapitulation is 0.61 of the exposition (Beethoven 9/i: 0.79).
- The coda is 13.7 % of the total (Beethoven 9/i: 22 %).
- The climb at 156–157 is short.

Unit 15 does not argue for lengthening any of them. It puts the expansion of III in the departure and lets V act without extra length (PDF 232, 234). Each lengthening would also shift bar numbers quoted by movements II and IV, or use material reserved for the finale ("Do not reuse" in coherence/mvt1.md). No change.
