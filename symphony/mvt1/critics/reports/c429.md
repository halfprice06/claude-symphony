## Comparison with Beethoven 4/i

What Beethoven's introduction does, and how this score compares:

1. **Proportion.** Beethoven's Adagio is 38 bars, about a fifth of the movement's length. This introduction is 14 bars at crotchet = 50, about 67 s of 430 s, or 16%. Both are a real slow preface, not a few bars of curtain-raiser. Not a gap.
2. **Economy of the opening sound.** Beethoven opens with one pp unison in pizzicato strings and a held wind chord, and lets the strings creep under it. Bars 1–4 here do the same with fewer means: a bare unison motto in B.cl and Vc, an octave below in the Cb, over a ppp timpani roll. Rimsky PDF 56 and 68 support this layout (bass phrase only with cellos or same-family winds). A conductor would accept it.
3. **Harmonic suspense.** Beethoven's introduction delays its tonic and drifts through remote, enharmonically spelled regions before it reaches the dominant. This introduction is tonally clear: i – ♭VI over G at 6, N6 at 7, I at 8, then i – iv – iiø7 – Ger6 – V at 9–13. That is the main difference in kind. It is a motto introduction in the Tchaikovsky and Brahms manner, not a Beethoven-style search. The intro length and 1–9 are protected (c379, c389, c409, c410: "soft introduction", "intro 1–9", "1–4 motto"). To match Beethoven's range, the intro would need more bars and a wider harmonic path, and the finale quotes bar 12 as its first chord. I do not reopen it.
4. **The join to the Allegro.** Beethoven's join is a dominant build that breaks into the Allegro at ff. Here the f arrives on the German sixth at 12, then the horn call fades over V at 13–14, a silence follows, and the strings start P alone. That reverses Beethoven's plan on purpose, and it is protected ("silence at 14", "strings-only first theme 15–22"). The contrast with Beethoven is a choice, not a weakness: Brahms 1/i and Tchaikovsky 5/i also let an introduction subside before the Allegro.

Would a conductor believe a master wrote it? The scoring of the introduction, judged against the 23 principles above, is idiomatic in every bar. The distance from Beethoven 4/i is the introduction's narrow harmonic span, and that is a protected design decision, not a flaw I can fix without breaking the cross-movement quotations.

## Findings

**F1. v419 (v421) → v429: nothing got worse in the music. No change.**
Changes since v421 are: Cl 1 and Vln II E♭4 at 24.3–25.1 and 136.3–137.1 (c422); upper viola A♭4 at 68 (c422); Fl a2 on the head at 117–121 with Ob 1 on the held F5–G5 line and Ob 2 on the lower octave (c424); Bsn 2 A2 and Bsn 1 E3 at 162 (c424); the horn clef plan (c425); the Cb up-bow at 5 (c426); and the MIDI volume function (c428). I checked each against the diff and the reading. The 162 change gives the natural A1–A2–E3 spacing (Rimsky PDF 85, 96). The 117–121 change puts the stationary line in a colour that carries under ff brass (Rimsky PDF 51, 91). The 24/136 and 68 changes are voice-leading corrections. I built a scratch copy of v429 as it stands: 0 warnings, 18 PDF pages, and only "bar 96: octaves F5->Bb2 over F2->Bb1" in analysis.txt. The front-page stamp has gone back from "v421, 27 October 2026" to "draft v429". kit/promote.sh does this on every promotion, so it is the expected working stamp, not a regression. The next release check should restamp it.

**F2. Bars 117–119: the flute pickups dip under Ob 1's held line. Keep.**
A per-quaver scan of full.midi finds Fl 1·2 below Ob 1 only at 117–119: the quaver pickups B4, D5 and E♭5 of the head pass under Ob 1's F5/G5. Rimsky PDF 65 calls flutes below oboes "far-fetched" when the instruments are combined on one line. Here the parts are a moving line crossing a held one, which ch. III treats as ordinary crossing (PDF 106–114). The pickups are also doubled by Vln I at the same pitch. In v421 the same crossing happened between Fl 1 and Fl 2, so c424 did not make it worse. Moving Ob 1 up an octave (F6/G6) would put the oboe at the top of its range at ff; moving it down (F4/G4) would bury it in the trombones' motto. Neither is better, so I recommend no change. Not applied.

**F3. Bar 47: the S head as a Hn 1 + Vc unison. Keep, because it is protected and the reading supports it.**
Rimsky PDF 66–67 says unison doubling "restricts the variety of colour and expression" and that intimate phrases belong to single timbres. That would argue for a solo horn at 47. But Rimsky's own Ex. 15 and 17 (PDF 189–193) give his most cantabile tune to mixed unisons (Vla+Vc+C.a., then Vln+Vc+C.a.), and he says the cor anglais is "absorbed" while the cellos lead. Horn + cello at 47 is the same kind of pairing. The movement also already has its single-timbre solos at 5–8 (C.a.), 13 (Hn 1) and 171–174 (solo cello). Protected since c040s ("horn + cello 47–55"). No change.

**F4. Bars 9–13: the introduction's rising-sixth line in Vln I alone. Keep.**
Rimsky PDF 59 says high violin melodies become isolated unless doubled in octaves. The line here peaks at A♭5, below the register that rule addresses (Note I, PDF 62; the example cited there, Ex. 24, is at the top of the violin range). Fl 1 joins in unison from 11.3 for the f, the arrangement of Ex. 3 (PDF 177: Vln I doubling the woodwind's upper register). Beethoven also keeps his introduction's line in plain string colour until the join. No change.

I found nothing in the assigned reading that this score breaks in a way worth changing. I did not edit the scratch copy, so there is no vl.txt diff to report.
