**Verdict.** At the large scale the succession has enough contrast. Thick and thin textures alternate every 4–8 bars, and there are four real contrapuntal stretches (33–40, 85–96, 109–113, 171–177).

Two types are overused:
- **The ff tutti in octaves over a repeated-eighth bass:** five times (27, 69, 123, 131, 163), plus 154 without the eighths.
- **The second group's accompaniment:** a one-bar rising eighth-note arpeggio in Vc and harp, plus viola off-beats, runs for about 35 bars (47–68 and 148–162).

148–153 and 158–162 are still the exposition's texture transposed; only the 148–151 viola fills are new. I leave the tutti type alone, because 163–166, 27–32 and 69–72 are protected, and I target the second group below.

## Findings

### 1. Recap counter 148–151 repeats the module and has three local faults
`recTwoCounter` bars 1–4 are the exposition module transposed: `g'2 r8 g'( a' b') | c''2) a'2 | a'2 r8 …`.

The faults:
- **148 and 150:** its three-eighth pickup falls on beats 3½–4½, in the same rhythm as the new viola fills (`g'8 f'( e' c')`), so the two lines compete.
- **149:** it lands in unison on the tune's c''.
- **151:** `b'2` doubles the tune's leading tone b'' at the octave and moves with Vln II in parallel octaves into 152 (vl2: Hn2 B4→D4 against Vln II B5→D5).

As critic 21 noted, the module is serviceable. A recapitulation counter can be new, though. Tchaikovsky 6/i 306–310 (cited by critic 17) and Dvořák 9/i 171ff both re-texture the returning theme.

Fix. Give the recap its own counter: a falling chromatic line that expands the motto's D–D♭–C tail. It runs C–B–B♭–A, then C–B♭–A–G–F, under the tune's rising sixths, the way the Brahms 2/ii bassoon falls against the rising cello theme. The tied c'' is the prepared seventh of Dm7; f' is G7's seventh, held into 152.
- `recTwoCounter` bars 1–4 (section 10–13 = 148–151; Cl 1 plays it through `recTwoClOne`): `r4 c''4(\mp b'4 bes'4 | a'2) c''2~( | c''4 bes'4 a'2 | g'2 f'2) |`
- `recTwoHnTwo` section bars 10–13 (the doubling, written out): `r4 c''4(\mp^\markup \italic "espr." b'4 bes'4 | a'2) c''2~( | c''4 bes'4 a'2 | g'2 f'2) |`

The rest of the counter (152–162) is unchanged. One caveat: the b' at 148 beat 3 sounds against Bsn 2 C3 (a passing major 7th over the tonic, one beat). I accept it as a chromatic passing note. Without it, the counter would need `c''4 b'8 bes'8`.

### 2. The ♭VI-climax counter cannot be heard (154–157)
At fff, Ob 2 + Cl 1 carry the counter (including cycle 12's E♭–C resolution at 156) against three octaves of tune in strings, Hn 1·2 and Tpt 1. Rimsky-Korsakov says exactly such a counter "will hardly be heard". Tchaikovsky 6/i 134–141 gives the counter-figure to the brass.

Meanwhile Hn 3 plays `c' b c' b`, the same pitches as Tpt 3 and Tbn 1 (a triple doubling).

Fix: `recTwoHnThree` section bars 16–19 (154–157):
```
g'2\ff c''2 | b'2 f'2 | es'4\fff c''4 r8 c''8( bes'8 aes'8 | aes'2) f'2 |
```
- Doublings after the change: Ob 2, Cl 1 and Hn 3 on the counter; Tpt 3 and Tbn 1 keep c'/b.
- Hn 4's dynamics (ff at 154, fff at 156) are equal on the shared staff.
- This does not flip cycle 14: Hn 1·2 stay on the tune.

### 3. The same cello arpeggio in every bar (149–150)
`recTwoArp` gives the cello the rising shape `X8( … Y2)` in every bar from 148 to 153 and again at 158–162, doubled by the harp, just as at 56–68. Dvořák 7/i 86–89 changes the cello figure's direction every bar under a constant accompaniment.

Fix: `recTwoArp` bars 2–3 (149–150):
```
a'8( f' c' a f2) | a'8( f' d' a f2) |
```
- The harp keeps its rising form, so the two figures move in contrary motion.
- My first try started 149 on f'. It made parallel fifths with the tune (g'/d'' → f'/c''), so I start from a'.
- Ending on f (not c or d) avoids new octaves with Bsn 2 and Cb.

## Not changed

- The five ff "tutti over bass eighths" passages are protected or structural. I note the overuse for the later movements.

None of these findings undoes an earlier fix. Finding 1 replaces cycle 7/14's recap counter notes at 148–151. Finding 2 adds to cycle 14's arrangement and does not reverse it.
