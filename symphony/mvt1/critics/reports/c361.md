## Findings (ranked)

None of the findings calls for a score change. They are ranked by how much they would matter if the verdict were wrong.

### 1. The themes: memorable, and no new version is warranted (no change)

Sebesky's question 6 (PDF 220) asks whether the tune is weak in its melody and what to do about it. I asked it of each theme, with the lens's question: would a listener hum it afterwards?

- **Motto** (G–E♭, then D–C–D♭–C). Yes. It is short, and its two features are clear: a rising minor sixth, then a chromatic fall with a turn back up. It returns in nine colours (1, 5, 73, 117, 127, 156, 171, 175, 177). Tchaikovsky's Fifth makes its motto memorable by the same means: one shape in changing colours.
- **P.** Yes, the head (15–16). The pickup quaver, the accented dotted crotchet on the sixth and the falling chromatic quavers come back at 17, 27, 29, 69, 77–83, 103, 131, 167. The continuation 19–22 rises to one peak (D♭6 sf at 20) and falls by step. That is a complete sentence with one climax, as in Brahms 1/i's Allegro theme.
- **S.** The head (46.4–48) yes. It keeps the motto's rhythm (dotted minim tied to a quaver) and its rising sixth, and it is planted at 13, 41 and 43 before it is heard. The continuation 52.4–55 (F5 down to the cadence on C4) is a long-line descent rather than a second hook, in the manner of Brahms and Elgar, not Tchaikovsky's repeated two-bar cells. I would not trade it for a more repetitive line. Its real high point comes in the restatement (C6–D♭6 at 62–63, f at 64) and at the climax 158–161 (G5–E6, then C6–A♭6), so the theme grows each time it is heard, which helps memory more than a second hook would.
- **Horn call** (E♭3–C4–B3). This is a signal, not a tune, and it is the S incipit with B♮. It does its job.

Every theme starts from the rising sixth. That makes the movement one family, not a set of look-alikes, because the four differ in rhythm, tempo, mode and colour. Sebesky's "recurrent figure apart from the tune" (PDF 206) supplies the contrast: the hemiola 23–25, the scale runs and the timpani pulse. Rewriting S or P would also break the finale's quotations (I 15–16 at IV 247–248, I 27–32 at IV 199–204, I 146–150 at IV 247–250, v99 155–159 at IV 234–238). The reading gives no reason that outweighs that.

### 2. Background motion against held tune notes (Ex. 280, Ex. 286; no change)

In Ex. 280 the violins move when the soloist holds (bar 1 beats 3–4, bar 3) and sustain when the soloist moves. Ex. 286 bar 13 gives the same rule as a fill "to take up the slack (two beats +)" (PDF 222). I listed every tune note of two beats or more in fourteen statements (15–22, 47–55, 56–68, 69–72, 97–110, 139–142, 147–166, 171–182) and the onsets in all other parts during each one:

- Under the held notes something moves in the gap, as Ex. 280 does. At 47, 51 and 55 the harp arpeggio moves. At 56, 60 and 64 the Cl 1/Hn 2 counter moves on beat 3-and, and the cello arpeggio moves on every quaver. At 98 the viola arpeggio moves. At 156 and 162 the violas and cellos move. At 147 the cor anglais enters at 3½. Under the held E♭5 at 178 the chorale moves.
- Holds with no onset in any other part: 50.3, 72.3, 150.1, 150.3, 155.1, 172.2, 174.1, 174.3, 180.3. Each one is a cadence or a phrase end (half cadences 50/150, codetta arrival 72, recap cadence 155, the duet's cadence 174, the last motto 180). At 172.2 the cor anglais has just moved and holds E♭5 above the cello. Sebesky's own condition (PDF 212: tempo, rhythm and harmony decide when to move) puts a breath at a cadence. Cycle 271 closed 50/150 on the same grounds.

Dvořák 7/i bars 90–91 (corpus PDF DvorakA_O70__DvorakSYMPH7_conductor.pdf, page 24) show the same practice: Vln II and violas run in semiquavers under Vln I's held dotted crotchets.

### 3. Shadowing and fill tones (Ex. 279, Ex. 280; no change)

Ex. 279 fails because the violins move with the soloist in almost constant similar motion (PDF 211). That is wider than parallel 3rds/6ths, which is all c271 measured. I counted, for every part against the tune, the share of coinciding onsets that move in the same direction, leaving out octave doublings. Parts above 60%, or with runs of four or more:

- Bass lines: Vc 15–22 and Cb 59–63. Sampled every half bar, they move with the tune's long notes, but they are bass lines a level below (c278), not echoes.
- Climax support: Vc 158–162, Bsn 1·2 160–162, Cl 1 161–163. These are parts of the tutti that follow the tune's harmony, and at 160–161 they are its lower octaves or tenths.
- Hn 4 104–105 and Vln II 109–110: held-chord parts whose few onsets fall with the tune. These are runs of four onsets, not a running echo.

No counter-melody shadows the tune. Ex. 280's second point is that the background takes "chordal tones not present in either the foreground solo line or the bass line" (PDF 212). I sampled every half beat. The Cl 1/Hn 2 counter at 56–68 is on the tune's pitch class 3% of the time, on the bass's 17% and on other chord tones 80%. Cl 1's counter at 156–166: 4%, 16%, 80%. This is the Ex. 280 model. The cor anglais at 147–155 meets the tune at 153.3, 154.4 and 155, where both lines close on the cadence. That is "touching down briefly" (PDF 212), and the C.a. answers are protected (c147, c337).

### 4. Push into the next phrase (Ex. 273; no change)

In Ex. 273 the cellos move from halves to quarters in bars 3–4 "to give extra push into the next phrase" (PDF 208). In both settings of S the double-bass pizzicato is on beat 1 only, except in the fourth bar of each four-bar unit, where it plucks beats 1 and 3: 50 and 54 (`es,4 r4 es,4 r4`), 150 and 154 (`g,,4 r4 g,,4 r4`, `g,4 r4 g,4 r4`). That is the device in Ex. 273, and it falls on the bars where the tune holds its half notes. In the violins' restatement 56–68 the cello arpeggio already runs in quavers, and the bar before each new unit (58, 62) has the extra figure (58 `bes,8( f des'4.) c'8 ces' bes`).

### 5. A lone moving voice that sets up the next entry (Ex. 281 note C; no change)

Note C of Ex. 281 has one voice move alone while the others sustain, and the line "also sets up the sax section soli two bars later" (PDF 216). Cl 1 at 45–46 (B♭4–A♭4–G4–F4–E♭4, dolce, diminuendo) is the only moving line of the link, and it ends on E♭4 one beat before Hn 1's E♭3 pickup to S. That is Sebesky's set-up, and it gives the entering voice its pitch beforehand (Ex. 285 bar 10, PDF 222). Notes D and G (rests for high or demanding parts) are met at 159.4, where Fl 1·2, Ob, Hn 1·2 and the trumpets breathe before the fff A♭6 at 160.

### 6. The low P at 131–134 against Sebesky's "four trumpets" warning (considered, no change)

Sebesky warns against covering a melody in a "low, weak register" with "four trumpets blaring in unison" (PDF 219). At 131–134 the P head lies in the tenor (G3–F4) in the cellos, Cl 1·2 and Bsn 1·2 (ff) and Tbn 1·2 (f), while Tpt 1·2·3 hammer G4/G4/E♭4 f and Fl/Ob/Vln I hold a ff syncopated counter above. Tpt 3's E♭4 is in the tune's register. I checked whether the warning applies. The trumpets do not play in unison. They play the `c4 r8 c8` pulse of 27–32 in short notes with rests, so the tune's long notes (the dotted crotchets after the pickups at 131 and 133) sound between the strokes. Seven parts carry the tune, five of them ff, against three trumpets at f. Earlier cycles already lowered the accompanying violas, horns, bass trombone and tuba to f here (c264, c308). The layout matches 27–32 and 167–170 on purpose, and the finale quotes 27–32 at pitch (protected: tutti layouts 27–32, 131–134, 167–170). The reading does not answer that protection, and the case it describes (a unison trumpet section over a weak low voice) is not the case here.

### 7. Colour on long notes, and effects held back (Ex. 285–286; no change)

Sebesky puts colour on the word held for six beats ("prayer"), not on the quick one ("carousel") (PDF 223). He holds instruments back so an effect lands (PDF 222). Every colour event in the score falls on a held note or chord: the tam-tam at 122 (fff held tremolo) and 175 (chorale), and the suspended-cymbal stroke at 160 on the held A♭6. None falls on a passing note. The harp is first heard at 47, the tam-tam at 122 and the cymbal at 159, each after long silence. The one Sebesky device the score does not use is quotation (PDF 224, "never more than once"). The motto is the movement's own material, so the rule does not apply.

### 8. Harmonic lifts masked by colour or dynamics (Ex. 284; no change)

Ex. 284 allows a "sudden harmonic lift" with disconnected leaps when a tremolo, a drop in volume or a change of colour covers it (PDF 217–218). The score's abrupt shifts are covered in the same way. At 96→97 (B♭ minor to B minor) the new key comes with the timpani B stroke and a ff restatement. At 102→103 (B minor to D minor) the dynamic falls to f and the texture changes to winds and horn. At 170→171 a fermata rest separates the stop chord from the solo cello.
