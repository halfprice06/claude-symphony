## Findings

### 1. Bar 141, beats 1–2: the timpani roll on A♭2 sits a minor third above the F2 bass (APPLIED)

**Problem.** In the recapitulation's ff Neapolitan (140–142) the timpani roll A♭2 through 140 with a crescendo, over the D♭ bass (D♭1 Cbsn, D♭2 Cb/tuba/Bsn 2). A♭2 over D♭2 is a fifth, and that is good. At 141 the bass moves to F (Cb, tuba, Bsn 2 on F2, Cbsn F1), and the score holds the roll on A♭2 ff for two more beats (`aes,4:32\ff~ aes,4 r2`). That gives a sustained minor third F2–A♭2 (87/104 Hz) between the tuba and the loudest drum stroke of the passage. My scan found it to be the only low third in the movement outside the protected stop chord at 26. Casanelles gives the harmonic series as the model for spacing: octave, fifth and fourth at the bottom, thirds only above them (PDF 259, 274), as Rimsky-Korsakov's "natural harmonic scale" does (rimsky-principles PDF 85: wide intervals in the lower part, closer ones higher up). Casanelles also notes that a sustained, even sound is heard as more present than a decaying one, and his example is a timpani roll against a single hit (PDF 290). Holding the roll makes the low third as present as it can be.

**Fix (sections/recOne.ily, recOneTimp, bar 141):** the crescendo roll ends in one stroke on the arrival.
`R1 | aes,1:32\f\< | aes,4->\ff r4 r2 | g,4-> r4 g,4-> r4 |` (was `aes,4:32\ff~ aes,4 r2`).
The ff accent on 141.1, where the violins and Fl 1 reach A♭, stays. The drum then decays under the F bass instead of sustaining against it, and the G2 strokes at 142 follow as before. Adler asks the composer to mark exactly where a roll ends and how long a note rings (adler2 PDF 80), and the stroke states that. No other part changes. The analogous Neapolitans at 20–21 and 172–173 have no timpani.

### 2. Bars 69–71: Tpt 2 holds A♭4/G4 at f inside the band of the P head's upper octave (no change)

The masking scan flags 69–71. Here the head's upper octave (Vln II, C.a., Cl 1·2, f with accents, G4–F5) passes through Tpt 2's held A♭4 (69–70) and G4 (71). By PDF 287 and 289, a forte trumpet is the densest sound in that band. I keep it, for four reasons. First, the head has four instruments there against one trumpet, and c331 added the cor anglais for this purpose. Second, where the two meet on A♭4 (69.4, 70.1) it is a unison, not a second, so it adds weight rather than covering the line. Third, the trumpets share a staff, so Tpt 2 cannot be marked below Tpt 1's S head (shared-stave rule). Fourth, the A♭/G is the pedal an octave above Hn 3 that carries I–ii7–vii°7/V–V7 into the 72 tutti. Removing it would thin the trumpet register just before the tutti returns.

### 3. Bar 82: the head's lowest octave lies inside the held brass (no change)

At 82 the violas and Bsn 1 play the head at E♭4–D♭4 among Hn 2 and Tpt 3 on E♭4 and Hn 3·4 and Tbn 1 on C4, all at f since c304. PDF 287 says the lowest octave will be partly masked. The head is carried by its two upper octaves (eight instruments ff: Fl 1·2 and Vln I at E♭6; Ob 1, Cl 1·2, Tpt 1 and Vln II at E♭5). The lowest octave adds body in the way of Adler's unison-octave tutti (c344). Moving the held brass out of that band would change the protected 82 layout (c304) for a gain that cannot be heard.

### 4. Flute–violin unisons (no change)

The flutes double the violins at the unison in about 60 bars. By PDF 277–278 the flute is then not heard as a second note, only as added upper partials. The score uses that on purpose: it brightens every A♭6 peak (31, 72, 122, 160). The flute's own colour is kept for the places where it is meant to be heard alone: the A♭-minor motto 73–76 over pp strings (the Fig. 6.1 pattern), the A♭6 above the violins' octave at 140–141, and the fade at 13. This is the right use of the doubling, and no change is needed.

### 5. The three A♭ climaxes against harmonic density (no change)

Counted at their peaks, 82 has 25 sounding parts with 10 brass (held at f, strokes ff), 122 has 29 with 11 brass at fff, and 160 has 27 with 11 brass at ff and fff (Tpt 1, Hn on the tune). By PDF 289, 122's fff brass with tam-tam is at least as dense as 160. The order still holds because the two peaks do different things. At 122 the density is harmonic: a V♭9 whose dissonant partials are loudest (PDF 275), with the tam-tam's dark inharmonic spectrum. At 160 the density is melodic: the S tune in three octaves, each with a string, a woodwind and a brass part, over a ♭VI chord spaced on the harmonic series of A♭1 (A♭2, E♭3, A♭3, C4, E♭4, A♭4, C5), with the brighter suspended cymbal (PDF 277). The protected climax levels (c244, c318, c334) agree with the reading.
