## Findings

No passage of v423 stands, under this reading, between the score and a master's work, so I made no edit. Since v404 the key boundaries, section lengths and cadence types are unchanged. The findings below are the questions this reading still raises, ranked by how much a change at that place would matter; each gives the evidence for keeping the passage.

### 1. The move to VI, 33–47, has the same harmonic plan as Beethoven 9/i's move to VI: keep

The reading builds a key area from the new key's own dominant prepared by V/V with ♯4̂ (PDF 214 §5, 216 §6). Beethoven 9/i, D minor to B♭ major, does this in the MCTL annotation at onsets 141–152 (bars 71.5–77): vii°4/3 of V, V/V, V7/V, V7, then I of B♭ at 77.

The score, C minor to A♭ major, follows the same order one key lower:
- 39–40: V/V of A♭ (B♭7 with D♮ = ♯4̂ in the key of A♭), with the soprano rising to the medial caesura at 41 (PDF 215, Ex. 14-4).
- 41–43: E♭, with D♭ added at 43.
- 44–45: V4/2–I6 of A♭, the Schubert progression of Ex. 14-1 bar 6 (PDF 211, labelled V4/2 and I6 under the example).
- 46–47: ii6/5–V7–I with the S head, after a poco rit., a double bar, a new key signature and a new texture (harp), which is the "punctuation in the music's flow" of PDF 216 §6.

Beethoven goes straight from V7/V to V7 to I. The score inserts the V4/2–I6 and then a second approach (46). That gives the arrival the extra weight a Romantic second group needs. Keep (protected c223, c403).

### 2. Record correction: G♭ at 75 is mixture, not the §10 "neutralizing 7th"; the score's real turn back to C is 77–80

c403's reading note 14 called the G♭ at 75 the device of PDF 219–220 §10. On a closer reading it is not. §10 says the added minor 7th turns the temporary tonic into V7 *of the main key*, and that this 7th is "the one scale degree in the tonic key that does not appear in the key of the dominant". That holds for V. For VI it does not: A♭ plus G♭ is V7 of D♭, and G♭ is not a degree of C minor at all. The degrees of C minor that A♭ major lacks are D♮ and B♮.

What the score does:
- 73.1–2: a common-tone diminished seventh A♭–C♭–D–F over the A♭ pedal (c172). This is the first D♮ after 72, but it is a neighbour chord inside A♭.
- 75–76: G♭, then F♭ (D♭ minor 6/4 over A♭). This darkens A♭ towards A♭ minor (mixture, Unit 23), the opposite direction from C.
- 77–79: A♭ minor (C♭ = B), then E minor (E–G–B). B♮ and G, the leading tone and dominant of C, sound here as members of a chord a major third away.
- 80: C minor, the P head, with no dominant. This is the "brief sojourn" case of §10 (PDF 219): the stay in E is two bars, so the tonic can simply return in a prominent place.

So the A♭ area is not neutralized by a 7th. It is first darkened by mixture, then left by the major-third cycle A♭–E–C, and the return to C uses the short-stay rule. This is a Romantic substitute for §10, and it works: the C at 80 sounds like a return because the B♮ of 78–79 has already pointed to it. No edit. HISTORY.md should not cite PDF 219–220 §10 for bar 75 in future; the right citation is A&S Unit 23 (mixture) for 75–76 and PDF 219 §10, first paragraph, for 80.

### 3. The recapitulation's C-major second group and its turn to minor match Beethoven 9/i: keep

PDF 216 §6 and 219 §9 ask that a key area be confirmed by its own dominant and an authentic cadence, and allow a deceptive cadence before the authentic one (Ex. 14-1 bars 6–10). The score's C-major area does exactly this:
- 145–147: V7 (with the major ninth E), poco rit., double bar, key signature, S head at pp.
- 155: imperfect cadence (tune on E, 3̂).
- 159–160: V7 to ♭VI (A♭), fff, with Tbn 1 B3→C4 (c375, c414). The leading tone rises, so the deceptive cadence is voiced as PDF 219 describes.
- 161–162: vii°7, V7, then vi (A minor): a second deceptive cadence.
- 163–166: IV, iiø7, cadential 6/4, V7, I, with D–C in Vln I: the PAC.
- 166.3: E♭ in the violas turns C major back to C minor for the coda.

Beethoven 9/i's recapitulation puts its second group in D major at onsets 672–700 (bars 337–351): ii4/2–V7–I–V7–I–V6/4–V4/2–I. At onset 700 (bar 351) it turns to D minor by vii°4/3 of V, that is, by mixture, not by a 7th. When the key area is the tonic's own major mode, §10's 7th is not needed; the change of one degree (F♮ in Beethoven, E♭ here) does the work. The score's C-major area lasts 20 bars against Beethoven's 14, and it carries the third climax. That is the right weight for a movement whose finale goes from C minor to C major: the major is heard and then withdrawn. Keep (c240, c293: no forte C-major cadence before the finale; the PAC at 166 is p).

### 4. B minor, 97–102: a key without its cadence, left by phrase modulation: keep

PDF 216 §6 says "even a fleeting passage centered on V will require a progression of several chords, often culminating in a cadence". B minor has i (97–98), iv (99), iiø7 (100) and V7 over an F♯ pedal (101–102), but no return to i. The next phrase starts in D minor at 103 without a pivot. That is the phrase modulation of Ex. 14-11 (PDF 223): a new key at the start of a phrase, set off by a change of texture (the two heads combined, f).

The reading's word is "often", not "always", and a development is the place where a key may be stated and not confirmed. Beethoven 9/i holds G minor for about 41 bars and A minor for about 38 in its development (harmony.csv, onsets 318–401 and 484–560). c113 asked for a held development key on that model, and c123 rejected it with a reason I accept: the development's stations are degrees of one large progression C–A♭–F–G (A&S Unit 16, PDF 249–258), and section lengths are protected. This reading gives no reason to reopen that. Keep.
