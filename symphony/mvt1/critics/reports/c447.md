# Critic 447: texture and counterpoint (v447)

Lens: texture and counterpoint: inner-voice interest, accompaniment figures, counter-melodies, the fugato, imitation, the bass line.
Reading: Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, ch. 17 "The Orchestra as Accompanist" (adler2 PDF 256-290), third pass.

Method. I read the text of every page from PDF 256 to 290, and I rendered PDF 257-289 at 110 dpi and looked at every example, XVII-1 to XVII-25. The landscape pages (Bizet XVII-20, Fidelio XVII-22, Bach XVII-23, Orff XVII-25) are too small at that size to read note by note, so for those I rely on the prose and on what the layout shows: which staves move and which hold. I looked at all 17 music pages of v447 at 70 dpi. I rebuilt full.midi in /tmp/critic-447/work and ran three scans over it with scripts in /tmp/critic-447/scripts:
- `dump.py`: lists the notes in a range of bars.
- `coattack.py`: finds accompanying parts that strike a tune's pitch at the same instant. It covers 41 tune spans, and these are the sections of the reading on dialogue, colour contrast and Mozart's horns.
- `rhythm.py`: finds accompanying parts that copy the tune's rhythm with other pitches, bar by bar. This is the rhythmic-independence section.

c247 and c347 already used this chapter for this lens, and c373 and c410 used it for other lenses. I do not repeat their points. This pass reruns their scans on v447, so it covers every change made since v347. I also looked for one passage where a master's texture would be clearly better.

## Findings

I found nothing in v447 that the reading shows to be wrong and that earlier cycles have not settled. I recommend no change. Below are the places where the reading came closest to asking for one, ranked by how much each could matter, with the reason for keeping each.

### 1. Whole-movement check: the accompaniment vocabulary already covers the chapter (keep)

Each guideline from PDF 257 has a clear, different example in the score:
- dialogue at 47→56 and 147→156;
- rhythmic independence at 15-22 and 56-68;
- the foreground/background exchange at 103-110;
- colour contrast at 5, 73 and 171;
- transparency at 147-155;
- register at 73-76.

No two neighbouring sections use the same accompaniment pattern. The scans found no accompanying part that copies a tune's rhythm with other pitches, except the cadence strokes at 72 and the bass-line horn at 104.

I also asked whether some moment within this lens would be forgettable next to a master's. Sustained pads are the background at 43-46, 73-76, 107-110, 147-155 and 171-174. In every case Adler's own examples call for a pad there:
- 73-76 is the Mozart flute layout (PDF 267);
- 147-155 is Sibelius (PDF 269);
- 171-174 is the light texture of Fidelio and Mahler (PDF 283).

Each has one moving element against the pad: the harp, the C.a. fills, the B.cl head, or the solo cello. Giving them more motion would undo the contrast the chapter asks for.

### 2. Bars 167-169: Hn 3 restrikes C4 at 168.1 inside the horns' own P head (keep)

At 167-169 Hn 1·2 (a2), Tpt 1·2 (an octave higher) and the violas play the P head at ff. Hn 3 holds C4 at f, written `c'1 | c'1 | c'1`, so it restrikes on each downbeat. Hn 4 and Tbn 2 move G3→A♭3 on 168.3. The scan finds Hn 3's C4 at 168.1 and the A♭3 at 168.3 sounding with the head's own C4 and A♭3 in Hn 1·2, which is the case PDF 264 warns about (Mozart keeps the orchestral horns off the solo horn's line).

I keep it, for three reasons:
- At 168.1 the head itself repeats the C4 it reached on 167.4.5. Hn 3's restrike adds no new attack to the line, and in 27-32 the held brass also restrike at 28.1.
- The trumpets carry the head an octave higher at ff. That is the octave a listener follows, and the held brass are a dynamic lower.
- The A♭3 on 168.3 is the change to the A♭ chord under the head (C–E♭–A♭ over the C pedal). If Hn 4 and Tbn 2 moved off that beat, the harmonic rhythm of the hammered layout that 27-32 sets up would change.

Tying Hn 3's C4 (`c'1~ | c'1~ | c'1`) would remove one attack that nobody could hear separately. It would also take away the brass pulse under the head, which matches the Cb quavers. This is not worth a change in a passage that c389 and c417 protect.

### 3. Flute doublings of Vln I (Bizet, PDF 279-281) (keep)

The scan finds Fl 1 on Vln I's pitches only at four places:
- 11.3-12, the top of the introduction's rising sixths;
- 64.1-65.3, the S peak in A♭;
- 115-116, the wedge's top;
- 158-161, the climax.

This is Bizet's rule exactly: the doubling an octave above, or at pitch, is kept for the climax. Elsewhere Vln I sings without a flute. Keep. The 64-66 Fl 1 exit is protected (c441).

### 4. Bars 13-14: the horn call inside the string chord (PDF 274, keep)

Hn 1's E♭3–C4–B3 (mp) lies among Vc G3 and Vla C4/E♭4–D4 (p diminuendo). That is not registrally distant. Still, the reading's own exception applies: "rhythmic contrast lessens the importance of this consideration" (PDF 274). The strings hold minims and semibreves while the horn moves. Hn 1 also never shares an attack with them, because the violas leave C4 for D4 exactly as the horn reaches C4 at 13.3. Hn 3 releases its G3 at 13.2 as Hn 1 enters, which is Mozart's practice (PDF 264). The horn call is protected and quoted. Keep.

### 5. Bars 47-55: viola pad tops on the S head's downbeat pitches (keep, protected)

The violas (div., pp) start held notes at 47.1 (C4), 49.1 (D♭4), 51.1 (E♭4) and 55.1 (C4). These are common tones with Hn 1 and the cellos at those instants. They are a different family from the horn, in a lower dynamic and in long notes. That is the heterophonic support in a contrasting colour that PDF 264 calls harmless. c247 protected the pad with this reading in hand. Keep.

### 6. Bar 104: Hn 4 follows the bass line in the tune's rhythm (keep)

`rhythm.py` flags Hn 4 (F3–G3–E3, crotchet–minim–crotchet) against Hn 1's head in 104. Hn 4 doubles the bass two octaves up (Bsn 2/B.cl/Cb D2–G2–A2), and in this bar the bass and the tune share their rhythm. There is no shared pitch with Hn 1, and c347's rests for Hn 3 at 104.1 and 105.3 still stand. Keep.

### 7. Fugato 85-96 and the bass line (PDF 286-287, keep)

Bach's example shows strong, clear harmony under the counterpoint. Here the subject is alone at 85-87, the bass enters at 90, and the bass is full under entries 3-4. The bass line uses five kinds of writing, each tied to one function:
- the quaver pulse under P (Orff, PDF 288);
- pizzicato under S (Liszt, PDF 274);
- held pedals at 73-76 and 117-130 (Mozart, PDF 267);
- the free fugato line;
- the motto bass in the chorale, doubled two octaves higher by Vln I from 177.4 (Fidelio, PDF 283).

Nothing to change.
