## Findings

No change. Nothing in this pass meets the bar of a real improvement that the reading or the masters support. I checked each principle above against the score bar by bar. Then I looked for places where the expression falls short of a master's, beyond what the rules require. Below are the four candidates I considered seriously, and why I leave each one.

### Considered and kept 1: Bars 160–162, the step from fff to ff with no diminuendo

**What I looked at.** The tune parts (Vln I·II, Vc, Fl 1·2, Ob 2) hold fff through 160 and through the falling bar 161 (F6–D6–B5–C6). At 162 the strings restart at ff on the A of the deceptive cadence and then fade from 162 to 163. 161 has no hairpin, so the drop from fff to ff falls exactly on the barline. Adler's Franck climax (PDF 38) shapes each bar of the climax with < >, and the dovetailing note (PDF 192) asks that seams not show.

**Why I keep it.** The seam at 162 is a structural accent and should show. It is the deceptive turn to A minor (the release after the ♭VI climax). The flutes leave there, the harp enters f, and the brass start their ff > mp exit (protected brass-first fade, c108, c188). So the drop arrives with a change of colour and harmony at once, which is the case Adler names as the exception ("unless ... one wishes each group ... to be accented"). A diminuendo through 161 would also weaken the fff span that the protected ladder 82 ff < 122 fff < 160 fff depends on (c098, c318), and the "largamente" already gives the bar its breadth. Per-bar < > swells, as at 97–102, would not suit 160–161 either: the tune there is in four octaves at fff, and Adler's Franck climax is at f, where a swell can still be heard.

### Considered and kept 2: Bars 156–157, p to ff in two bars

**What I looked at.** Hn 1·2 and Hn 3·4 go from p at 156 to ff at 158, and the bassoons, clarinets, violas and cellos from mp. That is a steep crescendo under the poco animando. Adler's two climax examples get there in different ways: Franck alternates soft choirs for several bars (PDF 38), and Tchaikovsky arrives suddenly after the strings thin out (PDF 38–40).

**Why I keep it.** The violins' pickup at 155.4 is already mf, so the line climbs from a higher level than the horns. The harp goes mf to f across 156–157 and the suspended cymbal swells from pp at 159, so the arrival is staged by colour as well as by dynamic. Lengthening the approach would mean changing the form, and the climax approach (156–159) and the proportions are protected (c393, c443). Within this lens the marks are consistent and playable.

### Considered and kept 3: Bar 84, "sub. p" after a rest

**What I looked at.** At 84 the cellos and basses enter "sub. p" after the ff strokes of 83 and a crotchet rest. By Adler's definition (PDF 190) this is not a scored subito piano, because nothing overlaps.

**Why I keep it.** It is protected (c098), and c248 and c348 kept it. The word still tells the players to drop at once rather than recover from the ff, which is what the following fugato entry at 85 needs. There is no case for adding an overlapping choir: any instrument held through 83–84 would cover the silence that separates the head dialogue from the fugato.

### Considered and kept 4: Bar 117, timpani f under the ff tutti

**What I looked at.** In Tchaikovsky's tutti (PDF 39–40) the timpani roll is at the full level with everything else. At 117 our timpani G roll starts f while the winds and brass are ff.

**Why I keep it.** The roll is the dominant pedal for 117–130. It has to grow to fff at 122 (devTimp: f, then < at 121, fff at 122) and then fade. Starting at ff would remove the crescendo into the movement's second climax and would cover the violas' and cellos' f figures at 117. Tchaikovsky's roll begins at the climax itself. Ours begins five bars before it.
