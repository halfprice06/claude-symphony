## Findings

The harp writing is idiomatic and c246's fixes hold. I found two places where the left hand has nothing that stops its low strings before a change of harmony. Both are in the second group's build to its peak. The left hand is free in both bars, so the fix is a marking and, at 61, one shortened note. Both are applied in my scratch copy.

### 1. Bars 60–63: the low G♭ and F♭ strings are not stopped before the sub. p at 62 and the G♮ change at 63 (applied)
At 60 the left hand strikes G♭1–G♭2, and at 61 it strikes F♭2–F♭3 twice. All of this is under the crescendo from 59. At 62 the orchestra drops sub. p onto the cadential 6/4 of A♭. The harp's left hand starts its E♭2–A♭2–C3–E♭3 figure on the same downbeat. The only damping mark is "étouffez" over the right hand's rest at 62. That rest lets the right hand stop its own F♭4/F♭5 chord. It does not stop the left hand's strings:
- The left hand is plucking E♭2 on that beat. The F♭2 string is the next string up, and the left hand cannot damp it and pluck its neighbour at the same instant. c246 made the same point at 68: E♭ and F are neighbouring strings, so placing a finger on one does not damp the other.
- G♭1 and G♭2 were struck at 60, under the crescendo. Neither lies next to any string the left hand uses at 62, so nothing damps them.
- At 63, beat 1, the G pedal goes from G♭ to G♮ for the G3 on beat 2½. If G♭1/G♭2 are still sounding, the pedal bends a vibrating wound string from G♭ to G♮. That slide is audible in a sub. p bar. Cycles 136 and 171 removed the same fault at 50 and 59–60 ("no pedal change on a ringing string").

Evidence: Adler PDF 113–114: the lower two octaves are the "somber, dark, and sonorous" register. These are the wound strings, and they ring longest. PDF 117: damping needs a passage slow enough for the hand to stop the strings. PDF 111–113: a pedal re-pitches every string of its letter, and the harpist moves it just before the next note. Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 46: in the middle and low octaves the strings ring on, the player stops them with the hands at a change of harmony, and where that is not possible "the mixture of one chord with another produces a discordant effect". Here the mixture is an E (F♭) and a G♭ under the E♭–A♭–C chord, in the bass, in the one bar the composer marked sub. p.

Fix (tTwo.ily, tTwoHarpL, bar 61): the second F♭ octave becomes a crotchet, and the left hand damps on beat 4.
`<fes, fes>2 <fes, fes>4 r4-\tweak self-alignment-X #RIGHT ^\markup \small \italic "étouffez (m.g.)" |`
The cellos, double basses and bassoons hold the bass through beat 4, so the harp's beat of silence is not exposed. The RH "étouffez" at 62 stays. Each hand now damps its own strings while it is free. The 63 G♮ change then happens on stopped strings.

### 2. Bars 63→64: the V7 bass rings into the F-minor octave at the peak (applied)
At 63 the left hand plays the V7 arpeggio E♭2–B♭2–D♭3–G3 on beats 1–2 and rests on beats 3–4. At 64 (the peak of the second group, V7→vi) it strikes F1–F2 mf. Nothing stops E♭2 (the neighbouring string of F2) or G3. The dominant bass then sounds a whole step under the new F and against the A♭ of the right hand's chord. This is the same progression and the same problem that c246 fixed at 67→68. At 68 the right hand had the rest. Here the left hand does.

Evidence: Adler PDF 113–114 (low strings sustain), PDF 117 (damping needs time), Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 46 (quoted above). The score's own scheme already damps at 62, 68, 163 and 164 for the same reason.

Fix (tTwo.ily, tTwoHarpL, bar 63):
`es,8(_\markup \small "G♮" bes, des g) r4_\markup \small "F♮" r4-\tweak self-alignment-X #RIGHT ^\markup \small \italic "étouffez (m.g.)" |`
The left hand keeps one and a half beats of resonance under the right hand's continuation of the line, then damps on beat 4. The right hand's B♭3–B♭4 carry the dominant into the barline. "(m.g.)" matches the existing "étouffez (m.d.)" at 164. No notes change.
