## Brahms 2/i: would a conductor believe a master wrote this?

On most pages, yes. These features stand comparison with Brahms 2/i:
- The motto works the way Brahms's D–C♯–D cell does (bars 1–5 in Vc and Cb, undivided, with the basses an octave below). A short figure stated low, pp and without harmony returns as the bass of the introduction, the head of P and S, and the coda chorale.
- The pp trombone chorale at 175–179 with the timpani under it is the same idea as Brahms's pp trombones and tuba over a timpani roll at 32–43. Our version keeps it for the end and adds a tam-tam.
- The coda's slow dissolution over a tonic pedal, with the last statement in one high voice, is shorter than Brahms's (16 bars against about 70, from 447), and that suits a movement of 182 bars.

One place does not stand comparison. At the climax at letter E (118–131) Brahms gives Vl I and Vl II separate lines, with Vl II below, and puts the winds above or between them. He never puts both violin sections in unison at the top of a ff tutti. Our 158–161 does exactly that (Finding 1). This is the only place in the movement where the high string line has no string octave under it. At 20, 31, 82, 97–102 and 122 the score already does what Brahms and Rimsky-Korsakov do.

## Findings

### 1. Bars 158–161: Vln II doubles Vln I at the unison at E6–A♭6. Move Vln II down an octave. (Applied)

**What is wrong.** At the fff climax both violin sections play E6–G6–C6–A♭6–F6–D6. Under them the next string octave is the cellos at E4–A♭4, two octaves lower. The violas play repeated C4/G4 chords. In this bar the A♭6 peak has the flutes and both violin sections at the top, then Ob 1, Tpt 1 and Hn 3·4 at A♭5/A♭4 and the cellos at A♭4, but no string in the octave just below it.

**Evidence.** Rimsky-Korsakov's rule for high violin melodies is octaves, not unison: "melodic figures in the very high register of the violins become too isolated from the rest of the ensemble unless doubled in octaves. Such doubling secures expression, fulness of tone and firmness of timbre" (PDF 58–59; Ex. 22, 23 and 25 are all Vln I over Vln II an octave lower). His note to Ex. 24 (PDF 59) explains why: keep a few desks on the top octave and the rest an octave lower, "the piercing quality of the highest notes will be diminished … and the expressive tone quality of the lower octave will be strengthened". Note I (PDF 62) generalises it: melodies in the extreme upper register "are generally doubled an octave below". The score already follows this at 31 and 122, where Vln II is on A♭5 under Vln I's A♭6. 160 is the third and highest of the A♭6 climaxes, and it is the one place without it. Brahms 2/i at E (118–131) also keeps Vl II below Vl I.

**Fix** (recTwo.ily, recTwoVlnTwo, the K bars):
```
| g'4\ff\downbow e''2.~ | e''4\< f''4 g''2 |
  c''4\fff\downbow aes''2~ aes''8 g''8 | f''2 d''4( b'8 c''8) | a'2.~(\ff ...
```
Only the octave changes; rhythm, bowing and dynamics stay the same. Two more things improve. Vln II now enters 158 from its G4 at 157 instead of leaping an octave. It also leaves 161 on C5 and falls a third to A4 at 162, not a tenth from C6. That removes the clOne/vlnTwo fifth F5→E5 / C6→A4 at 162.1 from vl.txt. The new vl.txt lines at 158, 161 and 162 are all octave doublings of the tune (Vc, Hn 1·2, Tpt 1).

**Protection answered.** c167 protected "unison violins 56–59 and 158–161" on Sebesky's Rule 4, "the higher the register, the less division" (sebesky PDF 139). That rule is about splitting one small section into divisi. Sebesky himself says the rules matter "in inverse ratio to the number of string players" (PDF 138). Here no section is divided: Vln II stays a full unison section, and only its octave changes. c141's "E-string climax 156–161" is kept, because Vln I stays on the E string. Bars 56–59 are not touched. The climax scoring of the winds, brass and percussion at 158–166 is unchanged. The Vln I peak A♭6 at 160 is unchanged, so the rule "no violin above A♭6" still holds. Vln II's highest note in the movement is now B♭5 (bar 96).

### 2. Bars 6–7: the double basses are divided in octaves (G1+G2, F1+F2). Write them unis. on G2 and F2. (Applied)

**What is wrong.** Rimsky-Korsakov, Note II (PDF 62): "Progression in octaves of divided strings of the same kind is generally to be avoided" (violas, cellos, basses), "for, in such cases the parts are played on strings which do not correspond, and unity of tone is impaired". He makes the exception for violins only. In the pp string chords at 6–7, half the basses play G1/F1 and half G2/F2, the only place in the movement where the basses are divided in octaves. c097 added the upper half so that the bass would not be two octaves below the cellos.

**Fix** (intro.ily, introCb): `c,1\pp | g,1 | f,1\< | c,1\p\> |`. The "div." and "unis." marks go. This keeps c097's aim with one line: G2 is an octave below the cellos' G3, and F2 is a tenth below their A♭3. The bass of 5–8 is now C2–G2–F2–C2, one undivided line, as Brahms writes his basses at 1–5 and 32–43. The pianissimo loses nothing: half the section was already at G2/F2. "Scoring of 1–4" and "CA 5–8" (protected) are untouched. In the vl.txt diff the `cb.lo` duplicates disappear (the bass track has no chords left), and no new line appears.

### 3. Bars 158–161: Cl 1 doubles the inner line an octave above Ob 2. Keep it.

**What I checked.** Cl 1 plays G5–C6–B5–F5–E♭5–C6 an octave above Ob 2, Hn 3 and Hn 4 (G4–C5–B4–F4–E♭4–C5). By PDF 67 (Ex. 56–59), "the clarinet above the oboe" in octaves is an inverted order with "unnatural resonance".

**Why keep it.** Rimsky-Korsakov limits the order rules to melody and to soft or characteristic doubling. On mixtures in loud tuttis he says they "produce increased resonance without creating a fresh atmosphere" (PDF 66). At fff with the brass, the inversion is not heard as a separate colour. The line is protected (Cl 1 counterline 158–162), and swapping the parts would put Ob 2 above Ob 1's tune on the shared staff. I record this so a later critic does not have to check it again.
