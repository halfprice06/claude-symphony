## Findings

All five findings end in no change. The scans found nothing that breaks a rule from the reading. I also tested each "could it be better" candidate below against the reading and the protected record, and none of them clears the bar.

### 1. Soft colour entries (Fig. 6.1 pattern): no change

**Reading.** In Fig. 6.1 (PDF 272) the woodwinds' pp chord stops one bar before the trumpets enter. The new colour comes into a space that has been cleared, over a string pedal that does not move.

**Scan.** I flagged every wind or brass entry at p or below that comes after at least a bar's rest and starts while another wind or brass part holds a note within a fourth. Out of the whole movement the scan found only two places:
- **145.3–147.3**: Hn 3 B3 enters under Bsn 1 and Cl 2. Ob 1 G4 enters over Cl 1·2. Hn 1 G3 enters at 146.4 over Hn 3's B3. The C.a. fill enters at 147.3 over Hn 1's E4. This is the hand-over into the recapitulation's S. The overlaps are meant to join the phrases (protected c349, c379).
- **181.1**: Hn 3·4 enter ppp on C4/G3 as Tbn 1 releases its C4. Hn 3 takes the same pitch, so this is a transfer on a common note (Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 125–126), not two colours blurring.

Everywhere else the movement follows the Mahler pattern:
- 5–9: Cb alone, then the C.a. over pp strings.
- 73–76: Fl 1 over pp strings and Hn 4.
- 77–81: B.cl, Cl 1, Hn 1·2 and Ob/Vln I take turns over the tremolo pad, each after the last has stopped.
- 174–177: the bassoons and C.a. stop at 174, the trombone chorale enters at 175, Cl 1 at 175.4, and Vln I at 177.4 as Cl 1's B4 fades. This is Fig. 6.1 almost bar for bar.

### 2. Register and dynamic (PDF 257, 275, 277): no change

**Reading.** Casanelles notes that the timbre of a loud clarinet "cannot be achieved when playing in a soft dynamic" (PDF 257). He also says the flute's low register has a weak fundamental and its pitch is heard lower (PDF 275, 277).

**Scan.** I looked for every note asked at p or below in a register where the instrument cannot sound soft:
- flute above A6
- oboe B♭3–D4, and C.a. E3–A♭3
- clarinet above C6
- bassoon below E2 at pp
- horns above C5 concert
- trumpets above G5
- trombones above F4

The only hit is Hn 1 C5 p at 43.4, the 7–6 suspension over the V of A♭. That is a written G5, which a first horn plays p without strain. No flute in the movement goes below B♭4. The flute motto at 73–76 lies at C5–A♭5 over pp strings with nothing in its band, so its weak low fundamental never comes into play.

### 3. Woodwind lines against brass in the same band (PDF 287, 289): no change

**Reading.** Sounds in the same band mask each other. A composer may leave out the brass where a flute line must be heard (PDF 287). Forte brass are the harmonically denser layer and come to the front (PDF 289).

**Scan.** I listed every woodwind note not doubled by a string that starts inside a brass note within a fifth, at the same or a higher MIDI level. Most hits are woodwinds doubling the brass chord: 26, 27–31, 33, 41, 69–72, 83, 117–120, 138–139, 143, 162 and 167–170. The rest are these:
- **56–62**: Cl 1 and Hn 2 play the counterline in unison. That is a deliberate blend (protected c371, c441), not one line covering another.
- **147–154**: the C.a. fills sound against Hn 1's S head (Hn 1 mp–mf, C.a. p dolce). The dump shows each fill starting while the horn holds a long note (147.3, 149.3, 151.3, 153.1). The two lines alternate in time and do not sit on top of each other. This is the frequency-allotment principle applied in time rather than in register.
- **159–161**: the Ob 1 and Cl 1 counter lies within a fifth of Tpt 1. Tpt 1 is on the tune and the counter moves in quavers against it, so the two are separated by rhythm. The counter has three reed players over Hn 3·4 (c408).

Nothing calls for removing brass.

### 4. Distance and perspective (PDF 271–274, 283–284): considered an offstage or "lontano" effect; rejected

**Reading.** The chapter's central example is Mahler's offstage trumpets: distance takes away the high partials and adds the room, and that gives the call its meaning (PDF 272–273). The Social Network shows a theme heard from further away each time it returns (PDF 283).

**What the score does.** It already changes the distance of its two signals by orchestration alone:
- **Horn call:** one horn mp at 13–14 (near, alone); four horns ff molto espr. at 41–42 (closest); Cl 1 pp at 175–177 inside the chorale (furthest).
- **Motto:** bare and low at 1–4; solo C.a. at 5–8; high solo flute at 73–76; full brass ff at 117–121; low clarinet at 127–130; Vln I pp on one string at 177–182 as the last and most distant statement.

This follows the Social Network principle without any stage effect.

**What I tested.** I considered placing the coda horn call or a trumpet echo off stage, or marking it "come da lontano". I reject it for three reasons:
1. Rimsky-Korsakov allows instruments in the wings only where "aesthetic considerations of greater importance" call for them, as in a staged scene (rimsky-principles PDF 147–148). In a symphony with no programme, an offstage call would bring a meaning the work does not use elsewhere.
2. The brass record in coherence/mvt1.md keeps movement I free of any soft trumpet melody. The finale's trumpet B→C arrival depends on that.
3. The Cl 1 pp horn call over the pp trombones already gives the change of distance the reading describes. The clarinet's odd-partial spectrum (PDF 275) sounds thinner and further away than a horn would.

### 5. The bar-160 climax and harmonic density (PDF 289): no change

**Reading.** By PDF 289 the densest layer comes to the front.

**What happens at 160.** Hn 3·4 play the counter in unison on C5 fff (a written G5), between the tune's A♭4 octave (Hn 1·2, Vc) and its A♭5 octave (Tpt 1, Vln II, Ob 2). A high horn pair at fff is a very dense sound. I checked whether it covers the tune's middle octave.

**Why it stays.** At 160 every octave of the tune has a brass instrument at fff:
- A♭4: Hn 1·2
- A♭5: Tpt 1
- A♭6: Fl 1·2 and Vln I, the top line, with nothing above it

So the counter is the denser layer only against single strings or reeds, and the tune has brass in each octave. The page (PDF page 16 of the score) shows the counter moving in quavers while the tune holds, which separates them in time as well. The layout is protected (c399, c408: "158–161 octave layout", "158–159 counter ff"). The reading gives no reason against it.
