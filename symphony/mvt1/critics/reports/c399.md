## Findings

### 1. Bar 123: the flutes hold G6/D6 for a whole bar through the fff–p diminuendo (applied)

**What is wrong.** At 122 Fl 1·2 play A♭6/D6 fff, and at 123 they hold G6/D6 as whole notes with a diminuendo (`\after 2.. \! g'''1\>`, `d'''1\>`). Since c318, bar 123 is where the upper parts begin to fade. At the top of the third octave the flute cannot fade: it holds a loud, hard G6 over violins that are trying to thin out. This is the exact case of Dvořák's Slavonic Dance No. 8, m. 33. There, every instrument in the ff–p diminuendo is put "in controllable ranges", and the piccolo is written an octave lower so that it can reach piano (adler2 PDF 213; score on PDF 212). Ravel makes his diminuendos the same way: the instruments "descend and drop out" (PDF 224). Bizet scores the opening diminuendo through exits instead of "leaving it to the players or conductor" (PDF 213; Ex. XVI-6, PDF 202). Adler's register chapter gives the reason: "some extreme high registers will be called shrill, and in most cases the performer can only play extremely loud" (adler1 PDF 185). The movement already follows this rule at the same pitch. At 41, c179 had Fl 1 take only the sf attack of G6 and leave on beat 3, and c179 cited the same Dvořák bar. The 123 case was missed.

**Rachmaninoff.** At the end of movement I (PDF 71–72) the flutes and piccolo are silent through the whole fade. Only clarinets, bassoons, horns and strings carry the diminuendo to pp, and he writes no soft high flute anywhere on those pages.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-399/work/sections/dev.ily).**
- `devFluteOne`: `\after 2.. \! g'''1\> | g''2.~\>( g''8 f''8)\! |` becomes `g'''2\> r2\! | g''2.~\mf\>( g''8 f''8)\! |`
- `devFluteTwo`: `\after 2.. \! d'''1\> |` becomes `d'''2\> r2\! |`

The flutes keep the A♭6→G6 resolution on the downbeat of 123, where it matters (c318). They leave on beat 3, and Fl 1 comes back on G5 at 124 marked mf, inside Ob 1's continuing diminuendo. The protected "Fl 1 octave drop at 124" stays (c825). G6 stays in Vln I (tremolo, 8va), and D stays in Ob 2 (D5) and Vln II. No chord tone is lost, and no other part changes.

### 2. Comparison with Rachmaninoff 3/i: no change needed

These points stay as they are, with reasons:
- **Opening.** Rachmaninoff opens with a four-bar Lento motto, pp, in a unison of mixed colours: clarinets a2, stopped horn and solo cello con sordino (PDF 7). Then a sudden f Allegro outburst (PDF 7–8). Our 1–4 is the same kind of thing: bass clarinet plus cellos in unison, double basses an octave below, timpani ppp. Our introduction is longer (14 bars) because it carries the rising-sixth chain and the horn call that the later movements quote. That is the right trade.
- **First theme.** Rachmaninoff gives his first theme to oboes and bassoons two octaves apart, e espressivo, over a light string background, and then alternates winds and strings every bar or two (PDF 9, fig. 1–2). This is the choir switching of Adler PDF 230 and 233–234. Our P sits on Vln I alone over strings, then builds by stages to 27. The transition 33–46 does the choir alternation (Bsn 1 solo 33, violas 34, clarinets and oboes 37–40). Both are master practice. The graded build 15–27 is protected and works.
- **Second theme.** Rachmaninoff's second subject (fig. 5, PDF 14) is in the cellos, dolce cantabile p. It sits over clarinets and bassoons dolce p, single harp chords, and pp string touches. Our 47–55 (horn and cellos over harp, a half-section pad and pizzicato basses) is close to this layout. Adler's points 9, 21, 22 and 33 hold here.
- **Coda.** Rachmaninoff's coda (fig. 34, PDF 68) brings back the second theme in a lighter texture: violins mf dolce, staccato horns, harp, and violas and cellos divided in three, pizzicato. The movement then fades over a timpani trill to pp (PDF 72). Our 171–182 (solo cello and C.a. with the S head in minor, the low motto in the opening colours, a ppp timpani roll on the last chord) does the same. It also follows Ravel's "return of the exact opening colours for the last five measures" (Adler PDF 224).
- **Where we differ on purpose.** Rachmaninoff's textures thicken through divisi in three (PDF 68). Ours use pairs, which the finale's reserves and the divisi scheme protect. His orchestra includes xylophone, triangle, side drum, bass drum and cymbals (staff list, PDF 7). We have no such battery, and Adler PDF 230 warns that a big battery can be "a bit excessive". Neither difference makes the score sound less like a master's work.
