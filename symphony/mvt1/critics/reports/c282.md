## Findings

### 1. Bar 143: the bass ♮2 sounds against the violins' passing ♭2 (applied in scratch)

**Bars.** 143, beats 3–4 (recOne.ily, Bsn 1 line 45, Vc line 165).

**Problem.** The tonic stop chord at 143 is followed by a thin texture: Cb C2, Vc and Bsn 1 in octaves C3, and Vln I. On beat 3 Vc/Bsn 1 move C3 → D3. Vln I plays the head E♭5–D5–D♭5–C5, so on beat 4 the violins' D♭5 sounds over the cellos' D3. This is a diminished octave between the bass voice and the top voice. In effect the ♭2–♮2 conflict is stated as a simultaneity in the outer voices.

**Evidence.** A&S treat ♭2 against ♮2 as a matter of placement. The successive cross relation is acceptable (PDF 484). The direct ♭2 → ♮2 is "possible" only when ♭2 is doubled in an inner voice and should be avoided in the soprano (PDF 484, Ex. 28-3; PDF 488, Ex. 28-12). When ♭2 appears as a passing note inside the tonic, its colour depends on the tonic underneath, typically held or pedalled (PDF 495, Ex. 28-20). The book also notes that figuration around ♭2 follows the tonic scale (PDF 496–500).

At 143 the D♭ is exactly that kind of passing ♭2 within I. The bass, however, moves to ♮2 at the same moment, so the passing chromatic note clashes with a diatonic one in the two outer parts.

The score's own other statements of this figure show the intended setting. At 27 and 167 the same Vln I E♭–D–D♭–C sounds over a held C in the bass, with the cellos on C and E♭. No ♮2 sounds under the D♭. Bar 143 is the only place where it does.

**Fix (applied).** Vc and Bsn 1 hold C3 through 143 and move to E♭3 at 144:
- recOne.ily line 45 (Bsn 1): `c2\f d2 | r8 g8…` → `c1\f | r8 g8…`
- recOne.ily line 165 (Vc): `c2\f d2 | es2.…` → `c1\f | es2.…`

The 144 i6, the Bsn 1 answer of the head at 144, and the c222 D3 at 145 are unchanged.

**Protection.** c239 lists "143–150 lead-in" as do-not-touch. That came from a holistic comparison, and its concern was the lead-in's events and pacing. The change keeps every event, rhythm and dynamic of the lead-in, the short brass chord at 143 (c174), and the stop-chord doublings at 142–143 (c222). It removes one passing bass note doubled in two parts. It does not undo an earlier recorded fix; no HISTORY entry introduced the D3.

### 2. Bar 116: the augmented sixth before the development's goal dominant lies above a C bass (not applied; keep)

**Bars.** 115–117 (dev.ily).

**Problem.** At 116 the harmony is the Fr-shaped C–D–F♯–A♭ over a C2 bass. The aug6 itself, A♭2–F♯3, lies between Tbn 2 and Tbn 1/Hn 4/Vc. A&S place the aug6 just before the climactic dominant of a development (PDF 503, Ex. 29-1) and as the standard return from a tonicized IV to the home V (PDF 516–517, Ex. 29-28). Both describe 111–117. A&S also say that with a bass other than ♭6 or ♯4 the chord "will not move by stepwise bass to root-position V" and has little structural weight before the late 19th century. The linear pull is strongest when the bass and an upper voice form the aug6 (PDF 520–521). On that reading the strongest version would put A♭ in the bass at 116.

**Why keep it.** The protection (cycle 57: no A♭ in the bass before the A♭ arrival; c133; "bass 111–116" in the 517 do-not-touch list) is partly stale. The 122 A♭ now sits in Vc A♭3 over the G pedal, not in the bass. But a second reason holds and the reading supports it: the wedge bass D♭–C–G is motivic. A&S accept a bass that bypasses the normal ♭6–5 when "the rising bass has an important motivic purpose" (PDF 516, Ex. 29-27, Mozart K. 491).

In addition, the ♭6–5 is still heard in the low register: the timpani roll A♭2 → G2 and Tbn 2 A♭2 lie just above the C2 bass and resolve to G at 117. This is a late-Romantic bass position, of the kind PDF 520 assigns to later music, and that fits the style of this symphony.

Recommendation: no change.

### 3. Bars 12–13: ♭6 doubled in the top voice moves in octaves with the bass (not applied; keep)

At 12.4–13.1 Fl 1/Vln I A♭5 → G5 doubles the bass A♭2 → G2. A&S's doubling rules for the It6 exist to avoid such octaves (PDF 505). For the Ger6 they show ♭6 only in the bass (PDF 505–506, Ex. 29-8).

The octave here is a register doubling of the bass line in a tutti. Cycle 112 already answered this with Rimsky-Korsakov and A&S PDF 84–85 (orchestral octave doublings), and 12–13 is protected in at least eight cycles. The A♭5 is also the end of the protected violin leaps 9–12. No change.
