## Findings

None of the points below reaches the bar for a change. I list the five I tested hardest and the reason each stays.

### Candidate 1. Bars 77–82: the major-third cycle uses minor triads (no change)

**What I checked.** The bass is A♭ (77) – E (78.3) – C (80) – A♭ (82), which divides the octave into major thirds. A&S PDF 573 says such cycles "usually descend" and "use only major triads". The score descends, but its triads are A♭ minor, E minor and C minor, and only the arrival at 82 is major, ff.

**Why it stays.**
- PDF 573 gives the major-triad form as the usual one, not the only one.
- The minor quality comes from the head motive, which is minor at every appearance in the dialogue.
- The ff A♭ major at 82 sounds as a release because it follows three minor chords. Making 77–80 major would take that release away.
- The notation follows PDF 573. The d4 is written A♭→E (allowed "for ease of reading", PDF 573), and the viola's C♭→B shows the enharmonic turn.
- 77–83 is protected (HISTORY lines 531, 616; c430).

### Candidate 2. Bars 147–155: a common-tone °7 or a CT augmented sixth in the C-major S (no change)

**What I checked.** A&S PDF 544 and 546 call the CT°7 the most frequent chromatic embellishment of major triads. Tchaikovsky 5/III (Ex. 30-24) puts one between I and V4/3. The recapitulation's S is the movement's longest stretch of C major, and its only chromatic chord is IV+ at 148.

**Why it stays.**
- PDF 532 says mixture and chromatic colour stand out more in major. The score saves its colour for 156–166: ♭VII, iv, ♭VI fff, iiø4/3 with A♭, and E♮→E♭.
- A CT°7 at 147–150 would spend that contrast early, so the climax would arrive with less colour against it.
- "S diatonic 47–55/147–155" is protected (c489), and the reason given there still holds.

### Candidate 3. Bars 108.3–110: Vln II D♯4 leaps a diminished third to F4 (no change)

**What I checked.** PDF 558–560 says a chromatic note should be spelled toward its goal. Here D♯ (leading tone of E minor) moves to F, not to E, as the harmony turns to B♭ minor at 109.

**Why it stays.** The line is E–E–D♯–F–F–E: D♯ and F are the lower and upper chromatic neighbours of E, and the line reaches E at 110.3 over V of F minor. The spelling points to the note the voice actually reaches, which is what PDF 559–560 asks. The 108→109 join and the 108.3 voicing are also protected (c462, line 616).

### Candidate 4. Bar 73: CT°7 spelled with C♭ instead of B♮ (no change; recorded as correct)

**What I checked.** The standard spelling is A♭–B♮–D–F (PDF 544 §13). The viola writes C♭4→C4, which moves up with a flat.

**Why it stays.** PDF 544–545 (§13, Ex. 30-21, Brahms Op. 87) gives the ♭3 spelling for passages where mixture matters, and 73–76 is such a passage: the flute motto turns A♭ major to A♭ minor (F♭ at 76, C♭ at 77). The C♭ looks ahead to that turn. This spelling has not been recorded in HISTORY before, so I record it here.

### Candidate 5. Bar 162: fifths by contrary motion, Tbn 2 F3→E3 against Vc C4→A2 (no change)

**What I checked.** This is the only P5 entry in vl.txt. The cello leaves the tune's lower octave (C4) for the bass of the vi chord (A2), against the seventh of V7 resolving down in Tbn 2.

**Why it stays.** The double basses (A1), tuba (A1), bass trombone (A2) and Bsn 2 (A2) carry the bass, so the cello's A2 is a doubling. The fifths are by contrary motion in a ff tutti. My reading does not treat this case, and moving the cello would break the protected 161–162 tune hand-off.
