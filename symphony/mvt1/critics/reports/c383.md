## Findings

Nothing in the assigned reading stands between this score and a master's first movement strongly enough to justify a change. The findings are ranked by how much each would matter if it were wrong. No change is applied in the scratch copy.

### 1. V and V7 at the phrase seams: each is where the reading puts it. Keep.

A&S say V7 "conveys an inappropriate feeling of activity" when the tune comes to rest, so "V7 is rarely used in a semicadence" (6 §10, PDF 99–100). I checked every point where a phrase or hypermeasure stops on the dominant.

The real semicadences all stand on a plain triad:
- 84: bare G.
- 102: F♯ major after the 6/4.
- 106: B with the ♭6.
- 110: C major as V of F.
- 126: G–B–D.
- 146: after the 13th.
- 174: D–G–B with no F, the coda's half cadence.

V7 appears at four seams, and none of them is a stopping point:
- 14 ends the introduction, which must lead into the Allegro (c293).
- 50 and 150 are the mid-points of the S statements, and V7 goes straight on to V6/5 of IV.
- At 59 the tune rests on G5 (7̂) over E♭ with D♭ in Hn 3, the harp, the cellos and the Cl 1/Hn 2 counterline. This D♭ does not resolve at 60. It is held through G♭ (60) and F♭ (61) and falls to C only at the cadential 6/4 of 62.

In A&S's terms, 59 is therefore a V7 whose seventh is prolonged and resolves in the last voice that holds it (Ex. 6-19, PDF 102). It is not a semicadence. The violins' fade at 58–59 (c368) is still right as phrasing, and V7 is right because the motion must continue to 62. Keep.

### 2. No authentic-cadence effect inside a phrase. Keep.

A&S warn against V–I with 1̂ on top in the middle of a phrase. They tell the student to lead the soprano to 3̂ or 5̂ instead (7 §12, PDF 115; PDF 117; box on PDF 119). cad.py found 15 root-position V→I motions. Those with 1̂ on top are all phrase ends or section openings: 22.3, 47.1, 55.1, 72.1, 143.1 and 166.1. The others carry 3̂ or 5̂ on top: 36.1 (5̂), 41.1 (3̂), 48.1 (5̂), 107.1 (3̂), 111.1 (3̂), 135.1 (3̂), 147.1 (5̂) and 148.1 (3̂).

The one doubtful hit is 140.1. The bass rises A♭→D♭ under a ff root-position D♭ chord with D♭6 on top, which matters for the reservation "D♭ is never a key in movement I" (coherence/mvt1.md). The chord before it, at 139.3–4, is F minor over A♭ (iv6). Its E♭5 and E4 are passing tones in the tune and the violas, so it is not V of D♭. Bar 141 then turns at once to iv–ii° and V. The exposition's bar 20 has the same progression, iv6–♭II. Keep.

### 3. Harmonic rhythm at the seams and the tension curve. Keep.

"Changing a chord ... tends to attract an accent" and weak→strong repetition "contradicts the meter", except for "an initial tonic" (6 §16, PDF 102). Motivic grouping can also justify it (Ex. 6-21, PDF 103; Ex. 7-18c, PDF 116). I checked 19 section and phrase seams (14→15, 32→33, 46→47, 68→69, 72→73, 76→77, 84→85, 96→97, 102→103, 110→111, 116→117, 126→127, 130→131, 146→147, 166→167, 170→171, 174→175, 178→179, 181→182). Seventeen change chord over the barline. The two exceptions are justified:
- 126→127 lies inside the G pedal.
- 166→167 repeats C minor weak→strong. Bar 167 is the coda's initial tonic, with a new tempo, the brass P head and a new texture, which is exactly A&S's justified case.

The harmonic-rhythm table shows a clear curve. The fastest bass motion in the development (103–110, 9.8 changes per 10 s) leads into the wedge (4.4) and then into 29 s of a single bass note (117–130). That is A&S's expanded V that "helps to shape the form" (Ex. 6-4, PDF 95). The pedal is in root position as 7 §8 requires (PDF 112). Inside it, the i6/4 chords at 118, 121 and 125 are the neighbour "I" of the Jupiter example (Ex. 8-14, PDF 129–130).

Corpus comparison:
- Mozart 41/i: the retransition dominant is G from 179 to 188 (MCTL harmony.csv, "G I"/"V7" alternations), 10 of 68 development bars, 15 %.
- Tchaikovsky 6/i: 26 of 144 bars, 18 % (HC 1110–1214).
- Here: 14 of 54 bars, 26 %, carrying the fff climax at 122.

The heavier weighting suits a short development that holds the main climax. Keep; the section lengths are protected (c013, c043, c243).

### 4. Sevenths and the V7–I6 rule. Keep.

sev.py lists nine V7→I changes where a part holding the seventh does not step down. Each one is resolved elsewhere or has already been settled:

| Bar | What happens | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 24.3 | Cl 1 and Vln II F4→G4 in the hemiola | c262 |
| 41.1 | Vln II A♭4→G5; G4 in Ob 2, Vla and Hn 4 at pitch | resolved elsewhere |
| 68.1 | Vc arpeggio leaps; Vla D♭4→C4 | resolved elsewhere |
| 101→102 | E4 under the 6/4 | resolved elsewhere |
| 166.1 | Vc takes the bass; Cl 1 F4→E4 | resolved elsewhere |
| 177→178 | doubled F (Vln II F4→E♭4, Tbn 2 F3→G3) | c309 |
| 50→51 and 150→151 | V7 → V6/5 of IV | discussed below |

Bars 50→51 and 150→151 are V7 → I6(♭7), a case A&S tell students to avoid (Ex. 7-7, PDF 109). Their remedy is V4/2 between the two chords (Ex. 8-8, PDF 126). The hazard they name is a hidden octave on 3̂, from 4̂–3̂ above a bass that leaps 5̂–3̂. That octave is not present here:
- At 50 the viola's D♭4 rises to E♭4, and the harp's D♭5 resolves to C5 two octaves above the bass C2.
- At 150 Vln II's F5 goes to C5/B♭5 (c312), and E sounds only in the bass.

The remedy would also cost something. A D♭ under the A♭–G of 50.3 (Cb `es,4 r4 des,4 r4`, harp LH `es,,2 des,2`, violas `<bes des'>2 <bes es'>2`) would add a chord change at the end of the theme's first four-bar unit, where the harmony now rests. It would also touch the S accompaniment, which HISTORY.md protects many times (47–55 and 147–155). Keep.

The doubled seventh at 177 breaks A&S's checklist ("make sure that neither the leading tone nor the 7th of V7 is doubled", PDF 104; summary PDF 132). c309 kept it with "repairs worse", and I tested that reason against the reading:
- The direct repair, Tbn 2 E♭3 on 178.1 (`es2 f2` for `g2 f2`), makes Tbn 2 shadow Vln II in octaves G–F–E♭–F from 176 to 178, so the chorale's inner voice stops being independent.
- Giving Tbn 2 the bass D3 at 177 puts it in unison with the bass trombone and empties the chord.

The reason stands. Keep.

### 5. The ending closes by contrapuntal cadences. Keep, with one more reason.

A&S: stepwise-bass cadences give "a much weaker articulation ... virtually never used at the end of a piece" (8 §11, PDF 129). The last root-position V→I in C minor is 142→143 (ff, C6). The last one in any mode is 165→166 (p, C major). After 167 the tonic is reached only over pedals or by step: the stop chord over C at 170→171, and vii°6/5→i at 177→178.

c293 kept this because the movement does not end (five parts tie into movement II, attacca), and I agree. I add the phrase model of A&S's own exercises (PDF 117–118). The coda is an antecedent, 171–174, that ends on a plain V triad, exactly the semicadence A&S prefer (row 11). The consequent, 175–182, expands the tonic by the bass arpeggiation G–E♭–(D)–C of Ex. 7-8b (Brahms 3/ii, PDF 110). Its passing vii°6/5 at 177 is the passing chord from I6 down to I (Ex. 8-5, PDF 124).

The 5̂–1̂ bass motion of an authentic cadence is still heard, in the timpani pulse C3–G2–C3 at 179–180. Corpus: Tchaikovsky 6/i ends with a plagal cadence over a tonic pedal (cadence.csv onset 1362, "PC"), not with V–I. Adding a root-position V under 177 would turn the protected i6 – V4/3 – i (c262) into a V♭9 and touch the coda notes (protected by c228, c249 and c328). Keep.

### 6. Cadence order and proportions. Keep.

The two second groups follow Ex. 6-3 (PDF 94–95): the weaker cadence first, the strongest last. In the exposition that is 55 (3̂ in the tune), deceptive at 64 and 68, then the PAC at 72. In the recapitulation it is 155 (E5, 3̂), deceptive at 160 and 162, then the PAC at 166. The codetta 69–76 is left out of the recapitulation, but its P-head material returns at 167–170 over a C pedal, so the 0.61 ratio loses no material. Keep the proportions (c013, c043, c243, c283).
