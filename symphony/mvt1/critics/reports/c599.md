## Findings

### 1. Bars 113–116: the development's main build has no tempo drive (applied in scratch)

**What is wrong.** The wedge 111–116 is the largest build in the movement: the bass falls F–E–E♭–D–D♭–C by whole bars, the S-head rhythm climbs to Vln I's D6 at 116, and the brass enter in stages (Tpt 1·2, Tpt 3, Tbn 1·2 pp cresc. at 113, B.tbn at 114, timpani mf–f at 115–116) into the ff dominant pedal at 117 and the fff at 122. Everything is notated except the tempo, which stays at crotchet = 126 from 68.3 to 120. The score already writes a tempo push before its other two big arrivals ("poco animando" 62–63 into the S peak at 64, and 156–157 into 158). The development's arrival, the one before the movement's first fff, is the only one left to the conductor.

**Evidence.**
- Sibelius 5/i moves the tempo inside the music at every large build. The cresc. molto at mm. 95–103 runs into "poco a poco meno moderato" (PDF 28), the B-major climax with trumpets follows (PDF 29), and the movement turns into "Allegro moderato (ma poco a poco stretto)" without a double bar (PDF 30). A conductor who knows this movement, or Tchaikovsky's development builds, would expect the composer to have marked the drive into the dominant pedal.
- Adler PDF 213: Bizet "orchestrated a diminuendo ... instead of being left to the players or conductor", and "did the same for the crescendo, mm. 13–18". Adler PDF 224: Ravel is "careful to notate even the slightest details". The reading's rule is to write the intended effect into the score.
- Adler PDF 202 (Beethoven 6/i): the crescendo is built by stages and lands on a strong arrival. At 117 the arrival is the trombone motto ff over the G pedal with the Cl and Vla triplets starting. Returning to 126 there (a tempo) gives that arrival weight and keeps the triplets at their present speed.
- Critic 589 (Schumann 2/i) found the same gap in general terms: "his tempo transitions run on in the music. This score marks its changes at section boundaries." That remark led to no change because 13–15 is protected. 113–116 is not protected for tempo, and the stringendo stays inside the development.

**Fix** (global.ily, the development line before mark G):
```
s1 s1 \break \tempo \markup \medium \italic "poco a poco stringendo" \mt 129 s1 \mt 132 s1 \mt 135 s1 \mt 138 s1
\mark \default \tempo "a tempo" \mt 126
```
The stringendo starts with the brass entry at 113, so the two pushes coincide. It reaches 138 (about 10% faster) at 116 and returns to 126 at 117, before the existing allargando 121–122, sostenuto 123–126 and a tempo 127. No note, dynamic or existing metronome value changes. The 121–127 tempo chain (cycle 78 do-not-touch) is unchanged; 127 becomes the second "a tempo" of the development, which is normal usage.

### 2. Comparison with Sibelius 5/i: the rest stands; no other change

I compared the movements where they do comparable work.

- **Opening.** Sibelius opens with horns p sempre over a timpani roll and woodwind answers poco f; the strings are silent for the opening page and enter only later (PDF 7–8, 12). Our 1–8 is also a low, bare opening over a timpani ppp roll (B.cl, Vc, Cb on the motto, C.a. answering), with the strings held to pp chords from 6. The device matches Sibelius's in function. Our version is tied to the motto and the coda, so I keep it.
- **String backgrounds.** Sibelius sets wind motifs over long string semiquaver figures, pp "cresc. un pochett." (PDF 17–19, mm. 47–56), and the bassoon "lugubre ... patetico" over ppp sempre string figuration (PDF 25–27, mm. 74–90). That is Adler's soft background against a contrasting solo colour (PDF 217, 239). Our nearest place is 107–110 (B.cl and violas p espr. over Vln II div. pp). It is four bars long, not twenty. A long soft development episode has been raised before (c459, c559, c569) and kept for proportions (section lengths, c215, c329, c493). I do not reopen it.
- **String continuity.** From full.midi, the cellos play 176 of 182 bars, the double basses 173 and the violas 164. Sibelius drops whole choirs for pages. Adler PDF 213 praises the dropout of continuously playing strings. Our dropouts at 34–36 and 103–106 do that job at the two places where the texture changes. Keeping the cellos as the bass elsewhere is normal Brahms–Tchaikovsky practice. No change.
- **Ending.** Sibelius ends Più Presto, ff tutti, with short final chords (PDF 66–70). Our movement ends ppp and attacca because the forte close belongs to the finale ("Do not reuse" list; c339, c379, c439). The difference is planned.
- **Where the distance remains.** Sibelius grows the whole movement from a few cells by changes of tempo and texture. This movement is a classical sonata form with sectional tempo marks. Finding 1 removes the one sectional join where that difference is clearly a weakness, without touching proportions. A conductor would accept the rest as the work of a skilled late-Romantic symphonist in the Tchaikovsky–Dvořák line, which is the commission's model, not Sibelius.
