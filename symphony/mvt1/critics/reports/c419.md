## Findings

### 1. Timpani roll 1–4 stops at full ppp under a fading motto (applied)

**Bars:** intro 1–5, timpani.

**What is wrong.** The movement opens with a C3 roll, ppp, under the bass clarinet and cellos' motto. The roll is held at the same level through bar 4 and then cuts off at the barline. c328 had the bass clarinet and cellos fade to nothing on the held C3 at 4, handing the tonic to the basses' C2. The timpani do not follow them. So at 5.1, while every other part has faded, a roll on the same pitch stops at its full level, and the end of the opening is marked by a cut-off instead of a dissolve. This is the only roll in the movement that neither fades nor ends on a stroke. The others end on a tied stroke (c166: 13–14, 115–116) or are taken over by another sound (174 into the tam-tam at 175).

**Evidence.** Casanelles describes a sound by volume, frequency and time, and treats "volume over time" as one of its basic dimensions (PDF 273). Timbre is "a dynamic process", the appearance and disappearance of partials over a note's life (PDF 276). Synthesis models that life with an envelope that ends in a release (PDF 279). A timpani roll held flat and then stopped has no release. Adler requires the timpani part to show "dynamics and the exact duration of each note; for instance, where a roll or trill is to end" (adler2 PDF 80). Rimsky-Korsakov asks for a diminuendo by having the instruments drop out one after another, and notes that clarinets "decrease their tone to a breath" (rimsky-principles PDF 130). c328 already applied that to the bass clarinet. Casanelles PDF 275–276 is the reason not to use the c166 tied-stroke ending here. A stroke is an attack transient, an audible inharmonic event, and at 5.1 it would sound under the basses alone in the Lento.

**Master comparison.** Coriolan's last bars (corpus PDF p. 39) let every sustained line die away before the three pizzicato Cs. No sound is cut at full level. Sibelius 1/i also opens with a solo woodwind over a soft timpani roll, the same scoring idea, and that roll does not simply stop either. I give that as general knowledge only, not as a checked score fact.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-419/work/sections/intro.ily):**
```

  c1:32\ppp~^\markup ... | c1:32~ | c1:32~ | c1:32\> |
  % c419: comment
  R1\! | R1*5 | aes,1:32\pp\< | ...
```
No pitches, attacks or retunings change (c276's timpani check stands). The hairpin ends on the bar-5 rest, as on the Hn 2 rest at 45, which parts-lib.ily already handles.

### 3. Holistic judgment against Coriolan (no change)

I checked the score against the master on the points where the two pieces share an aim.
- **Stop chords.** Coriolan states its tonic by unison Cs and isolated tutti stabs separated by silence. The stop chords at 26, 138 and 170 do the same job, and their layouts are quoted by the finale.
- **Second key.** Coriolan's second theme starts in E♭ (III) and soon moves on. This score's S in A♭ (VI), on horn and cellos over the harp (47–55), is a richer colour than Beethoven's and stays within the period's practice.
- **Ending.** Coriolan's coda breaks the main theme into fragments over a pp texture. The coda here (171–182) breaks up the motto in the same way, and the attacca into movement II replaces Beethoven's final pizzicati.

A conductor would see the spacing (reading note 5), the percussion economy (notes 3 and 7) and the written balance (note 1) as professional. The remaining distance from a master work lies in points that are protected and whose reasons I do not answer: the compact proportions (c215, c243, c283) and the reserves kept for the finale. I do not reopen them.
