## Findings

### 1. The development's new idea is bound into the movement by derivation, not by recombination. Keep.

Mendelssohn's development brings in a new theme, treats it as a fugato in the strings and later combines it with the first theme, so the new idea becomes part of the movement's argument (Halpern: a third theme "treated fugally"). This movement's fugato (85–96, entries D in the cellos, G in the violas, C in Vln II and the cor anglais, F in Vln I) does the same work by a different means. Its subject (`d8 bes4. a8 aes8 g8 | g4 fis8 g8 es4. d8`) is the motto's chromatic fall, already part of the movement's material, so the coda does not need to recall it separately. The two theme heads are then combined at 103–110, as Mendelssohn combines his. A&S PDF 399–400 explain why the entry plan works: the entries follow descending fifths with canonic imitation, the procedure of Mozart K. 491 (Ex. 24-1, 24-4). A conductor who knows the "Italian" would find the same logic here. No change.

### 2. A pulse as the unifying engine. Keep.

Mendelssohn's movement is held together by one rhythm, the repeated woodwind quavers under the violin tune. This movement has a comparable rhythmic thread that comes back at the main joins: the first group's bass `c4 r8 g,8` (15–22), the hammered timpani and trumpets at 27–30 and 131, the cellos' accelerating G pulse at 127–130 (crotchets, quavers, triplets, semiquavers), and the timpani pulse at 179–180, which the scherzo and finale quote. A&S PDF 346 (Unit 21, the "Jupiter" finale, Ex. 21-1) makes the point that rhythmic displacement and figuration "animate and individualize" a line. Here the pulse animates the form. The differences from Mendelssohn (seven tempo easings, a slow introduction) were weighed by c409 and are protected tempo and proportion decisions. No change.

### 3. Major against minor, the Mendelssohn way. Keep.

The reading's own Mendelssohn example (Ex. 23-1, PDF 386–387) shows minor running through a major-key movement, with the major returning ff only "much later". The "Italian" uses the same contrast across the whole symphony: an A-major first movement with minor-mode development regions, and a finale in A minor. This movement inverts the plan for a C-minor work. Major appears softly at 8 (the Picardy chord), in the C-major second group of the recapitulation (147–166) shaded by A♭ (♭VI fff at 160), and at the ppp chord at 182, whose violas' E♮ hands the key to movement II. A&S PDF 532 note that mixture is "more of a special device in major than in minor". The score follows that: A♭ in C major is used as a climax device, not as a constant colour. The scan found no ♭6 replaced by ♮6 before 5 (Ex. 23-2h). The only candidate, Vln I at 162.4–163.1, is a G♯5 lower neighbour to A5 over vi. No change.

### 4. Retransition: I considered a held high wind pedal at 127–130 and rejected it. Not applied.

Mendelssohn's approach to the recapitulation is a long soft build. Here 123–126 collapse from the fff at 122, and 127–130 build from the bottom up: Cl 1 motto p at 127, cellos' accelerating G, violas mf at 129, Bsn 1 and Vln II f at 130, timpani pp to f, then ff at 131. The register above C5 is empty from 127 to 130. I considered having Ob 1 hold G5 from 126 through 130 as an inverted dominant pedal, pp crescendo to f. I rejected it for three reasons. (a) The passage's design is entries rising from low to high, and Vln II's arrival at 130 is its top. A held G5 above would take away that arrival. (b) A&S's model of a long soft dominant before a ff tonic (Beethoven Op. 31/1, Ex. 27-3, PDF 464) keeps the dominant in the middle register with rests between strokes, which is what 127–130 already does. (c) 127–130 is protected (c263: no leading tone before 131; c479 and c467: no soft trumpets, retransition do-not-touch), and the idea does not answer those reasons. No change.

- c502 at 157: the violas' arpeggio is F3–A♭3–C4–F4 (it was A♭3–C4–F4–A♭4).
- c508 at 163: Hn 2 and Hn 4 have mf> on their restruck notes.

None changes a pitch relationship that the reading condemns. The 157 change removes a struck resolution note under a passing dissonance (A&S PDF 469–470, outside this reading but consistent with Unit 21's metric rule, PDF 347). The all-pairs voice-leading lists of v499 and v509 are byte-identical. The 163 marking writes out the brass decay and does not raise the level above the 162 diminuendo. Page count, warnings and outer-voice parallels are unchanged.

### 6. Where Mendelssohn is still ahead, and why it stays. Keep.

The "Italian" has a lightness and unbroken momentum that this movement does not try for: thin woodwind textures under one string tune, and few tempo changes. This movement's character is Romantic and tragic (models Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Dvořák, Elgar), and its weight, its three A♭ climaxes and its soft attacca ending are structural, quoted by other movements, and protected. Making 15–22 or 47–55 lighter in Mendelssohn's manner would change themes and colours that coherence/mvt1.md records as cross-movement material. I do not recommend it.
