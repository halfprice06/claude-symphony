## Overall verdict

Measured against Beethoven 5/i, this movement would pass a conductor's reading as the work of a skilled late-Romantic professional. It does not have Beethoven's single-cell saturation and drive, but it is not trying to: its models are Brahms and Tchaikovsky, and its movement-end (a soft coda attacca into an Adagio) rules out Beethoven's coda. Measured against the assigned reading, the doublings and distributions are almost all textbook Rimsky-Korsakov. I found nothing in v239 that is worse than v229. I recommend one small change (Finding 4, optional in weight but recommended) and keeping everything else.

## Findings

### 1. Development proportion and drive compared with Beethoven 77–130 (keep)
Beethoven's development (bars 125–247) is one continuous argument on the four-note cell. It rises through imitation, liquidates the S-horn-call figure to a single half-note chord tossed between winds and strings (196–227, diminuendo to pp), and then breaks out ff. Our development is 54 bars in six blocks: dialogue 77–83, fugato 85–96, S in B minor 97–102, combined heads 103–110, wedge 111–116, pedal 117–130. That is more sectional than Beethoven, and the earlier open item ("development opening 77–84 stop-start") is the visible result. Two things answer it. The blocks are joined by a stepwise key rise (D–E–F–G, 103–117, protected) and by Rimsky's PDF 81 rule: each change of texture coincides with a new idea, which is how Tchaikovsky and Dvořák build developments. And the 77–83 exchanges, short-breathed as they are, use the key cycle A♭–E–C (protected, cross-movement seed). Recommendation: keep. Not applied.

### 2. Liquidation at 123–126 (keep)
Beethoven's wind/string alternation at 196–227 is the classic case of Rimsky's "chords in different groups alternately" (Ex. 115, PDF 289: full wind f dim. answered by brass p). Our collapse after the fff at 122 thins instead of alternating: Fl 1/Ob 1/Vln I take G5 at 124, Ob 1/Vln I the descent at 125–126, over Cl/Vla/Vc/Hn 3·4 holding the dominant. A Beethoven-style alternation would need four new bars, and the passage sits inside the protected chain 117–130 (Fl 1's octave drop at 124, sostenuto, tam-tam fade). The present thinning already gives the sense of exhaustion that Beethoven gets by alternation, and it is 4 bars against his 32, which suits a 182-bar movement. Keep. Not applied.

### 3. Recapitulation solo and horn lead-in 143–147 (follows the master)
Beethoven halts the recapitulation with the oboe's Adagio (bar 268, `oboi.ly` line 54), and in the recapitulated S he has to give the horn call to the bassoons (303). Ours does both things better than the model. After the ff stop at 143, Bsn 1 has the head (144), Ob 1 enters dolce p with poco rit. (145–146), and Hn 1 leads into J dolce mp at 146, then plays the S head with the violas at 147–150. This is Rimsky's Va + Hn pairing (PDF 79, Ex. 92 PDF 269) and the "genial and poetic" horn in piano (PDF 72, Ex. 73 PDF 245). Keep.

### 4. 124–125: partial divisi in a sustained p chord (recommended; not applied)
Rimsky PDF 88 says that in sustained chords "the method of dividing strings ... should be avoided ... as certain parts of the chord will be divided and others will not". He gives full divisi as the balanced alternative (Ex. 100 PDF 278, Ex. 101 PDF 279–280) and double stops as the normal way to fill the middle. At 124 Vln II is divided (D4/F4) and Vc divided (G3/B3), while Vln I (G5), violas (B3) and basses are not. At p, after the fff, each half-section gives half the tone, so the D4/F4 and G3/B3 thirds are weaker than the single G5 and B3 they support. The fix is double stops, `\once` with "non div." text: Vln II `<d' f'>1` (D4 on the G string, F4 on the D string, first position, playable p) and Vc `<g b>1` (G3 on the D string, B3 on the A string). This would reverse the c196 decision ("Vln II div./unis. at 73–74 and 124–125"), which was made for playability. I do not apply it, for two reasons: c196's reason (a p sustained double stop on two adjacent strings is less secure than a divisi for a section) is a fair answer to Rimsky, and the difference is small at this dynamic with Cl 1·2, Hn 3·4 and Bsn 1 doubling the middle. I record the Rimsky point so a later playability critic can weigh it. Keep as is.

### 5. 35: bare fourth in the clarinet pair (keep)
Rimsky PDF 90 rule 4 gives concords, not fourths or fifths, to two instruments of the same kind, and PDF 97 asks a wind harmony above a string bass to avoid open fourths and fifths. At 35 Cl 1·2 hold B♭4/F4 p over the B♭ bass, with Bsn 1 F3→A♭3. Filling in the third would mean D♮4 or D♭4, and the strings' imitation in this bar has both (D♭5 Vln I on beat 2, D♭4 then D♮4 in the violas on beats 3–4). Either third in the clarinets would clash with one of them. The open fourth is the right choice here: it is Rimsky's own exception ("except for certain colour effects", PDF 90 rule 3). Keep.

### 6. Horn call register 41–42 (keep)
Rimsky PDF 71 puts fanfares in the "full, clear, ringing notes of the middle and upper register". Beethoven's call (59–62) starts on concert B♭4, two horns in unison ff (written g'' in E♭). Ours is four horns in unison on E♭3–C4–B♭3 ff, low-middle register. The low octave is justified: it rhymes with Hn 1's E♭3–C4–B3 at 13–14 (cross-movement material), four horns in unison give Rimsky's "extreme power" (PDF 73, Ex. 76 PDF 247), and Hn 1 at once echoes the call an octave higher at p (E♭4–C5–B♭4, 43–44). That gives the ringing register, softly, which Beethoven does not have. Keep (protected; no change proposed).

### 7. Coda proportion (keep)
Beethoven's coda (374–502) is 26% of the movement and works as a second development. Ours (167–182) is 9%: a forte head over C with the stop chord (167–170), then a soft coda. A Beethoven-weight coda would contradict the attacca into the A♭ Adagio and would use up what the finale needs (the "Do not reuse" list: brass chorale in major, the C-major arrival). Keep.

### 8. v229 → v239: nothing got worse
I checked every change since v229: Vln II an octave below Vln I at 158–161; Cb unison at 6–7; Cl 1 G♯5 at 78; cellos on the B-minor S at 97–102; tied cadential 4–3 at 66/164 with harp and Hn 3 at 165; brass voicing at 40, 72 and 166; beam slopes; Cb "change bow at will" at 3; violas' free voice at 94–95; portato on the P head's repeated C and the bowing marks.
- The vl.txt diff shows one new pair: Hn 4 B3→B♭3 against Vc B4→B♭4 at 101.3. That is an octave doubling of the S tune's inner line, not a part-writing fault.
- The removed lines (Cb low-octave pairs at 104–105, 114–117, 138–139) come from the double basses' single line.
- Density at 97–104 rises by 8 notes per four bars because the cellos now carry the tune, and the bass still has Cb, Cbsn, B.cl, Bsn 2 and B.tbn. Rimsky PDF 78 (only one of two octave parts doubled) and PDF 76 (corresponding registers) support the change.
- The 158–161 octave split keeps A♭6 in Vln I + Fl 1·2 and puts Vln II with Ob 1 an octave lower. This is Ex. 90's layout (PDF 265–267), and it removes the c184/c191 objection to Vln II on A♭6.
