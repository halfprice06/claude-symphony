## Findings

None of the reading's principles calls for a change in v287. The texture already applies nearly every rule in the chapter, and several of them by name in earlier cycles (the flute a level under the violins at 64, c167; the violas resting at 56–59, c277; the solo cello at 171, c269). Below are the five places where the reading comes closest to asking for something else, ranked by weight, with the reason each should stay. I made no change in the scratch copy.

### 1. 117–121 and 131–134: accompaniment inside the register of the brass line (keep)

What the reading says: when the brass come to the foreground, Sebesky drops the string chords for a sustained high G in the violins and rests the cellos "in order to avoid clutter" while the trombones play (PDF 173, Ex. 216 on PDF 171–172).

The score: at 131–134 the violins do what Ex. 216 shows: a tied high counter E♭6–D♭6–C6–B5 above the low P theme. The violas' off-beat dyads E♭4/G4 and D4/F4, Tpt 3 and Hn 3·4 sit inside the theme's range G3–F4. At 117–121 the violas and Cl 2 play triplet arpeggios B3–F4 inside the trombone and horn motto (G3–E♭4–D4–D♭4–C4).

Why it stays: Sebesky's reason is rhythmic. The trombones play "loose jazz phrasing," and a second moving part in the same register blurs it. Here the motto at 117–121 moves in whole notes, held by four horns, two trombones and the bass trombone at ff, so a triplet figure under long notes does not blur them. The low theme at 131–134 has seven players in unison (Cl 1·2, Bsn 1·2, Tbn 1·2, Vc). c264 already brought the violas and Hn 3·4 down to f under it (Adler2 PDF 159). Both passages are protected (117–122, 131–134), with no stated reason that the reading answers.

### 2. 122: strings in the middle register under the fff brass (keep)

What the reading says: in a large mixed tutti the strings are the weakest element and should be voiced simply in the high register (PDF 168; the final chord of Ex. 216, PDF 172–173).

The score: Vln I's A♭6 and Vln II's D5/A♭5 follow this. The violas' tremolo B3/D4 and the cellos' A♭3 lie under Hn 3, Tpt 3 (B3) and Tbn 1 (A♭3, the cellos' note), and at fff they will not be heard as pitches.

Why it stays: the inner strings matter two bars later. From 124 the brass fade, and the cellos' A♭3–G3 and their divided G/B–G/C carry the collapse to 126 with Hn 3 and Cl 2 (C4–B3). Moving them up at 122 would break that line. The climax scoring at 122 is protected (c264, c284).

### 3. 15–18: the first theme on Vln I alone (keep)

What the reading says: "Whenever a particularly strong ensemble sound is necessary, especially when the strings are playing a concerted melodic passage, the lead violin line should be reinforced an octave lower" (PDF 140, rule 6; Ex. 202 on PDF 151).

The score: P enters f in Vln I alone over syncopations in Vln II and violas (mf since c278). Vln II doubles only the peak D♭6 and its descent an octave lower at 20–21 (c121).

Why it stays: the rule applies when the strongest sound is wanted. Here the sound is graded on purpose: one line at f at 15, the octave added at the peak at 20, then three string octaves and the winds at the ff restatement at 27, which is exactly Sebesky's octave-reinforced climax. If the octave came at 15, the jump at 27 would be smaller. The notes of 15–22 are protected.

### 4. 109–110: Fl 1 mp in unison with the violas at p (keep; settled)

What the reading says: a flute on a string line thickens it; Sebesky marks the flute a level softer than the strings (PDF 164). One woodwind weighs about as much as sixteen violins.

The score: Fl 1 joins the violas' S head at 109 at mp, a level above the violas' p. At 64 the score does what the book says (Fl 1 mf under Vln I f).

Why it stays: HISTORY records this point as settled after two changes (c167 pp, c178 mp "on register grounds") and says not to change it again. I agree with c178 on the reading's own terms. Sebesky's rule is about a flute on the lead violin line at mf or louder. Here the flute is on D♭5–A♭5, its weak lower-middle register, doubling a viola section of about a dozen players. The only remaining alternative named in HISTORY, a rest at 109–110, would lose the colour change that c241 kept.

### 5. Cellos in octaves with the double basses (keep)

What the reading says: do not double the bass with the lowest cello by habit. Some distance between the lowest cello and the bass lets the strings float, and a bottom-rich sound is fresher when used sparingly (PDF 140 rule 7; PDF 145–146 on Ex. 194: the bottom cello takes the root only for temporary richness, never just to reinforce the bass).

The score: in full.midi the lowest cello note is an octave or unison with the double basses for at least three-quarters of the bar in 65 of the 166 bars where both sound. Almost all of these bars are pedals (73–76, 117–130, 175–182), tutti strokes and hammered bars (23–26, 32–33, 69–72, 82–84, 135–138, 143) or the motto in the bass (1–4). Everywhere else in the soft music the cellos have their own line: arpeggios or the tune at 47–68, the fugato and free voices at 85–110, the inner line of the wedge at 112–116, the counter at 139–142 and 167–169, the tune at 147–161. The two short soft exceptions, 13–14 (G3 over G2 under the horn call) and 35–36 (B♭2/E♭3 under the imitations), are protected passages and last two bars each. This is the "sparingly" the reading asks for.
