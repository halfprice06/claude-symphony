## Findings

Under this reading, no passage of v313 stands between the score and a master's work, so I made no edit. Cycle 223 reached the same verdict on v223. The ninety versions since then changed no key boundary, section length or cadence type. The findings below are the places where the reading asks a question that cycle 223 did not ask. Each gives the answer and the reason to keep the passage. They are ranked by how much a change there would matter.

### 1. The retransition prolongs V and does not tonicize it, 117–130: keep

Unit 14 offers two ways of handling the dominant before a return. The dominant can become a key of its own and then be turned back by its 7th (PDF 219–220 §10). Or a middle phrase can "prolong (but not tonicize) V" (PDF 224, Ex. 14-12). Bars 117–130 take the second way. Over the fourteen-bar G pedal the chords alternate V♭9 (117, 122), i6/4 (118, 121, 125) and V7 (119, 123–124), with the motto's D♭ colour at 120. No chord applied to V sounds over the pedal: no F♯ and no A♮ in any part between 117 and 130. G is never heard as a tonic, and the 7th F is present in every bar except 118 and 125–129, so the pull toward C stays intact (PDF 219–220).

The corpus models do it differently. In Tchaikovsky 6/i the F♯ (V) region alternates V6/5 of V with V for some forty quarters (MCTL harmony.csv, onsets 842–882), which is the reading's ♯4̂ emphasis of V (PDF 215). Beethoven 9/i makes the minor dominant a real key in the development (A minor, onsets 483.5–560), moves to F, and returns through vii°7 (588–600) to the D-major tonic at 600. The score's model is the Brahms 1/i type, a long dominant pedal with tonic 6/4s over it. Unit 14 accepts it: a prolonged V does not need ♯4̂. Adding V/V chords over the pedal would replace the motto's chords at 119–121 and touch the protected chain 117–130 (c239, c249, c303). Keep.

### 2. The arrival in A♭ follows the reading step by step, 39–47: keep

This passage is the clearest textbook case in the movement, and cycle 223 did not describe it.
- 39–40: B♭ pedal with D♮ (♯4̂ of A♭) and A♭. This is V7 of E♭, the chord applied to the new key's dominant (PDF 214 §5).
- 41: E♭ major sf, held at 41–42 and tonicized as a passing key. It has its own dominant, a break in the flow and a new motive (the four-horn call; PDF 216 §6).
- 43: D♭ joins the E♭ chord (Cl 1·2, Vln II, Cb E♭1 below). The 7th turns the temporary tonic back into a dominant, V7 of A♭, as PDF 219–220 §10 recommends. D♭ is the one degree of A♭ that E♭ major lacks.
- 44–45: V4/2 (bass D♭) to I6 (bass C) of A♭. This is exactly Schubert's entry into D in Ex. 14-1, bars 6–7 (PDF 211; the analysis "V4/2 … I6" printed under the example), and PDF 219 §9 names this route.
- 46–47: ii6/5 – V7 – I with the S head. The new key is confirmed by its own dominant before the theme starts (PDF 216 §6).

The S group then cadences IAC (55), DC (68) and PAC (72), the order of Ex. 14-1, bars 9–10 (PDF 219 §9). Keep 39–47 (also c253, c281, c303).

### 3. The development begins on the dominant as a key, 84–87: keep

Bar 84 is a bare G at sub. p. Heard from the C-minor chord at 80–83 it is V of C. Heard from 85, where the cello subject on D outlines G minor (85–87: D–B♭, G–A♭–A–B♭), it is i of G minor. This is the pivot V = I of the reading's Exercise 2c (PDF 228), and the "double meaning" of the last chord of an area (PDF 219 §10). The movement's only key area on V is therefore at the start of the development. In a major-key sonata, the place of the dominant key is the end of the exposition (PDF 225–226). From there the fugato moves flatward by fifths (G–C–F–B♭ minor, 85–96), and the dominant does not return until the pedal at 117. So the reading's "V as a key area" has a real role in this C-minor form: it opens the departure, and a non-tonicized pedal closes it. Keep (fugato protected, c237, c257).

### 4. The recapitulation's dominant before S has no pre-dominant, 143–146: keep

In the exposition, the dominant that precedes S is reached through V/V (39–41), the strongest semicadence (PDF 215, Ex. 14-4). In the recapitulation, 143 is i (the ff stroke), 144 is i6/4 over G (Bsn 1's answer), and 145–146 are V7 at pp with no pre-dominant and no F♯. PDF 215 says a V reached this way "wants to continue moving; it doesn't sound nearly as much like a cadential goal".

That is right here. The pre-dominant work was done in the cadence 139–143 (iv – N – iiø7 – V – i), so 143–146 is a post-cadential link, not a second medial caesura. The V at 145 has to run on into the mode change at 147, and Ob 1's G4–E♮5 at 145 announces C major inside the V. I tried an F♯ (vii°7/V) on beat 3 of 144 on paper. It would make 145 a goal, repeat the effect of 41, slow the shortened recapitulation, and alter the bars protected by c239, c261 and c282. Keep.

### 5. Census of ♯4̂ in chords applied to V: no new violation

PDF 214 says ♯4̂ "must not be doubled". Every sounding ♯4̂ in v313:
- 12: F♯3 (Hn 3·4) and F♯4 (Ob 2, Vln II) on the German sixth. This doubling is protected: it is the seed of the finale's first chord (c222, c284).
- 37–38: the F♯ is G♭, the third of E♭ minor, not a leading tone.
- 70: D♮ only in the bass octave, D2–D3 (Cb, Cbsn, Bsn 2, Vc, B.cl). This is orchestral doubling of the bass line, and the line rises D–E♭ in every octave.
- 101–102: F♯ is the root of V of B minor, not ♯4̂.
- 104–106 (A♯ in E minor) and 108–110 (B♮ in F minor): single or bass-octave only.
- 116: F♯3 and F♯4. Treated by c223 (finding 4) and protected.

The C-major group 147–166 contains no chord applied to V at all. Its dominants at 150, 154, 158–159 and 165 are reached from ii or iv. This is consistent with the movement's plan of soft, withheld C major (c240, c293: no forte C-major confirmation before the finale), so I recommend no V/V there.

These are unchanged from v223, within one second.
- A♭ takes 63 of the exposition's 124 s (0.51). Beethoven 9/i gives its second key B♭ 177 of 318 quarters (0.56; MCTL onsets 141–318).
- The three A♭ climaxes fall at 200 s (82), 277 s (122) and 355 s (160), at even intervals of 77 and 78 s.
- The tonic is absent from 101 s to 296 s, except for C minor at 80–81 and the C entry of the fugato at 91. That gives the strong departure and return PDF 220 §11 asks for.

By MCTL quarter counts the coda is shorter in the models: Beethoven 9/i 68.5 of 1092.5 quarters (onsets 1024–1092.5, 6%), Tchaikovsky 6/i 80 of 1414 (onsets 1334–1414, 6%). The score's coda is longer in proportion because its Lento epilogue (175–182, 38 s) mirrors the 14-bar Lento introduction and leads attacca into movement II. PDF 220 §11 calls the coda of Ex. 14-1 a "varied repetition of the final cadence". Bars 171–182 repeat the cadence twice, i–iv–N6–6/4–V at 171–174, then the chorale over the motto bass, which is that function. The coda length is protected (c239, c243). Keep.
