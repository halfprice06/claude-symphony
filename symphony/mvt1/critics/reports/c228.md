## Findings

### F1. Tam-tam at 122–126 rings at full strength into the p arrival (dev). Applied.

**What is wrong.** At 122 the tam-tam is struck fff with a heavy beater and written as a tied semibreve through 126, then damped at 127 (c166). The only dynamic is the fff. Meanwhile the orchestra falls away. The trumpets leave after 123. The trombones go > at 124 and stop at 125. The flutes stop at 125. The timpani go > from 124 to pp at 126. At 126 Vln I, Vln II, Ob 1 and the violas reach p, and the tuba, Cbsn, B.cl and Cb reach pp. A fff stroke with a heavy beater swells for several seconds and is still loud ten seconds later. At sostenuto, crotchet = 100, 122–126 lasts about 12 s. The fall in Vln I and Ob 1 (G5–F5, E♭5–D5–C5, D5 at p) is the end of the climax, and it sounds under an undamped fff gong.

**Evidence.** Rimsky PDF 135: "the bass drum, cymbals, gong ... played fortissimo, is sufficient to overpower any orchestral tutti". Here it is overpowering a diminuendo. Rimsky PDF 130: a diminuendo is made by taking instruments away, brass first. Every other group in 122–126 follows that order and the timpani carry their own > to pp, but the tam-tam has no shading. Rimsky PDF 131: in a converging progression the doubling parts thin out. Support: Tchaikovsky 6/iv (corpus `musicxml-converted/ly/Tchaikovsky_Sym6_mvt4.ly`, tam-tam 137–142) marks its long tam-tam note p, ends it on a written release, and does not leave a loud stroke under soft music. Adler (adler2 PDF 72–73, cited by c166) also says to show how long the sound lasts. This finding keeps c166's length and adds how the sound gets there.

**Fix.** Keep the stroke, the beater, the written length and the damp at 127, and add a gradual damping that reaches p with the strings at 126 (dev.ily, devPerc):
```
tt1\fff~^\markup { "1: Tam-tam" \italic "heavy beater" } | tt1~ | tt1~\>_\markup \small \italic "damp gradually" | tt1~ | tt1\p | R1^\markup \small \italic "damp" | R1*3 |
```
Players damp a tam-tam gradually with the hand or a cloth. It is a normal instruction. This keeps the tam-tam the only percussion at 122 (protected) and leaves its placement unchanged.

### F2. Held brass have no crescendo into the arrivals at 41 and 122 (trans, dev). Applied.

**What is wrong.**
- **Bar 121 (allargando into the fff at 122).** Flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, strings, tuba and timpani all have a hairpin into 122. Horns 1–4, trombones 1·2 and the bass trombone hold their notes at ff from 117 with no hairpin, then jump to fff at 122. Hn 1·2 hold C4 tied from 120. Hn 3·4 hold G4/G3. Tbn 1·2 and B.tbn hold C4/C3 up to the eighth-note breath at the end of 121.
- **Bar 40 (into the sf at 41).** Fl 1, Ob 1, Vln I (the scale runs) and the timpani roll have a crescendo into 41. Hn 3·4, Tbn 1·2 and B.tbn hold a flat f for the whole bar, then strike sf (trombones) or ff (Hn 3·4) at 41.

At both points, the group that should lead the crescendo is the one group without it.

**Evidence.** Rimsky PDF 130: "After the strings, the brass is the group most facile in producing dynamic shades, glorifying crescendo chords into the most brilliant sforzando climaxes." Ex. 255 (PDF 419) shows exactly this: the horns' held chord goes p< to sf at the end of the wind crescendo. In Ex. 256 (PDF 419) the horns carry "cresc. poco" with the wind. Rimsky PDF 130 also says a long crescendo is completed by the brass. Here the brass are already playing, so their part in completing it has to come through their own dynamic. At 159→160 and 169→170 the score already does this (Tpt/Hn/Tbn `\<` into 160; Tbn 1, Tpt 3, Hn 3 `\<` into 170). 40 and 121 are the exceptions.

**Fix.** Add hairpins only. No notes, rhythms or breaths change.
- dev.ily, bar 121: Hn 1 and Hn 2 `c'1\<`; Hn 3 `g'1\<`; Hn 4 `g1\<`; Tbn 1 and Tbn 2 `c'2..\< r8\!`; B.tbn `c2..\< r8\!`. The trombone hairpins end on the breath, so c087's breath is kept, and the part-combined pair carry identical hairpins.
- trans.ily, bar 40: Hn 3 `d'1\f\<`; Hn 4 `f1\f\<`; Tbn 1 `bes1\f\<`; Tbn 2 `f1\f\<`; B.tbn `bes,1\f\<`.

The trumpets still enter at 122 without a crescendo (held back on purpose; Rimsky PDF 136 on a silent group's fresh return). The brass layout at 117–123 and the horn call at 41–42 (protected) are unchanged.

### F3. Cl 1's unresolved B4 at 177 is cut off at pp instead of fading (coda). Applied; reopens the coda dynamics, with the reason answered below.

**What is wrong.** At 175–177 Cl 1 plays E♭4–C5–B4 at pp, the horn call's falling semitone, with the B left unresolved. The B4 is a minim on beats 3–4 of 177 under "rit.". It stops at the barline with no shading. On beat 4 Vln I enter pp with the last motto (G4), so for one beat the clarinet's B and the violins' pickup sound at the same level.

**Evidence.** Rimsky PDF 130: "Clarinets specialise in diminuendo effects and are capable of decreasing their tone to a breath (morendo)." This is the clarinet's last note in the movement, and it is a note meant to stay unresolved: a fade suits it better than a release. The best-known example is in the corpus. Tchaikovsky 6/i, clarinet, bars 155–160 (`Tchaikovsky_Sym6_mvt1.ly`, clarinet part, ll. 1840–1851) goes p > ppp, pppp, > ppppp on a falling line before the double bar. The fade also makes room for Vln I's pickup at 177. That is Rimsky's handover principle from PDF 125–126, which c150 already used for the C.a. G at 9.

**Fix** (coda.ily, codaClOne):
```
r2. es'4(\pp | c''1~ | c''2 b'2\>) |
R1\! | R1*3 | R1\fermata |
```

**Protection.** Several earlier cycles in HISTORY.md list "coda 171–182 notes and dynamics" (also "coda 167–182 (notes, dynamics, fermatas, tempo)") and "Cl 1 at 175–177" as do-not-touch. The reason given is that the coda's notes and its pp/ppp levels are settled. This change keeps every note, the unresolved B, the pp level and the timing. It shapes only the release of one note, in the direction the coda already moves (violas "morendo", strings > ppp). If the composer reads the protection as covering hairpins too, this is the finding to drop. F1 and F2 do not depend on it.
