## Findings

### F1. 59 and 110: the head's appoggiatura is the only accented dissonance in the phrase without a mark, and its resolution comes out louder — APPLIED

**Problem.** The fourth bar of the second-theme head is always an appoggiatura or 4–3 over V followed by its resolution (A♭–G in A♭ major). Most statements mark the dissonance:

- 50, 150 and 174 put the peak of a swell on it;
- 106 starts the diminuendo on it;
- 101 has a tenuto (c148).

Two statements leave it unmarked and also place it inside a crescendo, so the resolution G is louder than the dissonance:

- At 59 (Vln I and II in unison, A♭5 over the E♭ bass), the crescendo runs from 58 to the *mf* at 60.
- At 110 (Fl 1 and violas, A♭5 as the 6 of the cadential 6/4 over C, with Vln II's 4–3 F–E below), the crescendo leads into 111.

**Evidence.**
- A&S PDF 54–55, Ex. 3-15c: the appoggiatura is an accented dissonance, and the resolution is the weaker note.
- A&S PDF 50: dissonant notes carry a built-in accent.
- A&S PDF 51, Ex. 3-6: when the texture does not supply the emphasis, the composer marks it.
- Corpus: Tchaikovsky 6/i, Vln I, bars 93–95 and 97–99, at *f*, gives every strong-beat melody note that falls by step onto a weaker note its own mark: tenuto on A5 (beat 3) and G5 (beat 3), accent on F♯5 and E5 (beat 1). The resolutions are unmarked. He keeps these marks inside a rising phrase, which is the case at 59 and 110.
- Web: Drake, Dowling & Palmer (1991) found that tunes are reproduced best when accent types coincide. Here the bar-4 dissonance is the melody's own accent, and it should not be cancelled by the dynamic.

This is c148's unapplied proposal for 59, now extended to 110. The composer's reason for not applying it was that 59 sits next to the protected head phrasing 47–58. A tenuto adds no hairpin and changes no note, slur or scoring, and it does not touch 47–58. It makes 59 match the protected statements at 50/150/174 and 101.

**Fix (applied).**
- tTwo.ily 59: Vln I `aes''2-- g''2)\stopTextSpan`; Vln II `aes''2-- g''2)\stopTextSpan`.
- dev.ily 110: Fl 1 `aes''2--\< g''4 c''4\!)`; Vla `aes''2--\< g''4 c''4)`.

Cycle 88's rule (doubling parts carry identical marks) is kept. The crescendi are unchanged. 108 already has the same tenuto on the viola's C6.

### F2. 140–141 against 160: the A♭6 before the climax carries more built-in accent than the A♭6 at the climax (NOT applied; for decision; new evidence for c184's open item)

**Problem.** Vln I's ceiling A♭6 by bar, measured from the source:

| Bar | Position | Duration | Dynamic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 | downbeat | 1.5 beats | ff |
| 72 | downbeat | 1 beat | f |
| 122 | downbeat | 4 beats | fff |
| 140–141 | from beat 3, tied over the bar line | 3.5 beats | ff |
| 160 | from beat 2 | 2.5 beats | fff |

The recapitulation's first group (140) therefore holds the ceiling a beat longer than the movement's last climax (160), 19 bars earlier. It also holds it as a syncopation across the bar line into the strong bar 141. A&S PDF 50 names length, height and dissonance as built-in accents, and PDF 51 says a note held from a weak beat through a strong one conflicts "sometimes very strongly" with the meter. So 140–141 combines three of the reading's accent types on the climax pitch. At 160 the A♭6 comes after a C6 on the downbeat, which drops a fifth below the G6 that ended 159, and lasts 2.5 beats.

**History.** Cycle 24 judged 160 to win "by weight", and cycle 61 set A♭6 at 31/140 deliberately. Cycle 184 raised the register argument (Adler PDF 129, 133). At v204 the composer declined it because it reverses the cycle 24/61 rule and touches protected bars. My new point is about duration and syncopation, not register or orchestral mass. Even with the same pitch, 140 is the longer and more strongly syncopated A♭6.

**Options (not applied, because protected).**
- (a) c184's tested fix: Vln I an octave lower at 139–142, in unison with Vln II, with Fl 1 keeping the A♭6. This keeps cycle 61's A♭6 at 140 in another colour.
- (b) If the violins must keep the pitch, shorten the hold: 140 `des'''8 es''' f''' g''' aes'''4.-> g'''8 | f'''4 ...`. This would rewrite 141's line, which is part of the protected Neapolitan setting 140–142, so I do not recommend it.

I recommend (a) or no change. I do not recommend lengthening the A♭6 at 160: that would remove the motto's C–A♭ minor sixth from the climax.

### F3. 121–122: the development climax falls on a hypermetrically weak bar by plain count (checked; no change)

Counting from G (117) and back from H (131), 122 is an even-numbered bar of a four-bar group (A&S PDF 53). In practice the ladder 119–120 halves the group length, and the allargando at 121, played as an upbeat bar (A&S PDF 52, groups that begin on an upbeat, applied at bar level), makes 122 heard as a downbeat. The movement's other main arrivals, 27/31, 131 and 158/160, fall on strong bars. No change, and 117–130 is protected.

### F4. Checked and consistent with the reading (no change)

- **Motto 1–4, 5–8 and 73–76.** Long notes fall on strong beats, the chromatic note (D♭) gets the swell, and the anticipations are unaccented (PDF 50–51, 56).
- **First theme 15–22.** The upbeat group with an accented beat-2 syncopation is a marked syncopation in the manner of Ex. 3-6. The same group three times, then the D♭6 peak tied into the strong bar 21, makes an eight-bar phrase that ends on the tonic at 22 (PDF 51–54). This rhythmic signature is what makes the head recognizable in its later statements (fugato 85, 103, 117–120, 143).
- **Second theme 47–55 and 56–68.** It begins on the downbeat with a pickup, unlike the first theme. Each bar of the head has a different rhythm, and the phrase is 8+1. The suspensions and the 54.1 cadence dissonance are placed on strong beats (PDF 52–55).
- **Climax 158–161.** E6 and A♭6 are syncopations on beat 2 in strong bars 158 and 160, consistent with the head's rhythm (PDF 51, 53).
- **Liquidation 143–146.** The head ends with a restruck anticipation into G (Ex. 3-20).
