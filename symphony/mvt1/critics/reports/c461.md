# Critic 461: Melody and memorability (v461), third pass of Adler ch. 7

Assigned reading: Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, ch. 7 "Individual Woodwinds" (adler1 PDF 184–232).

How I read it:
- I read every text page of PDF 184–232 in full.
- I rendered all 49 pages at 110 dpi and looked at every page as an image. This covers the register charts (Ex. VII-2, VII-21, VII-26, VII-32, VII-47, VII-56, VII-89) and every music example from VII-3 to VII-101. I took pitches, slurs and dynamics from the images, not from the OCR.
- Critics 151, 261 and 351 used this reading for the melody lens, and critics 298 and 398 used it for expression. Their accepted changes are in the score: Bsn 1's answer at 144 (c261), Fl 1's swell into A♭5 at 73–74 and the cor anglais slur at 153–155 (c398). I do not repeat their points.
- I read the v461 score (all 17 music pages; pages 6–8, 10, 14, 15 and 18 at 100–110 dpi) and the woodwind, horn and string melody variables in sections/*.ily.
- I scanned full.midi for every woodwind note in the registers the reading warns about (scripts in /tmp/critic-461).

Result: no change to the score. I checked each principle bar by bar and the score follows all of them. I then asked where a phrase could sing more. Every candidate I found is either already the best choice the reading allows or is protected, and none of the protections can be answered from this reading. Details follow.

## Findings

### 1. The four themes against the reading's model tunes (no change)

The reading's best-known melodies (Dvořák 9/i Ex. VII-3, Brahms 4/iv VII-4, Brahms 1/iv VII-9, Schumann 2/iii VII-34, Berlioz VII-48, Tristan VII-49, Tchaikovsky 5/i VII-59 and 6/i VII-93) share three things. Each has a short rhythmic cell that comes back. Each has one marked peak placed late. In slow tunes, each puts its long notes on strong beats with a swell. I checked each theme again for these three things, and also asked whether a listener would hum it.

- **Motto (1–8).** It is one cell (rising minor sixth, semitone fall, neighbour D♭) with a swell on each slur, answered a fifth higher in a new colour. That is the Tchaikovsky 5 and 6 pattern (PDF 211, 227). Every return has its own colour. It is the most memorable idea in the movement and needs nothing.
- **P (15–22).** Its cell is the syncopated accent after the pickup. The cell moves up by sequence at 17 and 19, and the sf D♭6 at 20 is the late peak, in bar 6 of 8, as in Ex. VII-34 and VII-48.
- **S (46.4–55).** It has the rising-sixth pickup E♭–C, a held C with a swell (the long-note type of Ex. VII-9), a second sixth at 51 (E♭–C5), and an octave leap to F5 at 52, where Cl 1 joins the cello. The clarinet arriving on the peak note in its clarino register is the reading's point that a woodwind gives the climax note its colour (PDF 184, 210). The violins' statement peaks later and higher (C6–D♭6 at 62–63).
- **Horn call (13–14).** E♭3–C4–B3 works as a motive, not a tune. The reading's alphorn model (Ex. VII-9) is a four-bar tune, but movement II's theme A fills this same span in the same horn (coherence/mvt1.md seeds), so the call stays as it is.

### 2. Bar 64–65: the flute's short doubling of the violins after the peak (no change)

This is the one place in the S group where a woodwind melody stops halfway through a phrase. Fl 1 enters mf on A♭5 with Vln I (f) at 64 and drops out on D♭5 at 65 while the violins go on (F5–A♭5, then B♭5 at 66). The reading's Brahms 1/iv dovetail (PDF 188) and the "transfer" rule could argue for the flute to carry on to 66, so I checked that.

I recommend no change. The flute adds edge on the A♭5 return, which lies in its "clear and brilliant" band (Ex. VII-2, PDF 185). It then leaves before the line falls into the B♭4–C♯5 band, where it has "little carrying power" and would only thicken the violins. That is exactly what PDF 185 tells the orchestrator to avoid. The exit is also protected (c441: "64–66 Fl 1 exit"). The phrase does not lose anything, because the violins carry the whole line.

### 3. The oboe still has no solo theme in movement I (no change; confirms c351)

PDF 198–199 calls the oboe "basically a lyrical instrument" with "bountiful solo use". In movement I, Ob 1 only doubles (23–25, 69–72, 135–137), answers for a beat or two (79, 80), and plays the dolce link at 145–146. I asked again whether a master's first movement would give it a phrase. Two reasons say no:
- Movement II gives Ob 1 its first solo, in dialogue with the solo violin at II 15–22. Holding the oboe back here keeps that colour fresh, as Dvořák 9 does with the cor anglais (Adler's list of English horn solos, PDF 206).
- The cor anglais already supplies the double-reed solo colour in movement I (5–8, 147–153, 171–174).

The places where an oboe phrase would fit (47–63, 147–155, 171–174) are all protected for reasons this reading does not answer.

### 4. The cor anglais never uses its rich lowest sixth (no change)

Ex. VII-47 and PDF 205 single out the cor anglais's lowest fifth or sixth (written B3–G4, sounding E3–C4). Its solos here lie a fifth to an octave higher: sounding D4–B♭4 at 5–8, G4–D5 at 147–153, G4–E♭5 at 171–174. I checked whether one statement could go lower.
- 5–8 must state the motto on D (a fifth above the B.cl's G2), and D3 is below the instrument.
- 147–153 answers the horn and violas in C major. An octave lower (G3–D4) would put it under Hn 1 and inside the violas' line, which covers it. PDF 185 and 226 say not to do that to an accompanied solo.
- 171–174 must start on the motto's G4 against the solo cello.

All three are in the "mellow, reedy, sonorous" band, which suits a p espressivo line. I recommend no change.

### 5. Soft woodwind melodies are never covered (no change)

I rechecked every soft woodwind melody against its accompaniment, bar by bar, against PDF 185, 186, 203 and 226:
- C.a. 5–9 (p over pp strings)
- Bsn 1 33–36 (f>mf over Cb fp, Vla mp, Vc p)
- Fl 1 73–76 (p espr. over pp)
- B.cl 77 and Cl 1 77.3–78 (mf/mp over p tremolo)
- B.cl 107–109 and Fl 1 109–110 (p/mp over pp/p)
- Cl 1 127–130 (low motto)
- Bsn 1 144 (strings mp>pp)
- Ob 1 145–146
- C.a. 147–153 in the gaps of the S
- C.a. 171–174
- Cl 1 175–177 (pp over pp trombones)

In each one the accompaniment is a level softer than the solo or lies below it. Nothing changed in these bars since c351, except bar-level notation and the MIDI expression (c428).

### 6. Register edges and peaks (no change)

The MIDI scan of full.midi gives these extremes:
- Flute: B♭4–A♭6.
- Oboe: low notes B3 and F4 only at f–ff; above C6 only at ff.
- Cor anglais: B3–F5 sounding. The B3 is at 99, ff, doubled.
- Clarinets: G3–C6 sounding.
- Bass clarinet: B♭1–G3 sounding. The low notes are labelled for the low-C instrument (c146).
- Bassoons: B♭1–F4. The lowest-fifth notes are p or louder.
- Contrabassoon: C1–F2.

Each peak lies inside the limits the reading sets (PDF 186, 198, 205, 217, 226, 230).

### Where the music is short of a master's, within this lens

I looked for a phrase that could sing more, a woodwind colour that could be more vivid, and a moment a listener would not remember. The closest candidates are the oboe solo (Finding 3) and a longer flute line at 64–66 (Finding 2). Both would add colour, but the reading itself, and the movement's role ahead of movement II, argue against them. The remaining distance from Dvořák or Tchaikovsky in this lens is in the length of the themes. S is nine bars and P eight, where Tchaikovsky 5/i and Dvořák 8/i spread their second themes over 16 bars or more. The proportion critics have protected that length repeatedly (c243, c283, c413), and Adler ch. 7 gives no grounds to reopen it.
