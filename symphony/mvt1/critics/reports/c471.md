## Findings (ranked)

None of the findings calls for a score change. They are ranked by how much they would matter if my verdict were wrong.

### 1. The themes: memorable; no new version is warranted (no change)

Sebesky's question 6 (PDF 220) asks whether a tune is weak in melody and how to make up for it; question 4 asks how to keep a known tune fresh. I asked both of each theme, with the lens's question: would a listener hum it afterwards?

- **Motto** (G–E♭, D–C–D♭–C, 1–4). Yes. It has two clear features, a rising minor sixth and a chromatic fall that turns back up a semitone. It returns in fixed colours (B.cl+Vc, C.a., flute, brass, Vln I), which is how Tchaikovsky makes the motto of his Fifth stick: one shape, changing colour. Question 4 is answered by the colour plan, not by changing notes.
- **P** (15–22). Yes, the head. The pickup, the accented dotted crotchet on the high E♭5, and the chromatic fall come back at 17, 27, 29, 69, 77–83, 103, 131 and 167. The continuation rises to one peak (D♭6 sf at 20) and falls by step to the cadence at 22. It is a sentence with a single high point, like the Allegro theme of Brahms 1/i.
- **S** (46.4–55). Yes, the head. It has the motto's rhythm (dotted minim tied to a quaver) and its rising sixth, and 13, 41 and 43 plant it before it is heard. Its real high points come later: the restatement reaches D♭6 at 63 and f at 64; the recapitulation climax 158–161 rises G5–E6 and then C6–A♭6, the motto's sixth at fff. The theme grows each time it returns. That helps memory more than a second hook would.
- **Horn call** (E♭3–C4–B3, 13–14). It is a signal, not a tune, and it is the S incipit with B♮. It does its job.

All four begin with a rising sixth. They differ in mode, tempo, rhythm and colour, so they sound like one family, not like copies. Rewriting P or S would also break the finale's quotations (I 15–16 at IV 247–248, I 27–32 at IV 199–204, I 146–150 at IV 247–250). The reading gives no reason that outweighs this.

### 2. "Carry one bar into the next": whole-orchestra stasis scan (PDF 207, PDF 212; no change)

Sebesky asks for "something going which is interesting to the ear... enough character to carry one bar into the next" (PDF 207), and says when to move depends on "tempo, rhythmic complexity, and harmonic motion" (PDF 212). c361 checked only holds inside fourteen tune statements. I listed every span of two beats or more in which no part of the orchestra starts a note, from 15 to 170 (stasis.py). There are 33 such spans. Each one is one of these:

- Stop chords and silences: 26, 138, 170 (protected), 32.2–4, 83.1–84 (strokes, then the rest before the fugato).
- Held climax or collapse chords: 41.1–4 (sf, horn call pickup on 41.4), 122–124.4½ (fff, tremolo in MIDI as held notes), 125.3–127.1.
- Cadences and phrase ends: 50.3, 72.3, 110.1–3 (cadential 6/4), 150, 155, 166.3.
- The link 42.3–46 under "poco rit." (half-note motion: Hn 1 echo, then Cl 1's descending line, Ex. 281 note C).
- The flute motto 73–76 and the retransition to S 145–147.3½ (see Finding 3).

None falls inside a running phrase at tempo. Where the music holds, it is at a point the form wants to hold: a breath before a section, a stop chord, or the arrival of a theme. This is the "concentrate on every bar" test applied to the whole score, and it passes.

### 3. Inner-voice motion under soft pads (Ex. 271–272, Ex. 277–278; no change)

Ex. 272 is a "chromatic inner melody" that "changes chord quality" (PDF 207). Ex. 278 shows what Sebesky does not want: a soft background whose lead moves only in semibreves, by step, without character (PDF 210). The scan in Finding 2 found the flute motto 73–76 as the longest soft hold outside a cadence: the flute holds A♭5 for 3½ beats at 74 and the pad holds alone at 76. Earlier passes did not check this passage against the reading. I did:

- Under the flute, Vln II moves one note per bar, E♭4 (74) – G♭4 (75) – F♭4 (76). The chord changes from A♭ major through A♭ minor to the iv6/4 at 76 (c182's F♭5 in the flute leads into it). This is Ex. 272: one chromatic inner voice changes the chord's quality while the lead holds. The flute line itself (C5–A♭5–G5–G♭5–F5–E♭5–F♭5) has the character Ex. 277 asks for.
- The bareness is the motto's character. Its first statement (1–4) is just as bare, and the flute motto is protected (c170, c190). Adding a fill under 74 would put Sebesky's "carousel" device on a "prayer" passage (PDF 223).
- Dvořák does the same in the flute's G-major theme of 9/i (reference corpus, musicxml-converted/ly/Dvorak_Sym9_mvt1.ly, bars 150–156; flute at lines 179–186): while the flute holds D5 at 153 (2/4), the strings under it change notes at most once per bar (Vln II B–E–D, cellos G–E–B, in minims) and add no fill.

The other soft pads also have one moving inner voice, or a moving lead above or below them: 43–46 (Hn 1 echo, then Cl 1 under Vln II D♭4–E♭4–E♭4–D♭4); 107–110 (viola tune and B.cl counter under Vln II div., upper voice E4–E4/D♯4–F4–F4/E4, lower B3–G3/A3–B♭3/D♭4–C4); 147–154 (lower halo voice C–C♯–D at 148, tune below); 175–179 (chorale; top line is the motto). None is an Ex. 278 case.

### 4. Lead against bass (Ex. 276; no change)

Sebesky: "Contrary motion, especially between the lead voice and the bass line, can provide a subtle touch of melodic character" (PDF 209). I counted, between consecutive tune onsets, how the tune and the lowest sounding note move (contrary.py):

| Statement | Contrary | Similar | Oblique | Contrary share of moving pairs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P 15–22 (Vln I) | 11 | 18 | 8 | 38% |
| S 47–55 (Vc) | 9 | 9 | 12 | 50% |
| S 56–68 (Vln I) | 9 | 5 | 32 | 64% |
| Codetta 69–72 (Fl 1) | 5 | 2 | 5 | 71% |
| S in B minor 97–102 (Vln I) | 4 | 5 | 12 | 44% |
| S recap 147–155 (Vla) | 11 | 6 | 17 | 65% |
| S climax 156–166 (Vln I) | 6 | 4 | 22 | 60% |
| Coda 171–174 (Vc, bass without it) | 2 | 0 | 9 | 100% |

P has the lowest share. I looked at why. At its two most memorable points the motion is contrary: the chromatic fall D–D♭–C at 15.3–16.1 runs against the cello rising C3–D3, and the fall from F5 at 17 runs against F3–A♭3. The similar pairs come from the double basses' C2/G1 pulse moving down with the tune's cadential descents at 16 and 18, and from the 21–22 descent over the bass F–G–C. Those are cadences, where the bass must move to its root with the line. Ex. 276 offers contrary motion as a source of character, not a rule; Sebesky's own Ex. 276 has parallel tenths in its first bar. The lyrical statements (S at 56–68, 147–155, 156–166) are mostly contrary or oblique, which fits a long cantabile line over a slower bass. No change.

### 5. The introduction as a mood introduction (Ex. 288; no change)

Ex. 288 builds an introduction from texture and colour (tremolo strings, harp glissandi, a held-back open horn solo) and keeps the voice out until the stage is set (PDF 226–230). Bars 1–14 do the same with symphonic means: the motto low over a ppp timpani roll, the C.a. answer a fifth higher, a rising-sixth ladder in the strings over a German sixth (12), and the solo horn call (13–14) as the last, held-back colour before the Allegro. As in Ex. 288, the harp and the trumpets are kept out of the introduction. Ex. 285 bar 10 gives the entering voice its pitch: the C.a. hands G4 to Vln I at 9.4, and Vln II holds G4 under Vln I's last motto at 177.4. Nothing to change.

### 6. Quotation (Ex. 287; not applicable, recorded)

Sebesky uses a quotation "never, never more than once in the same arrangement" and only if it is well known (PDF 224–225). Movement I quotes nothing. The motto's returns are not quotations in his sense; they are the work's own cyclic material, and their number and colours are protected (c160, c190). The reserve kept for movement III (recTwoMelodyA, the violins' C-major S head) also fits the spirit of the rule: a striking statement is saved for one place.
