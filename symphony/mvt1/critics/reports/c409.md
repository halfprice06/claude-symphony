## Findings

### 1. Beethoven 7/i and this movement share their three main structural devices. Keep.

(a) Retransition. Beethoven 264–277 (corpus PDF 74–77) keeps the dactyl in the strings over the dominant, then raises the winds to "più f" at 274 and lands the recapitulation ff at 278 with the theme in the full orchestra. Bars 117–130 here do the same in a Romantic form: a 14-bar G pedal, the brass motto, fff at 122, the collapse, then the half-augmented motto in stretto over the cellos' quaver-then-triplet pulse and a timpani roll from pp at 127 to f at 130, and ff at 131. A&S describe the same shape at the scale of a phrase: Beethoven's long V♭9 at pp before the ff tonic in Op. 31/1 (Ex. 27-3, PDF 464). The leading tone is kept out of 127–130 (c263), which saves its arrival for 131 as Beethoven saves the full tutti for 278.

(b) Chromatic bass under a crescendo. Beethoven's coda (397–422, corpus PDF 108–112) is a two-bar chromatic ground in violas, cellos and basses (E–D♯–D–C♯ and back) under held winds, sempre pp rising to ff. The wedge 111–116 here has a bass falling F–E–E♭–D–D♭–C by whole bars under "mp cresc." to ff at 117. A&S PDF 552–553 give the reason both work: a chromatic bass on a diatonic frame, the lament figure, read as one motion to V. Beethoven's runs 22 bars; this one runs six. The difference is one of proportion, and the section lengths are protected (c243, c333).

(c) Rhetorical silences. Beethoven's first bars (corpus PDF 1) set tutti f strokes with rests against a soft wind line, and his final bars (446–450, PDF 119) are tutti strokes separated by rests. This score uses silence the same way at the stop chords 26, 138 and 170, each a vii°7 ff followed by a rest or fermata. A&S PDF 483 place exactly these chords and N6 "at fermatas, before rhetorical silences".

A conductor who knows Beethoven 7 would recognise a composer in control of the large rhythm. No change.

### 2. Where Beethoven differs: one rhythm drives his whole Vivace; this Allegro eases its tempo seven times. Keep.

Beethoven's Vivace starts (bar 63, PDF 22) with the dactyl in flute and oboe "sempre p" and never changes tempo; continuity comes from that one rhythm. This Allegro relaxes at 45–46 (poco rit.), 47 (Poco meno mosso), 66–68 (rit.), 121–126 (allargando, sostenuto), 145–146 (poco rit.), 147 (Poco meno mosso) and 165–166 (rit.), before 170 (fermata) and 171 (Più lento). Next to Beethoven this is a lot. It stays, for three reasons. The named models (Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Dvořák) use exactly this lyric easing for second groups, and Beethoven is a comparison here, not the style model. The tempo plan is protected (c143, c268, c368). And the score already has a Beethoven-like unifying pulse that comes back at the main joins: the first group's bass `c4 r8 g,8` (15–22), the timpani's hammered form at 27, 30 and 131, the Cb pulse at 143, and the timpani pulse at 179–180, which the scherzo and finale quote (coherence/mvt1.md seeds). Removing any of the rits would also change the S phrasing at 45–47 and 145–147, which is protected (c088, c239).

### 3. The introduction is soft throughout, while Beethoven's starts with tutti forte strokes. Keep.

Beethoven's Poco sostenuto opens with f tutti chords punctuating a soft oboe line (bars 1–5, PDF 1), and it takes about a quarter of the movement. Here the introduction has one forte, the German sixth at 12, and takes 67 of 430 seconds (16%). Forte strokes at 1–8 would break the low pp motto that is protected (c229, c251) and that the coda's 175–179 brings back as the frame. The German sixth at 12 is the finale's first chord (coherence/mvt1.md seeds). Its single f, followed by the solo horn call, does the job Beethoven's strokes do: it makes the start of the Allegro an event. No change.
