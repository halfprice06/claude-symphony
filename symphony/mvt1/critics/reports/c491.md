## Findings

This is the third pass of this reading. The score follows every principle in the chapter that applies to it. I looked separately for places where the brass presentation of a theme falls short of a master's, and found none that would justify a change. I applied nothing. The findings record what I checked and why each passage should stay as it is.

### 1. Bars 117–121, 131 and 167: one theme head, two registers. Keep.

**The reading.** Adler (PDF 19–20) uses Ex. XI-5 to show that register decides whether a brass unison sounds dark or bright. Hindemith "could have transposed it up a third or a fourth" if he had wanted brilliance. As written, the brilliance "comes from the unison of the horns and trombones rather than of blaring trumpets."

**The score.**
- **117–121:** the motto is dark. Hn 1·2 and Tbn 1·2 play G3–E♭4–D4–D♭4–C4 in unison, ff, with the bass trombone an octave lower and no trumpets.
- **131–134:** the P head is in the tenor, with the counterline above it.
- **167–169:** the P head is bright. Tpt 1·2 play it a2 at G4–E♭5, ff, with Hn 1·2 an octave lower.

**Why it works.** The listener hears the rising sixth G–E♭ dark twice before hearing it bright once, at the coda. Keeping the trumpets off the head until 167 follows Adler's Beethoven point (PDF 19): the brass entry is the event.

**Would anything gain?** Moving the 117 motto up a third or fourth would brighten it. But it would take away the contrast that 167 depends on, and the brighter colour then belongs to the finale's major motto ("Do not reuse": the falling major motto in full brass at forte).

**Fix.** None.

### 2. Bars 47–55, 103–106 and 147–155: horn with strings on S. Keep.

**The reading.** Adler's first combination (PDF 27) is the horn doubling a long, sustained string melody. In the image of Ex. XI-10, Strauss lets the horns take parts of the cello line in turn rather than doubling all of it.

**The score.** The S head is in Hn 1 with the strings every time, with a different partner and key:

| Bars | Key | Doubling |
|---|---|---|
| 47–55 | A♭ | Hn 1 + Vc, p dolce |
| 103–106 | D minor | Hn 1 + Vc, Bsn 1 an octave lower, mf |
| 147–155 | C major | Hn 1 + Vla, Vc an octave lower |

At 152–153 Hn 1 leaves the line where it rises to F5 (written C6) and rejoins at 154. That is the Strauss procedure: the horn takes the part of the line that lies well for it, and the string carries on alone.

**Effect on memorability.** The horn colour on S is what movement II takes up for its theme A (mvt1.md). It gives S a fixed timbral identity across three keys, and a listener remembers a tune partly by its colour. Dvořák 9/i does the same with its horn theme at the start of the Allegro (reference-corpus/mutopia/DvorakA_O95/Sym9/Sym9-lys/brass.notesA.ily, `hornIIImA`).

**Fix.** None.

### 3. Bars 158–161: the S climax in octaves, trumpet with oboe. Keep.

**The reading.**
- Elgar (Ex. XI-8, PDF 24): octave and double-octave doublings make a massive sound.
- Weinberger (Ex. XI-11, PDF 28): trumpet and oboe together get through a full texture.
- PDF 23: know which notes must dominate, and how.

**The score.** The tune is laid out as follows:

| Octave | Instruments |
|---|---|
| E6 / A♭6 | Vln I, Fl 1·2 |
| E5 / A♭5 | Tpt 1, Ob 2, Vln II |
| E4 / A♭4 | Hn 1·2, Vc |

The two notes that must dominate are the rising-sixth goals:
- G5→E6 at 158, ff;
- C6→A♭6 at 160, fff, largamente.

Each is the longest note of its bar, falls on a downbeat, and carries a tenuto in every part with the tune. Repeating the motto's own interval twice at the top is what makes this climax recognisable as the same melody. Tchaikovsky's 6/i second theme and Rachmaninoff's second-group climaxes work the same way.

**Schuller (PDF 26).** His warning concerns Hn 3·4 a2 at 158–161. Answered by c174, c181, c266, c291 and c381: the notes are single beats, and Tpt 1·2 share the line. I have no new reason to reopen it.

**Fix.** None.

### 4. Bars 13–14 and 41–44: the horn call. Keep.

**The reading.** Adler (PDF 26) calls the four-horn unison "a brilliant, high concept".

**The score.** The movement keeps that brilliant form back:
- 13–14: Hn 1 solo, mp espressivo, E♭3–C4–B3, the B unresolved.
- 41–42: four horns ff, low, E♭3–C4–B♭3.
- 43–44: Hn 1 alone answers an octave higher at p.

**Memorability.** The call is two bars long. Its shape (a rising sixth, then a semitone down) is the motto's, so a listener files it with the motto rather than as a separate tune. That is right for an introduction whose completion, the major call in the trumpets, is reserved for the finale. Raising 41–42 by an octave would make it brighter, but it would spend the finale's high four-horn unison.

**Fix.** None.

### 5. Bars 175–181: trombone chorale and the motto canon. Keep.

**The reading.**
- **Stravinsky (Ex. XI-7, PDF 24):** the last phrase of a thick brass passage must not be muddy. Here the three trombones are pp in open position.
- **Berlioz (Ex. XI-14, PDF 30–32):** a canon works by colour contrast. Here Tbn 1's motto is answered by Vln I at 177.4, brass against strings, and the timpani pulse at 179–180 takes the place of the ostinato.
- **Schubert (Ex. XI-12, PDF 28):** a diminuendo by thinning the trombones. Here Tbn 2 and the bass trombone stop after 179, and Tbn 1 alone restrikes C4 at 180 and fades by 181.

**Model.** Dvořák 9/ii opens with a pp trombone chorale that grows only briefly (reference-corpus/mutopia/DvorakA_O95/Sym9/Sym9-lys/brass.notesL.ily, `tromboneImL`, bars 1–4, ppp). The soft brass chorale as the sound a listener carries out of a movement is a Romantic model, and this coda uses it.

**Fix.** None.

### 6. The themes as tunes: would a listener hum them?

This part answers the lens directly; the reading is about presentation.

- **Motto (G–E♭–D–C–D♭–C).** Short, with a rising minor sixth and a chromatic return, heard in seven colours. It is as easy to keep in mind as Tchaikovsky 5's motto.
- **S (E♭ | C–B♭ | A♭–F–E♭ | D♭–C–B♭–D♭–F | A♭–G …).** This is the hummable tune: a rising sixth, then a long stepwise fall. It has three peaks that rise:
  - B♭5 at 60;
  - C6 at 62;
  - A♭6 at 160 (the sixth again, fff).

  That is the plan of a Tchaikovsky or Rachmaninoff second group.
- **P.** A driving sentence that peaks at D♭6 (20). Its memorability comes from the motto's sixth and D♭ in its head and from the hammered tutti, which is right for an Allegro appassionato.

I looked for a phrase that could sing more and found none worth the cost:
- **S at 47.** A longer upbeat (a crotchet E♭ is already there) would weaken the downbeat C, which is the tune's arrival.
- **Climax at 160.** A higher peak would go past the movement's A♭6 ceiling, which keeps C7 for the finale.

Every change to a quoted theme would also have to be passed to three other movements, and the reading offers no gain to set against that.

**Fix.** None.
