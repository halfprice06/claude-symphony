### 2. ♭VII7 at 156 (Ex. 24-18, PDF 410–411): considered, not proposed

A&S present ♭VII7 → V6/5 as "surprising and dramatic". In their words it is "a valuable alternative to extending dominant harmony through changes within V7 itself". The recapitulation's climax approach has ♭VII at 156 (B♭ major, darkened to B♭ minor on beat 4.5), then iv at 157, the 6/4 at 158 and V7 at 159. Adding A♭ to the B♭ chord on beat 1 would make it the real ♭VII7 of the example. However, a ♭VII7 here is also V7 of E♭, and it would point to E♭ just before the music turns to F minor. The Handel example works because the expected III never comes and V6/5 arrives instead. At 156–157, iv comes instead, so the device would raise an expectation the score answers with a weaker chord, not the dominant.

The cello's A♭3 on 156.4 already hints at the seventh, as an anticipation of Hn 3 at 157 (c362). The passage is also protected ("156–166 pitches", c346). Keep.

### 3. Where the harmony could be more vivid: the places I tested on paper

The reading's colour devices are already used in the bars where they matter most:

- **Deceptive cadences.** The exposition has two before its authentic close at 72: V7–vii°7/vi–vi at 63–64, and vi at 68. The recapitulation stacks ♭VI (160), vii°7 over D (161) and vi (162) before 165–166. This is the "delay, not cancel" of PDF 427, used twice and graded.
- **Augmented sixths.** There are two forms: the German sixth at 12, and the ♯4 over the tonic bass at 116.
- **5–♯5–6 into ii.** It appears at 148, the recapitulation's only new applied chord, at pp dolce.
- **Apparent seventh chords.** There are added-6th sonorities at 46.1 and 153, and the tonic-pedal complex at 167–170. These cover §14–15 of Unit 24.

I looked for a dominant prolonged only "through changes within V7 itself", where Ex. 24-18 would give an alternative. There are five: 13–14, 32, 83–84, 117–130 and 144–146. All five are protected, and the first four are quoted or answered by the other movements. At 144–146 the soprano 13th (c192) is the colour of that dominant, and replacing it with a ♭VII7 approach would rewrite the retransition into C major. I found no bar where a technique from this reading would be a clear gain over what is written.

## Checked and kept (with the reason the reading does not overturn)

1. **Bar 40: doubled leading tone D in V7 of E♭** (PDF 419, 439). Every D goes to E♭, and A♭ is answered by G in the same register. Kept for the reasons given by c234, c252, c272 and c362. Nothing new here answers them.

2. **177.4→178: doubled 7th F.** Vln II F4→E♭4 resolves it; Tbn 2 F3→G3 rises. This breaks the rule of doubling (PDF 439, point 3). c309 tried both repairs: E♭3 makes octaves with Vln II into the D♭ chord, and a held E♭3 makes a low major second at pp. The F in Tbn 2 is held from iiø7, where it is a chord 3rd, and becomes a 7th only when the B and G arrive on beats 3–4. The chorale is on the cross-movement list (coda 171–182). Keep.

3. **161.4→162: Vln I and II carry B down to A over V7–vi.** The leading tone's resolution to C is in Tpt 1 (B4→C5) and the brass. This is a transferred resolution into a lower voice (PDF 406–407), not the same-register ideal. The trumpet's B→C is a recorded cross-movement place (coherence/mvt1.md, "Brass use"), and the violins' F6–D6–B5–A5 is the arpeggiated descent of the climax line. Keep.

4. **64.3→65: viiø7/V of A♭ (D–F–A♭–C) moves to IV (D♭), not to V.** By PDF 430–431 this is an apparent applied chord inside the falling-thirds approach: the D♮ is a chromatic passing note in the bass F–D–D♭–B♭–E♭, kept in one voice (PDF 420). Protected (c142, c162). Keep.
