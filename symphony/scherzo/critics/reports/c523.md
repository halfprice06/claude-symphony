## Findings

### 1. Bars 127 and 283: a mid-phrase authentic cadence inside the E-minor second idea (APPLIED)

**Problem.** The second idea in E minor (121–132, reprise 277–288) has three groups: 121–124, 125–128 and the hemiola cadence 129–131. In bar 3 of the second group the music makes a full authentic cadence. At 126 beats 2–3 the bass is B under a complete V7♭9 (Cl 1 A4, Cl 2 C4, Hn 2 D♯4, Bsn 1 F♯3, melody F♯6). At 127 the bass falls a fifth to E (Vc E3, Cb E2), every upper voice resolves (A→G, C→B, D♯→E), and the melody lands on 1̂ (F♯6→E6, Fl 1, Ob 1, Vln I/II) on the downbeat. That is a root-position V7–i with 2̂–1̂ on top, four bars before the real cadence at 131. The G-major statement does not do this: its parallel bar 51 is A7 over E, an inverted dominant, so the phrase keeps moving to 55.

**Evidence.** The reading names this exact case three times. PDF 115, §12: in a regular pulse "the progression V(7)–I [is] undesirable except at cadential points, especially if the soprano moves to 1̂ … Using VII6 or V6 instead of root-position V, or using I6 instead of I5/3, will also help." PDF 116, point 8: "To avoid an unwanted cadential effect, don't use V–I5/3 in the middle of a phrase if the soprano goes to 1̂." PDF 110, box item 5: "the melodic succession 2̂–1̂ or 7̂–1̂ should not be set with the progression V(7)–I5/3." The sample solution on PDF 118 is our situation bar for bar: bar 3 of a four-bar group, melody on 1̂ after a V in the bar before, and the authors choose I6 "because … a root-position tonic might produce an inappropriate cadential effect." The form lens is why this matters here. A cadence is "a rhythmic as well as a tonal event" that ends a group (PDF 94). An early PAC at 127 takes weight from the hemiola cadence at 131/287, which is the cadence that ends the exposition's close and the reprise.

**Fix (applied).** The upper voices stay as they are. Only the bass instruments change, on beats 1–3 of 127 and the tie into 128. Under the melody's E6 the bass takes G (i6) for the minim and returns to B (V7) on beat 3. It then falls to A♯ (vii°7 of V) and B as before. The bass becomes A–C | C–B | G–B | B–A♯ | B. Bars 126–130 are now one expanded dominant with the tonic as a neighbour chord inside it. This is the Jupiter pattern of PDF 129–130 (Ex. 8-14: "I" chords that "extend and intensify dominant harmony").

- sC.ily, `sCVc`, bar 127–128: `e2( fis4~ | fis4 ais,2)` → `g,2( b,4~ | b,4 ais,2)`
- sC.ily, `sCCb`: `e,2( fis,4~ | fis,4 ais,,2)` → `g,,2( b,,4~ | b,,4 ais,,2)`
- sC.ily, `sCBsnTwo`: `e2 fis4~ | fis4 ais,2` → `g,2 b,4~ | b,4 ais,2`
- sC.ily, `sCCbsn`: `e,2( fis,4~ | fis,4 ais,,2)` → `g,,2( b,,4~ | b,,4 ais,,2)`
- dA.ily, bar 283–284, the same in `dACb`, `dABsnTwo` and `dACbsn` (the cellos have the melody there).

**Voice leading checked.** 126→127: the bass B→G moves against Bsn 1 F♯3→G3, Hn 2 D♯4→E4, the melody F♯→E and Cl 2/violas C→B, with no parallel perfect intervals. The 7th A4→G4 (Cl 1) meets the bass's G in similar motion. That is the V7–I6 hidden octave of Ex. 7-7 (PDF 109). The book limits that warning to "simple textures" and illustrates it with the 7th in the soprano. Here the 7th is an inner clarinet line under five octaves of melody, so I accept it. I tried a rising bass (B2→G3) to avoid it. That gives Bsn 1 a similar-motion unison on G3 (Ex. 8-9a, "poor") and leaves the same Cl 1 octave at G→F♯, so it is worse. I also tried keeping beat 3 on F♯ (i6 → V4/3). That moves in octaves with Cl 1's G4→F♯4, so I rejected it. In the applied version, 127.1→127.3 has the bass G→B against Cl 1 G→F♯ and the melody E→D♯ (contrary), with violas, Hn 4 and Cl 2 holding B (oblique). The tie of V7 over the bar line into 128 was already in the theme (its syncopated figure, PDF 102: conflict of rhythm and meter as a resource). The G in the bass, Bsn 1 and Cl 1 triples the 3rd, which PDF 108 allows ("any tone of I6 may be doubled"). No C-major triad. c082/c132's V♭9 voicing at 126/282 is untouched, and so is c412/c452's vii°7 of V rising a semitone into 129/285.

**Why a listener hears it.** At 127 the basses used to fall a fifth to the tonic under a resolving V♭9, which sounded like an ending. Now they drop a third and come back to B, so the phrase leans on to the hemiola, and the bass circles the dominant B–G–B–A♯–B for four bars. Both E-minor statements now match the G-major one, where the mid-phrase dominant is inverted. This is the change the reading supports that the ear will notice most: a bass line at mp–f under the movement's second theme, twice.

### 2. Coda 341–344: T's A-major statement over the root bass A–A–E–A (considered, not applied)

This is the same Ex. 7-1/7-8 case c433 fixed at 170: bar 2 of T (E4–A4) is pure tonic, so an I6 (C♯2) at 342 would be easy. I did not change it. The statement recalls T's first statement, harp off-beats and root bass included. c433's do-not-touch keeps that musette bass at 153, and the recall is heard as the opening of the trio coming back. Giving it an I6 would weaken the reminiscence and add nothing to its function as IV in the coda's i–IV–I (339–356).

### 3. Theme A's first PAC at 20 (112, 260, 312): kept

PDF 94–95 reserves the perfect cadence mostly for the end, and an IAC on 3̂ (G5) at 20 would leave 28 as the theme's only full close. I rejected this candidate on the reading's own model. 13–20 is the textbook period of PDF 117–118 (four bars to an HC at 16, four bars to an authentic cadence at 20), and the timpani's B–B–E answer at 20 and 28 needs a full stop. The change would also alter theme A's notes in all four statements for no gain in direction.

### 4. The hemiola V7 at 101–104 and 301–304: kept

PDF 102 says a chord change attracts an accent. Alternating V7 with a "I" neighbour (Ex. 8-14) on the hemiola minims would put a harmonic accent on each minim. I worked this out on paper and rejected it. It would put a tonic 6/4 on the last minim of 104 and repeat the tonic from weak to strong into 105, which is the "poor" case of Ex. 6-20a. It would also take away the freshness of the ff tonic arrival that the four bars of pure V7 prepare (PDF 95: the expanded V shapes the form). c442 had already kept the plain V7 here for other reasons.

### 5. Weak–strong scan and the long tonic spans: kept

At every four-bar join only 84→85 holds a chord from a weak into a strong bar: the F/A of the Neapolitan approach. It is the end of Cl 1's first group and the start of its second, which is the exception of Ex. 6-21 (PDF 103). The E stretches 133–152, 225–240 and 357–376 are root-position pedals, as PDF 112 requires for a chord that carries a section. Their timing is unchanged (about 14, 15 and 21 s).
