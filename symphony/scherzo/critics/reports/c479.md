## Holistic comparison with Brahms, Op. 11/ii

- **The same problem as ch. 16.** Op. 11 is Brahms transcribing himself, from a nonet-sized band to a classical orchestra. The scherzo was written into that process. The chamber core (one flute, clarinets, bassoon, one horn, strings) stays audible inside the larger scoring. This score works the same way:
  - Theme A at 13–28 is bare strings.
  - The F-major episode at 73–88 has no strings.
  - The trio is built on single wind colours (Cl 1, Ob 1, Hn 3).
  - The trumpets and low brass are kept back for the tuttis (Adler PDF 236's "save the brass").
  
  A conductor who knows Op. 11 would recognise that economy as a habit of the same school.
- **Tempo of the trio.** Brahms's trio is *Poco più moto*; this one is *Un poco meno mosso*, after the Eroica and Dvořák 8. c129's reason (proportions between the Adagio and the finale) stands, and I do not re-raise it.
- **Key of the trio.** Brahms moves to ♭VI (B♭ in D minor). This trio is in the parallel major so that the movement can begin and end on G♯. That is a cyclic rule, and I leave it.
- **Would a conductor believe a master wrote it?** For the scoring, yes, page after page. The one place where the ink shows an orchestrator's slip rather than a choice is the end of the trio's last statement (finding 1). The second idea is still the least memorable tune (open since c401), and this reading gives no reason to change its notes.

## Findings

### 1. 223–224: Fl 1 holds the trio's last E6 into a diminuendo to nothing; let it drop out after two beats (applied)

**Bars:** 222–224, Fl 1 (tC.ily, tCFluteOne).

**Problem:** At 217 Fl 1 joins Vln I at pitch for T's climax phrase, which is right (PDF 208–209: emphasis by doubling). Then it is asked to tie E6 from 223 into 224 and fade with the violins to nothing. That has two costs:
- A held E6 in the flute's third octave does not diminuendo cleanly; it goes flat and thins.
- The fade is "left to the players", with six sections diminuendoing on the same notes.

**Evidence:**
- Adler PDF 213 (Dvořák, Ex. XVI-10 mm. 33–40) calls this "a model of exactly how one accomplishes a diminuendo from fortissimo to piano". The piccolo is rewritten an octave lower "to enable the player to accomplish the diminuendo", "all other instruments are also in controllable ranges", and the drop-out is part of the diminuendo.
- PDF 213 (Bizet): "the diminuendo clearly orchestrated, instead of being left to the players or conductor".
- PDF 224 (Ravel): notate "the decay and cessation of a pitch". As instruments "descend and drop out", the diminuendo is orchestrated.
- The score's own neighbours already do this: Hn 1·2 and Hn 4 stop at the end of 223, and Bsn 1/Cbsn thin out. The flute is the only top-register part left holding.

The octave cannot be lowered here: E5 is already Cl 1 and Vln II. Dropping out is the method that remains.

### 2. 185–192: sustained "harmonic counterpoint" under the Ob 1 call and the cellos' answer (considered, not applied)

Dvořák (PDF 209) and Ravel (PDF 217) would put a soft sustained string background under a wind call, and at 185–192 there is only harp off-beats and pizz. bass. I rejected it for two reasons:
- Every string section's entry at 193 is the start of the trio's string build. A pad at 185 spends that entry in advance (PDF 245: the drop-out "provides a great freshness for the re-entrance"; PDF 228).
- The only free register is the solo's own or the harp's (c028 kept accompaniment out of the solo's register).

A horn pad has the same cost at 195/201. The point is protected (c222, c279) for these reasons, and the reading confirms them.

### 3. 217–220: Ob 1 doubling the lower octave of T's climax (considered, not applied)

PDF 208 says Brahms "orchestrates the mezzo-forte by doubling the violins with horns and oboes", and PDF 209 says Dvořák gets emphasis from the largest doubling. Against that, Ob 1 is silent from 201 so that its *dolce* entry at 225 is the codetta's new colour. Four bars of oboe before it would make that entry stale (PDF 228, Stravinsky: change colour "so that when the oboe and bassoon play the solos ... their entrance is very fresh"). Cl 1 and Vln II already carry that octave. Kept.

### 4. 330–331: timpani motive x in the coda crescendo (considered, not applied)

PDF 230 notes that Schoenberg adds "a completely new rhythm in the timpani, which lends great excitement to the crescendo". But the bass at 330–331 is D, then C under F (Neapolitan 6/4, the French sixth). Against an E–G–B–E tuning, motive x on E or B would clash with both. The entries already orchestrate the crescendo: Vc 325, Vla 326, Vln II 327, Vln I 328, winds 329, horns 331–332, roll 332. Kept.

### 5. 105–108 and 305–308: withholding the flutes until 109/309 (considered, not applied)

PDF 245 supports c124's old plan of holding back a group so its tutti re-entry is fresh. c454 reversed that plan for a stronger reason: the tutti's top must be complete from its downbeat (Rimsky-Korsakov's resonance chapter). Flipping it back would undo a recent, reasoned decision. Kept.

### 6. 13–28: an independent cello rhythm under theme A, after Brahms's Hungarian Dance (considered, not applied)

PDF 205 praises "the steady pizzicato bass and the independent cello part". Here the cellos and basses share the downbeat pizzicato. The bare first statement is protected (c259, c359) so that the 31 and 253 statements can grow from it. The Brahms model's first lesson, the steady pizzicato bass, is already here. Kept.
