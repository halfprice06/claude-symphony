## Findings

### 1. Bars 45–52: Fl 1 doubles Vln I's second idea at pitch (built, rejected)

**The case for it.** In Brahms 3/i mm. 183–87 (PDF 163–164, XV-14) the violins carry the forte tune and the high flutes "help by emphasizing the first melody note in each measure" with held notes. Schubert (PDF 168–169, XV-19) leaves the tune to the strings and keeps the flutes apart from them. PDF 165 says at-pitch doublings lose the colour of both instruments and that sustained doublings tire the ear. Here Fl 1 doubles Vln I at pitch (D6–A6) for eight bars, and the second idea is still the least memorable idea (open since c289). A change in the top colour of its first statement would be the most audible change this reading supports.

**What I built** (/tmp/critic-584/work/sections/sA.ily, sAFluteOne 45–52): the Brahms device, with Fl 1 holding the first melody note of each bar.
```
old: d'''4.->(\mf^\markup \italic "espress."\< c'''8 b''4~ | b''4 g'''2-\tweak stencil ##f \f\> | fis'''2 e'''4~ | e'''4-\tweak stencil ##f \mf d'''4. fis'''8) | e'''4.->(\< d'''8 cis'''4~ | cis'''4 a'''2 | g'''2 fis'''4~ | fis'''4 e'''4. d'''8) |
new: d'''2.->(\mf^\markup \italic "espress."\< | b''2.-\tweak stencil ##f \f\> | fis'''2. | e'''2.-\tweak stencil ##f \mf) | e'''2.->(\< | cis'''2. | g'''2. | fis'''2.) |
```
The build was clean: build-warnings.txt empty, 13 pages, "part lengths: all match", "C-major triads: none", and the same single analyser parallel as v584 (bar 69, by design). Themecheck reported one change, secondIdeaG FluteOne 45. No rest moved, so no part cue was affected.

**Why it makes the music worse.** Brahms's tune puts its main notes on the downbeats, so the flutes' held first notes are the tune's peaks. The second idea is a hemiola: its accents fall on beats 1, 3 and 2, and both peaks come on beat 2 (G6 at 46, A6 at 50). With the device, Fl 1 holds B5 under the violins' G6 at 46 and C♯6 under their A6 at 50. The flute drops out of the top exactly at the two peaks and becomes an inner voice. The version that follows the hemiola (minims on beats 1 and 3, then 2) is the tune itself without its two passing quavers, which is what Fl 1 plays now. The present doubling is therefore the hemiola form of Brahms's device. Bar 45 is also the arrival of the f build at 41–44. Wagner's rule (PDF 172) adds the highs at the arrival and does not take them away there. I reverted the change. This answers c404's first reason more exactly and confirms it, and it confirms c224 and c314.

### 2. Bars 217–220: the trio's ff peak has no wind harmony between the violins' octaves (considered, not applied)

At the trio's climax T sounds in three octaves: Vln I with Fl 1 (C♯6–A6), Vln II with Cl 1 (C♯5–A5) and the cellos (C♯4–A4). The harmony lies below, in the violas, Hn 1·2 (pp<mf), the bassoons and the bass clarinet. Brahms XV-14 (PDF 164) has "each chord fully represented in all the choirs and in every octave". Fl 2 and Cl 2 are free here. I wrote out a held wind chord for them and did not build it. The fifth octave is not empty: Vln II and Cl 1 move through the whole of C♯5–A5. A held E5 or G♯5 would cross the tune's lower octave on every bar (at 220 the tune falls to A4 under it), against the chapter's first rule (PDF 159). Adler's opening page (PDF 159) treats a chamber-like use of the large orchestra as equally valid, and the trio's transparency at its peak is that choice. c494's reason (Ob 1's dolce entry at 225) also still holds for the oboes. Kept.

### 3. Bars 329–332: the motto's chromatic tail at p under the strings' motive x at mf (kept)

Cl 1 G5–D♯5 and the cor anglais an octave lower play the tail at p< while the strings' stretto ostinato is at mf<. Schoenberg's rule (PDF 184) would mark the principal voice louder. However, the tail is a sustained legato line against detached repeated notes, and PDF 169 says that opposing timbres and designs are enough for the ear to separate two ideas. The two groups' dynamics follow Rimsky-Korsakov's crescendo by groups (c050). The tail is heard by its design, not by its level. Kept.

### 4. Bars 129–130 and 285–286: timpani rolls enter at f (kept)

Bloch (PDF 184) marks his rolls p and then crescendo, so that they do not cover the combined themes. These two rolls are not crescendos into a climax. They enter with the orchestra's f on the dominant under the hemiola cadence, on B2, more than two octaves under the tune (B5–F♯5), and they stop at the tonic stroke. That is Holst's "heavy bass" that steadies a cadence (PDF 181). The rolls that do build (101, 205–207, 332) are already marked soft and then crescendo. Kept.

### 5. Bars 41–44 and 273–276: the same scoring both times (kept)

Mahler's rule (PDF 170–171) is to colour a recurring passage differently each time. The f call on V of G is scored the same in the exposition and the reprise. It is a four-bar link in a sonata reprise, and what follows it is recoloured: the second idea returns at 277 as Fl 1 over Ob 1, sub. p, in E major. The texture and harmony of the link stay recognisable, which is the other half of Mahler's rule. Kept.

### 6. Bars 361–364: the soft final chord (follows)

Brahms's "dangerous" chord (PDF 162) has the flutes on top in their high octave. Here Fl 1 holds G♯5 pp, which is in the flute's second octave and can be played softly. At 364 the top note passes to Vln I on the same pitch. That is Adler's remedy, strings on top (c550). The rolled harp chord at 361 is the soft "ping" (PDF 165). Follows.

### 7. The trio's colours against Mahler 4/iv and *Scheherazade* (follows)

The cellos and Hn 3 at pitch at 169, with Vln I an octave higher from 177 and Vln II's figuration above, match the layering of XV-27: cello and horns on the tune with clarinets an octave higher, a "dance figure" middleground and a held background. The harp is the constant under the soft statements and call (153, 185, 225, 341–348). It supports the trio's tonic arrival at 225 together with the bass E, as Rimsky-Korsakov's harp supports the final cadence (PDF 179). Follows.

### 8. Bars 57–68: the fugato has no wind colours on its entries (kept)

Mozart colours each statement of the *Jupiter* cantus firmus with different winds (PDF 176). That passage is the coda of a quintuple fugue, where the colours help separate five combined subjects. This fugato is a single subject over one countersubject, pp, after the G-major tutti, and is modelled on Beethoven 9/ii's string fugato. c007 kept the strings alone, and the stretto at 69 brings the winds in at the moment of combination. That matches PDF 177 and 181: elements are heard alone before they are combined. Kept.
