## What else I studied

Dvořák 8/iii is not in the local corpus. I worked from general knowledge of the score and checked the facts I rely on against the web. The movement is a G-minor waltz (Allegretto grazioso, 3/8). Its trio is in G major and is a Bohemian folk-dance tune, which flute and oboe play **in unison**. The coda (Molto vivace, 2/4) rebuilds the trio's opening notes in duple time. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._8_(Dvo%C5%99%C3%A1k), https://saskatoonsymphony.org/?p=6909, https://www.riphil.org/blog/the-story-behind-dvorak-s-symphony-no-8. I looked at every page of v99's score.pdf and read every sections/*.ily file. I diffed v89 against v99 section by section (134 changed non-comment lines) and checked each change against HISTORY.md for cycles 89–98. I compared the coda's harp voicing with the trio's (tA 153–168).

**Holistic verdict.** Measured against Dvořák 8/iii, the movement would convince a conductor on three counts. First, its scoring is transparent: the theme is in the strings over pizzicato bass, there is a string-free F-major episode, and each trio statement has its own colour. Second, the trio is soft and folk-like. Third, its coda recalls the trio. Dvořák's coda transforms the trio tune and speeds it up. This coda restates T slowly and then closes on the motto, and the cyclic plan requires that, so I do not count it against the score. Where Dvořák is more refined is in how his accompaniment yields. His accompaniment almost always sits one level below the tune and outside the tune's register. The three findings below are all places where v99 does not do this. None of them changes notes in a theme.

**Against v89.** Nothing has got worse. The ten accepted changes (c089–c098) all hold up in the score. Bar 300's ⊓/V split sounds the same. The stretto at 69–71/297–299 now has entries a bar apart, and its downbeat chords are unchanged. The closing timpani motive x replaces the roll that copied movement I. The B-major cadence at 180–184 is clean, and the harp now rests at 169–184. The only side effect I found is a dynamic left over from c094: the harp at 185 came back at p, level with the oboe's solo, and is now one degree too loud (Finding 2).

## Findings

### 1. Coda 345–348: the harp's chords sit in the band of Bsn 1's countermelody (applied)

**Problem.** At 345–348 Ob 1 has T's second phrase, D5–B5, p dolce. Bsn 1 has its countermelody (c078), A3–F♯3–D3–D♯3–E3–G♯3–B3, p dolce. The harp's off-beat chords are written in the bass clef at D3–B3 (`<d fis a>`, `<d fis b>`, `<fis a b>`, `<e gis b>`), which is exactly the bassoon's range. Each harp attack lands inside the bassoon's line, on beats 2 and 3 where the bassoon holds its minims. The violas (ppp) are in the same band too.

**Evidence.** Casanelles PDF 277–278: ensemble timbre comes from the interaction of instruments, and sounds in one frequency area mask each other. PDF 274: the harmonic series spaces low notes widely, and close triads below about D4 thicken. The score already follows this rule at 189–192, where c027/c028 moved the harp up out of the cor anglais' register. At 341–344 the low harp voicing is right, because the solo (Hn 1) is an octave above it.

**Fix (applied).** Put the harp up an octave from 345, between the bassoon and the oboe, and move the treble clef to 345:
```
\clef treble r4 <d' fis' a'>4 <d' fis' a'>4 | r4 <d' fis' b'>4 <d' fis' b'>4 | r4 <fis' a' b'>4 <fis' a' b'>4 | r4 <e' gis' b'>4 <e' gis' b'>4 | R2.*3 R2.*9 |
```
The `\clef treble` that stood before 357's rest is removed. The pedals are unchanged (D C♯ B / E F♯ G♯ A). Harmony, rhythm and dynamics are unchanged. 341–344 is left as it was.

### 2. Trio 153 and 185: the harp's off-beat chords are marked as loud as the solos above them (applied)

**Problem.** At 153 Cl 1 has T, p dolce. The harp's struck chords (G♯3–E4, two per bar, "non arpegg.") and its bass are also p. At 185 Ob 1's call is p espress. and the cor anglais/cello answer at 189 is p dolce, but the harp is again p (c094 set it so when the harp returned). The movement's own practice is different: at 341 the coda's recall of T puts the harp at pp under Hn 1's p, and at 225 the codetta harp is pp under Ob 1/Cl 1 p. In both trio places the accompaniment is marked level with the tune it accompanies.

**Evidence.** Casanelles PDF 271–272 and Fig. 6.1: Mahler writes the dynamic he wants the listener to hear. The trumpets that should sound behind the woodwinds are marked ppp against their pp, one level softer, whatever the players have to do to get that result. A struck harp chord at p has a strong attack transient (PDF 275–276), and here it falls between the clarinet's notes on every beat 2 and 3. Dvořák's trio in 8/iii keeps the tune (flute and oboe in unison) clearly above an accompaniment that is marked softer.

**Fix (applied).** In tA.ily, `tAHarpL = { e,4\pp r4 r4 ...` (was `\p`). In tB.ily, `tBHarpL = { cis,4\pp r4 r4 ...` (was `\p`). The harp's dynamics print once, under the LH, and the PianoStaff carries them for both hands, so these two marks cover both staves. This answers c094 on dynamics only: the harp still rests at 169–184 and returns at 185, at pp instead of p.

### 3. Retransition 241: Hn 1's G♮ re-enters without a dynamic (applied)

**Problem.** Hn 1 holds G♯4 at 237–240, breaks off with a crotchet rest, and re-attacks at 241 on G♮4. This G♮ is the turn back to E minor, and the global comment marks it as the reason the key signature changes at 241. The new attack has no dynamic. It inherits the pp from 237, four bars and a rest earlier, and in the Hn 1 part it follows a page of the trio.

**Evidence.** Casanelles PDF 279 (ADSR): every new attack is a new event with its own shape. PDF 271: the dynamic states the result the composer wants. Earlier engraving critics (c045, c055) restated dynamics after rests for the same reason.

**Fix (applied).** `retrHnOne = { g'2.~\pp | g'2.~ | ...`. Nothing else changes, and Hn 2 rests, so the shared staff has no conflict.
