## Findings

### 1. The violas join the cellos' T for its consequent, 177–184. Applied.
**Problem.** The cellos carry the second statement of T (169–184) alone at mp. The phrase that climbs to the tune's peak (A4 tied over 178–179, mf) and turns to B at 181–184 gets no more weight than the first two phrases. The violas are free throughout 153–192 (tAVla is all rests).
**Evidence.** Adler PDF 89 picks out "the very successful coupling of violas and cellos" and prints Ex. III-39 (PDF 91): Beethoven 5/ii, the movement's main tune in violas and cellos in unison, p dolce. On PDF 99–100 he says the cello shows best in "solo passages featuring the entire section" (Brahms 3/iii 1–12). Both models apply to this one statement. The cellos alone for the first two phrases follow Brahms. The coupled unison follows Beethoven for the phrase that climbs, so the climb gains carrying power without pushing the cellos past mf.
**Recorded reason.** c344 rested the violas because at pitch their off-beat dyads "would strike the cellos' T in unison". That reason concerns accompaniment figures, not the tune. A unison doubling cannot collide with the line it doubles, so it does not apply here.
**Plan check.** The trio's graded plan (do-not-touch, 149–240) is kept. Strings only play 169–184. Vln I still enters first at 193. A unison entry inside a phrase adds weight, not a new colour event. The violas' next entry, the Cl 1 + Vla statement at 197, is unchanged.
**Range.** D♯3–A4 lies on the viola's C, G and D strings, the dark solo colour of Ex. III-31 (PDF 87). No viola part cue falls in 177–184 (the viola part's only trio cue is the cor anglais at 189).
**Fix (tA.ily):**

### 2. T's falling sixth in the strings is a string crossing, not a shift. Kept.
c321 added a falling sixth slurred on a quaver: D♯–F♯ at 171 (Vc), 199 (Vln II, Vla) and 211 (Vln I, Vln II). Adler PDF 82–83 lists legato wide skips as a section hazard (Ex. III-24). In each part, both notes fall in one position on adjacent strings, so the skip needs no shift:
- Vln I at 211: D♯6 is the 2nd finger on E in 5th position, F♯5 the 1st finger on A in 5th position.
- Vln II and Vla: D♯5 on A and F♯4 on D, in 3rd position.
- Vc: D♯4 on A and F♯3 on D.
No change.

### 3. Open-string pitches on T's suspensions. Kept, no mark.
The 4–3 suspension's A falls on an open string's pitch in three places: Vc A3 at 171–172, Vln II A4 at 211–212, Vla A4 at 199–200. Adler PDF 73–74 says slow expressive tones should be stopped and even, and that an open string "stands out peculiarly". He also says fingering "is best left to the performer" and is marked only under special circumstances (PDF 73). Section players take an expressive held A on the lower string by habit. A "sul D" would be clutter. No change.

### 4. T's forte return keeps the high E-string octave, 209–224. Kept.
Vln I plays T at G♯5–A6, f with ff peaks at 214 and 218. That is the E string's high, forceful colour (PDF 75). I considered moving Vln I to the A string or down an octave, in line with Ex. III-7, because the trio is soft. I rejected both:
- Ex. III-7 stays within about a ninth of the open A, and T reaches A6, beyond the A string for a section (c121, c301).
- An octave lower would merge Vln I with Vln II and Cl 1 and lose the one place the trio opens to the high register. c344 saved that register for this bar.
- Vln II's lower octave on the D and A strings (PDF 75) already supplies the warmth, so the climax has brilliance above and warmth below.

### 5. The second idea against the reading's lyrical models. Kept.
Every lyrical example in the chapter leads into its long notes with a short-note figure (Ex. III-5, 6, 7, 39, 59). The second idea has one such figure, the dotted lead-on in bars 4 and 8, which c031 took from this chapter. The rest is hemiola minims. I considered two ways to add one and rejected both:
- A pickup before 45. It would sit on the last beat of the counterstatement's half cadence (41–44) and blur it.
- Splitting a minim. This breaks the three-minim hemiola, which c361 rejected.
The tune works the way Schumann's Rhenish opening does: long notes across the bar, one dotted figure. Ex. III-4 (Brahms 1/iv) also holds long notes across the bar with few ornaments. No change. The theme's remaining weakness is that it is heard only three times. That is a form question, not one this chapter answers.

### 6. Two solo violins at 349. Kept.
Adler PDF 81: two soloists are the first stand. The marking "div.: 2 soli / gli altri", pp on the E string up to G♯6, is the "clear and luminous" soft E string (PDF 75). Compare Ex. III-20, Brahms 1/ii, where the solo violin colours a late return of the main theme.

### Memorability verdict
- **Theme A** works like Beethoven 9/ii and the head of Dvořák 9/iii: its rhythm alone (motive x) identifies it, and the timpani prove it.
- **T** has, since c321–c331, a leap and a suspension in its first phrase, a held second-phrase peak and a tied A climax. It is heard in four colourings across the trio and in the coda.
- Finding 1 gives its second statement the coupling behind one of the best-remembered string tunes in the repertoire, without changing a note.
- **The second idea** is still the least memorable idea, for the reason given in finding 5.
