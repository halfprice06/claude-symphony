## Holistic verdict against Sibelius 1/iii

Several features would already look familiar to a conductor who knows Sibelius 1:
- Both movements are minor-key symphonic scherzi with an E-major trio.
- Pizzicato and the timpani carry the scherzo's rhythm. Here that is motive x at 9–12 and 20/28, and the strokes of the close at 363–371.
- The trio is slower and pastoral, with horns.

Sibelius ends his scherzo with a short loud stroke. Ours fades to the horn's G♯. The fade is the right choice for this work: the cyclic frame (G♯ = movement II's A♭, and the finale's first sound) depends on it, and HISTORY protects it.

Where Sibelius's trio is plainly richer is the inside of its texture. Its wind solos have other wind lines moving under them. Our trio's middle part (185–192) had a solo oboe call and a solo cello answer over harp off-beats and pizzicato roots. Nothing in between moved. The reading supports filling exactly that gap: in Ex. 21-1 Mozart puts a wind line of tied, displaced notes against the subject. That is finding 1.

## Findings

### 1. 185–192: a suspension line under the call and response (applied)
**Problem.** For eight bars the only moving voices are the Ob 1 call (185–188) and the cellos' answer (189–192). The harp chords on beats 2–3 and the plucked root on beat 1 repeat the same rhythm in every bar. These are the trio's only bars where the accompaniment is pure pulse with no held or moving inner voice, and the phrase sounds thin after the full texture of 169–184.

**Evidence.**
- A&S PDF 346 (Ex. 21-1, Jupiter iv): a tied line that enters off the beat becomes a counterpoint, and its held tones turn into dissonances against the next harmony (4–3, 7, 9–8).
- PDF 348 sec. 4: suspensions bring an inner voice into prominence, the dissonance accented and the resolution softer.
- PDF 347 sec. 2: the suspension is strong against its release.

**Fix (in /tmp/critic-489/work).** In sections/tB.ily, Bsn 1 plays pp dolce from 185 under \partCombineSoloI (Bsn 2's solo directive moves to 185):

```
tBBsnOne = { \partCombineSoloI r4 gis2~(\pp^\markup \italic "dolce" | gis4 e2~ | e2.~ | e4 dis2) | r4 dis2~( | dis2.~ | dis2.~ | dis4 cis2) | a2.\p\< | ...
```

How the line works against the harmony:
- G♯3 and E3 are chord tones of C♯ minor (185–186).
- E3 is held through 187 as the 7th of iv (F♯ minor), prepared.
- Over the V (G♯) at 188, E3 becomes the ♭6 and falls to D♯3 on beat 2: a 6–5.
- The answer has the same shape a step lower. D♯3 (the 3rd of B) is held over IV (E) at 191 and into V of V (F♯) at 192, where it falls as 6–5 to C♯3.
- The line then runs straight into Bsn 1's existing entry, A3 at 193.

The line sits inside the harp chord's register (G♯3–E4) and below the cellos. Every dissonance falls on a downbeat, and the harp's chord sounds on the resolution beat.

The call, the answer, c302's sequence and the 196 breath are unchanged. This adds a counter-line, not a unison doubling of the cellos, so c427's aim (no colour doubling the answer at the unison) still holds. An earlier version with F♯3 at 189 made a cross-instrument octave in the analyser at 190; the held D♯3 removes it.

### 3. The second idea has no accented dissonance before its cadence (not applied; reason)
In 45–52 every downbeat of the tune is a chord tone: D/G, B/G6, F♯/D, E/E, E/A, C♯/A, G/A7 (a passing 7th), F♯/D. Schumann's Ex. 20-1 (PDF 327–328) gets its character from accented dissonances. This is the reading's best argument on the "least memorable theme" point that c401, c409 and c431 left open.

I tried one candidate on paper: bass E under the F♯6 minim at 47, making a 9–8 into the E on beat 3. I rejected it:
- It replaces the stepwise bass G–B–D–E (c032, c389) with a static E across 47–48.
- In the five-octave doubling of the tune the resolution tone E would sound against the F♯ in the inner voices (A&S PDF 348; c442's scan).
- The same change would be needed in the E-minor statements, where 123 is V with D♯ as a chord tone.

The tune keeps its single 9–8 at the cadence.

### 4. The G♯/G♮ frame against Unit 30 and Unit 32 (no change)
Chopin's Op. 28/9 (Ex. 30-1) is in E major, the trio's key, and sets G♯ major against G major as a motive before V–I. Widmung (Ex. 32-3) holds A♭/G♯ across a move by a third.

The movement does both with one pitch:
- Hn 1 G♯4 at 1–2.
- G♮ at 7–8.
- G→G♯ at 141–145.
- G♯→G at 241.

c389 tried a G♯-major triad in the coda's cadence and found it does not fit T's B♮ and C♯→D♯. That still holds.
