## Findings

### 1. T's half cadence (bars 7–8 of the tune) is the only phrase ending without a held note over the bar line. Applied.
Bars: 159–160 (Cl 1), 175–176 (Vc), 215–216 (Cl 1, Vln I, Vln II).

Problem: T has four phrases, and three of them are built on a note tied over the bar line:
- bars 3–4: the 4–3 on A–G♯ (c331);
- bars 10–11: the anticipated A (c082);
- bars 13–14: the cadential 4–3 on E–D♯ (c061).

The second phrase ends `e''4. dis''8 cis''4 | ais'4 b'2`. The leading tone A♯ drops a third onto the downbeat with no preparation. Its rhythm is the same as bar 3 (dotted crotchet, quaver, crotchet), but bar 4's tie is missing. So the half cadence, which closes the antecedent, is the weakest cadence in the tune. It is also the one place where the first half does not answer itself.

Evidence:
- A&S PDF 352 §8: the cadential suspension "enhance[s] the effect of a cadence by emphasizing the leading tone."
- PDF 358 §19 and Ex. 21-24a: a leading tone suspended into the chord of its tonic resolves up (7–8), "especially at a cadence."
- PDF 346 §1: with the suspension, "rhythm rather than melody assumes paramount importance". Here the rhythm is what makes the phrases rhyme.
- PDF 347: the preparation is usually consonant. A♯ on beat 3 of bar 7 is the third of the harmony there (harp C♯–E–F♯ over F♯ at 159; F♯7/A♯ at 215; Vln II's own A♯ at 175). So the bar becomes a full V7 of B with a consonant preparation.

After the change, bars 3–4 and 7–8 have the same rhythm: D♯–F♯–A tied | A–G♯, then E–D♯–A♯ tied | A♯–B. Bar 4 falls 4–3 and bar 8 rises 7–8. Each four-bar half ends on a held dissonance, and the antecedent's two halves answer each other the way the paired halves of Dvořák's and Brahms's trio tunes do.

### 2. The coda's A-major fragment of T (Ob 1/Cl 1, 345–348) keeps the old bars 7–8. Decided: no change.
Applying Finding 1 here would give Ob 1 at 347 `a''4. gis''8 dis''4~ | dis''4 e''2`, with Cl 1 a sixth below (`fis'4~ | fis'4 gis'2`). But Bsn 1's chromatic line D–D♯–E steps to E3 on beat 3 of 347. That would sound the note of resolution under the held D♯5 for a whole beat in a soft wind texture, which PDF 352 §12 calls poor. Rewriting the bassoon line to avoid this would cost more than the change gains in a four-bar hand-over. The coda's full statement of T (Vln I, 349–356) begins at bar 9, so it does not contain bars 7–8.

### 3. The second idea (45–55, 121–131, 277–287): every tied note is consonant. No change.
In the hemiola, the ties (B over G at 45–46, E over E at 47–48, C♯ over A at 49–50, F♯ over D at 51–52) each fall on the strong minim of a temporary 3/2. This is the case of Ex. 21-31 (PDF 362), and it follows the metric rule. None of these ties is dissonant, so the idea has none of the tension that PDF 348 says brings a line forward. This may be part of why it is still recorded as the least memorable idea (c401, c409).

The one reading-based fix I could find is a 9–8 at 47–48: F♯6 held against the bass E. It would replace the tie E–E over the bar line with F♯ held across beat 3, which breaks the tie over the bar line and the hemiola minim that c411 protected. That makes the hemiola weaker, so I did not apply it.

### 4. Theme A's peak at 24 (C6 over B, falling to B5). No change.
The C6 is an accented dissonance (♭9) that falls a step at once. It is an appoggiatura, not a suspension, and that is right here: it comes after a rising run (F♯–G–A–B), and a held preparation would stop the run. PDF 348's rule (an accented dissonance resolves down by step) is met.

### 5. Harp melody 73–80 and Cl 1 dolce 81–88. No change.
Every long note is a chord tone or the 7th of a dominant (B♭6 over C7/G at 76), and every 7th resolves down. The dissonances against the Hn 3·4/bassoon chords are carried by the harmony, not by suspensions. There is nothing here for Unit 21 to fix.
