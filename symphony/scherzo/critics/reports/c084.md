## What else I studied

Rimsky-Korsakov, *Principles of Orchestration*: "Played forte, the horns are more powerful than the wood-wind", and balance in difficult cases is secured "by judicious dynamic grading, marking the wood-wind one degree louder than the brass" (rimsky-principles PDF 108 and 112). Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, on the flute's low register: a register called weak is a warning not to "cover" the instrument, "for it is physically impossible for the player to extract more volume out of his instrument in that register" (adler1 PDF 185). ## Findings

### 1. Second idea, 45–56: Hn 1 holds D5 at f on the first note of the tune's lower octave. Applied.

**Problem.** At 45 the second idea starts with an accented D5 in Ob 1 (mf) and Vln II (f), the tune's lower octave. Hn 1 holds the same D5 for two bars at f, then sits in that octave (A4–B4) to 56. Hn 2 holds G3 f. So the horns are marked at the tune's level, and Hn 1 is in the tune's register. The oboe is a full degree softer than the horn on the same note. At 45 the oboe disappears into the horn, and the lower octave of the tune sounds like horn and violins. The same happens to the clarinets' inner thirds at 49–52.

**Evidence.** Mahler marks his distant trumpets ppp under woodwind pp so that the sound heard in the hall comes out right, not the effort (Casanelles PDF 271–272, Fig. 6.1). Loud brass have "dissonant upper partials as the loudest components" (PDF 275), and denser sounds come to the front (PDF 289). Sounds in one band mask each other, and the composer may keep brass out of a woodwind line's band for that reason (PDF 287). Rimsky-Korsakov says the same thing: forte horns are stronger than the woodwind, so mark the woodwind a degree louder (PDF 108, 112). Cycle 47 applied this rule at 197 (horns mf under woodwind f), and this score does it at 209 (Hn 1·2 p under T f). Bar 45 is the one f string tune where the horns still match the tune's level.

**Fix (applied).** sA.ily: Hn 1 B4 instead of D5 at 45–46, so it no longer doubles the tune's accented note (it joins Cl 1's B4 instead), and Hn 1·2 mf. The horns' dotted minims still set the bar against the hemiola.
```
sAHnOne = { ... | d'4 r4 r4 | b'2.\mf | b'2. | a'2. | b'2. | ...
sAHnTwo = { ... | a4 r4 r4 | g2.\mf | g2. | fis2. | ...
```
Both horns get the same dynamic, so the shared staff prints one mf. The harmony stays G major (G3–B4). 121 and 277 stay as they are: there the horns are at sub. p with the tune, and soft horns blend (principle 12 applies to forte brass).

### 2. Trio forte, 197–200: Fl 2 holds C♯5–B♯4 at f, in the flute's weak register, inside six other parts. Applied.

**Problem.** At the trio's only forte, Fl 1 plays the oboe's call at E6 with Vln I. Fl 2 holds C♯5 for three bars and B♯4 for one, f. In that band are Ob 1 (E5–G♯4), Ob 2, Cl 1 and the violas (T at G♯4–B4), Hn 1·2 mf, and Vln II. C♯ and B♯ are already in Cl 2, Bsn 1 and the bass, so Fl 2 adds no harmony. Its sound adds nothing either, because the flute's low fundamental is weak and is covered by the reeds and horns.

**Evidence.** "The flute's weak fundamental tone in its low register" (Casanelles PDF 275). Same-band masking (PDF 287). A flute in unison with the violins adds its high partials to the line (PDF 277–278). Adler: a weak register cannot be made louder by the player, so it must not be covered (adler1 PDF 185). Cycles 36 and 78 moved the flutes out of this register in the other tuttis ("flutes a2 in the upper octave"). This bar was missed.

**Fix (applied).** tB.ily: Fl 2 plays the call a2 with Fl 1 at E6, so the top octave of the trio's climax has two flutes over Vln I.
```
tBFluteTwo = { R2.*12 | e'''4(\f dis'''8 cis'''8 dis'''8 e'''8 | cis'''2 gis''4) | a''4.( b''8 cis'''4) | gis''2.\> | R2.\! | ...
```
The part combiner prints "a2" at 197 (checked on page 7). Fl 2 then rests 201–204 before its pp motto-tail entry at 205, as before.
