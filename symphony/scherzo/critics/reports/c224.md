## Findings

### 1. Bars 133–136 and 321–323: the cor anglais holds B3, the top of its lowest fifth, in a p chord that dies away (applied)

**Problem.** In the closing bars of the scherzo section (133–136) and of the reprise (321–323), the winds hold an E-minor chord, p with a diminuendo to nothing, while the cellos play motive x: Bsn 1 E3, C.a. B3, Cl 2 E4, Cl 1 G4. B3 is the cor anglais's written F♯4, the top note of its lowest fifth.

**Evidence.** Adler's second rule for orchestral chords (adler2 PDF 159) is to "assign pitches to the instruments in their best registral positions so that the notes may be performed at the desired dynamic". Adler part 1 says the lowest fifth of the oboe family is thick and cannot be played softly (adler1 PDF 198, 205). On those grounds c221 moved the cor anglais from B3 to B4 at 269–271, where the same four instruments hold the same chord. 133–136 and 321–323 were not changed with it. In the MIDI the cor anglais at 133 plays at velocity 53 against 37 for the clarinets, so the instrument that is meant to fade is the strongest voice in the chord. Adler's Debussy example makes the same point (PDF 164–165): a chord that has to die away goes to instruments that can control the diminuendo. A cor anglais in its middle register can do this; at the bottom of its range it cannot.

**Fix (applied).** sC.ily, `sCCorAnglais`: `b2.\p | b2. | b2.\> | b2.\!` → `b'2.\p | b'2. | b'2.\> | b'2.\!`. dC.ily, `dCCorAnglais`: `b2.\p | b2. | b2.\> | R2.\!` → `b'2.\p | b'2.\> | b'2 r4\! | R2.`. The chord is now laid out as at 269: C.a. B4 above the clarinet pair, Bsn 1 below. That is a superposed order, which is correct (Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 91–93). It keeps c094's aim of not enclosing the cor anglais between the clarinets, because it now lies above them. At 323 the cor anglais releases on beat 3, when the cellos' G2 enters, and the clarinets and bassoon fade out to the bar line. Without this, the held B4 is the top voice when the bass moves E2 → B1 into 324, and the analyser reported that as fifths (B4/E2 → F♯2/B1). Releasing a beat early removes the report and costs nothing audible inside a diminuendo. No other part, rhythm or dynamic changes.

### Checked and kept (no change)

2. **Bars 129–130 and 285–286, timpani roll f.** Bloch's example (PDF 184) warns that a loud roll covers themes that are being combined. This roll is a two-bar cadential roll on the dominant under a homophonic f cadence. No themes are combined there, and the rolls that lead into a build (101, 301, 332) already start soft. Kept.

3. **Bar 361–364, Fl 1 G♯5 pp alone at the top, an octave above Vln II and the horns.** This is not the dangerous high chord of PDF 162. G♯5 lies in the flute's easy middle register, where pp is secure. The octave gap is at the top, and the protected last page (357–376) depends on this G♯ reappearing in Vln I at 369. Kept.

4. **Bars 101–104, held winds (Fl 2 and Ob 1 F♯5, Ob 2 D♯5, Cl 1 A5) in the register of the hemiola melody (Vln I and Fl 1 B5–A5–F♯5).** Principle 1 would ask for separation. The melody, however, is carried by Vln I/II, Fl 1, C.a. and violas with accents and a crescendo to ff, and the held notes make Tchaikovsky's "punctuation" chord (PDF 166–167). Tpt 1 D♯5 is doubled by Ob 2, Weber's preferred affinity (PDF 161). c184 and c187 settled this voicing. Kept.

5. **Bars 45–56, Fl 1 with Vln I and Ob 1 with Vln II at pitch for 12 bars.** This is the longest at-pitch doubling outside the tuttis (Principle 15). It is a forte melody where the doubling is for power. The returns are coloured differently (121 sub. p; 277 adds its doublings one at a time, c044, following PDF 170–172). c120 limits unison winds to two per string section. Kept.
