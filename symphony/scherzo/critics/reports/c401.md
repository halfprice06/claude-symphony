## What else I studied

I listed every woodwind part's range, its extreme notes by bar, and its longest stretches without a rest, using coherence/lyparse.py. I read c311 (the last pass on this chapter under this lens), c321, c331, c381, c391 and c396, so as not to repeat them.

## Findings

No finding needs a score change. The melodies sit where the reading says each instrument is strongest, and every wind solo has its accompaniment one level softer. I looked for the largest improvement this chapter could support, especially for T, which earlier critics called the least memorable idea (c289–c311). The changes since then (c321's falling sixth, c331's tied seventh) answered that, and this chapter gives no stronger reason to change T's notes. Below are the points that came closest, ranked, each with the decision I made.

### 1. T bar 3: the falling sixth now lands on a throat tone (155, 199, 211). Kept; record corrected.
c311 kept "T in Cl 1's clarino at 153" because T then lay entirely in the clarino (written B4–C6). That reason has lapsed. Since c321, bar 3 falls D♯5–F♯4. Concert F♯4 on the A clarinet is written A4, the throat register Adler calls "rather pale" (PDF 210, Ex. VII-56). The slur F♯4–A4 then crosses the break upward (written A4–C5). It is the only note of T outside the clarino. On a B♭ clarinet it would be written G♯4, which is also a throat tone, so a change of instrument does not help. I still keep it, for a different reason: it is a quaver passing note at p dolce, and the drop in colour on the low note of the leap matches its function. The sixth is meant as a dip, not an arrival, and the phrase arrives on the tied A (written C5, clarino). Adler says the break "presents no difficulties whatsoever" for an experienced player (PDF 210). The fix I considered was `dis''4. b'8 a'4~` (a falling fourth to B4, written D5, clarino). I rejected it because it gives up the sixth that c321 chose and that is now quoted in movement I's record. The do-not-touch wording should now read: "T in Cl 1 at 153, clarino except the bar-3 quaver."

### 2. Cl 1 dolce 81–88 on the A clarinet in F major. Kept.
Adler advises the B♭ clarinet for flat keys (PDF 210). On the A clarinet this passage is written in A♭ major, and its second half (85–88: concert A4–F4–F4–A4, C5–B4–B♭4, A4–F♯4–F♯4–D♯4, E4) lies mostly in the throat (written A♭4, G4, A4, F♯4) and crosses the break at 83, 85 and 87. A B♭ clarinet would put concert A4 and C5 above the break (written B4, D5). But it would need a change of instrument twice in a fast movement (A at 45–72, B♭ at 81, A again by 101), with only eight bars of rest before 81. The pale throat colour also comes at the diminuendo to pp into the vii°7 at 88, where the phrase should fade. This passage is also the clarinet's lyrical transformation of motive x and the chromatic tail (C5–B4–B♭4 at 86). It is the movement's clearest example of theme A being developed, and its notes should stay. Kept, as c311 decided.

### 3. A second-clarinet partner for T's first statement (153–168), after the Mozart 39 Trio. Considered, rejected.
The chapter's only trio example (Ex. VII-64, PDF 213) gives the second clarinet running chalumeau arpeggios under the first clarinet's tune. That is Adler's "independent parts in different registers" (PDF 212), and it is part of why that trio is remembered. I worked out a Cl 2 line for 153–156: `e4( gis8 b e' b | e gis b e' b gis) | fis( a b dis' b a | e gis b e' b gis)`, written E3–E4 sounding, in the chalumeau. I decided against it. The harp's struck off-beats over the Hn 3·4 drone already supply the moving accompaniment in that trio texture, so a Cl 2 figure would double the harp's harmonies in a second rhythm. The trio's model here is the still, soft trio (Eroica, Dvořák 8/iii; BRIEF.md), not a ländler with running accompaniment. The first statement is also the plain version that the cellos (169), Cl 1 with violas (197) and the forte return (209) then recolour. Adler's own point, that the clarinet should do what no other instrument can, is already met by T in the clarino and the chalumeau notes of Cl 2 at 87–88.

### 4. No low-flute melody anywhere (lowest note B4). Kept.
Adler's two most beautiful flute examples are in the low register over a thin accompaniment (PDF 186). The second of them, Dvořák 9/i, is an E-minor symphony's G-major tune. The scherzo's thin passages (73–88, 153–168, 225–236, 341–376) all carry protected melodies in other colours. A low flute in any of them would add a fourth colour to a passage that already has its colour. The low flute is better kept for the finale. Kept.

### 5. The trio call and answer: Ob 1 (185) and C.a. with the cellos (189). Follows.
Ob 1 E5–F♯5–E5–D♯5–E5 | C♯5–G♯4 | A4–B4–C♯5 | G♯4 lies in the oboe's "warm, prominent" range (PDF 198, Ex. VII-32), at p, with rests on both sides (PDF 198: frequent rests). The answer is written C♯4–A♯4 on the cor anglais, in its "richly beautiful" lowest sixth (PDF 205), with the cellos in unison. The cellos lead and the C.a. adds the reed edge, which is the oboe family's doubling role (PDF 201). The call's turn is the trio's second memorable figure and the clearest example of the chapter's "personality" principle in the movement. Follows.

### 6. Theme A's woodwind colours: Fl 1/Ob 1 unison (29), Fl 1 heterophony (253, 305), Bsn 1 "scherz." (269). Follows.
Theme A is the movement's tune, and its rhythm alone (motive x) identifies it. Each wind statement gives it a different personality. The flute and oboe unison at E5 has the oboe's edge, softened by the flute (PDF 185, 201). The flute's quaver variant at 305–320 uses its agility (PDF 184, 187). The bassoon's staccato at 269 is the comic bassoon of PDF 226, and it is the same device as Dukas's bassoon scherzo theme (Ex. VII-95). Follows.

### 7. The second idea: Fl 1 D6–A6, Ob 1 an octave lower, with Vln I (45–56, 121–132, 277–288). Follows.
The flute is in its "clear and brilliant" register at mf–f, the oboe in its most effective range, and the oboe doubles a significant line without leading it (PDF 185, 198, 201). Fl 1 plays 37–56 with only a crotchet rest at 44. Its legato slurs are four bars long, under three seconds at 2. = 88, so the player can breathe between slurs at 49 and 53 (PDF 188). The idea is well made but less memorable than theme A or T: eight bars of hemiola minims make a texture more than a tune. This reading is about register and colour, and it gives no new reason to change notes that c321 and c361 kept because every rewrite lost the rising sixth or the hemiola.

### 8. The coda's return of T (341–356): Hn 3 → Ob 1 with Cl 1 → two solo violins. Follows.
Ob 1 at 345–348 (D5–B5 in A major) is in its warm middle register at p. Cl 1 is a sixth below, mostly in the clarino (its F♯4 at 345 and 348 is an inner-voice throat note). The harp and violas are pp/ppp below both (PDF 185, 198, 212). Handing the tune from colour to colour makes the last statement sound like a recollection rather than a repeat. Follows.

### Memorability verdict
- **Theme A**: the movement's tune; the timpani alone at 9 prove it can be recognised from its rhythm.
- **T**: singable, with a leap of its own in bar 3, a held climax A5 and a 4–3 cadence. It is the movement's second most memorable idea.
- **Trio call (185)**: short and memorable because of its turn.
- **Second idea**: effective in context, the least memorable of the four.
- **Harp melody 73–80 and Cl 1 81–88**: motive x turned lyrical; unchanged.
