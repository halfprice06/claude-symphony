## What else I studied

I compared the scherzo's woodwind melodies with the solo examples in the chapter that are closest to them: the clarinet trio of Mozart 39/iii (Ex. VII-64, PDF 213), the low-clarinet motto of Tchaikovsky 5 (Ex. VII-59, PDF 211), the low flute tune of Dvořák 9/i (Ex. VII-3, PDF 186), the cor anglais solo of Berlioz's *Roman Carnival* (Ex. VII-48, PDF 205) and the oboe solo of Schumann 2/iii (Ex. VII-34, PDF 199). I listed every woodwind part's range and register use with the coherence parser, and I read c291, c301 (the last two melody critics) and c306 (playability, same chapter) so as not to repeat them.

## Findings

No finding needs a score change. The woodwind melodies are placed where the reading says each instrument is strongest, and every accompaniment under a wind solo is marked one level softer. Below are the six points that came closest. I tested each against the reading and decided to keep the score as it is.

### 1. T's first presentation: Cl 1 alone in the clarino (153–168). Kept.
T in the A clarinet is written B4–C6. This is exactly the clarino register, which Adler calls "bright, incisive, expressive" (PDF 210, Ex. VII-56). It starts on written B4, the first note above the break, so no note of the tune falls in the pale throat register. Its peak (concert A5, written C6) is the last note before the "piercing, shrill" top. It is a sharp key on the A clarinet, as Adler advises (PDF 210). The accompaniment is pp under p dolce (harp, Hn 3·4 drone, pizz. basses), so the solo is not covered (PDF 185). Mozart's 39 trio (Ex. VII-64) gives the second clarinet running chalumeau figures under the tune, and that pair of clarinet colours is part of why the tune is remembered. I considered giving Cl 2 a chalumeau line under T at 153. I decided against it: the first statement is the plain reference that the later statements recolour (c090, c099; do-not-touch "trio first statement 153–168"), and the harp-and-drone musette already gives it its own colour. The memorability gap c299/c301 named (T's first phrase is a filled-in horn call with no leap of its own) is real. This reading is about register and colour, not invention, so it gives no basis for changing T's notes. Its head G♯4–A4–B4–E5 is also quoted by movement I.

### 2. Cl 1 dolce 81–88 ends in the throat register and crosses the break. Kept.
Written for the A clarinet, the second half of the F-major episode answer is C5–A♭4–A♭4–C5 (85), C5–A4–A4–F♯4 (87) and G4 (88). Its last notes lie in the throat register, which Adler calls "rather pale" (PDF 210), and it crosses the break at 83, 85 and 87. Adler warns about the break but adds that for an experienced player it "presents no difficulties whatsoever" (PDF 210). The pale throat colour coincides with the diminuendo to pp into the A♯°7 at 88, which is where the phrase should lose colour. A B♭ clarinet would not avoid the break (concert G4/B♭4 is written A4/C5 on it) and would need a muta in a fast movement. Earlier critics kept 81–88 for the same passage (HISTORY: "clarinet break 81–88" kept; the F-major episode 73–88 is do-not-touch). Kept.

### 3. The flute's low register never carries a melody (lowest note B4). Kept.
Adler's most memorable flute examples on PDF 186 are in the low register: Dvořák 9/i (D4–B4) and Brahms 4/iv, "thanks to the thin-textured accompaniment". The scherzo never takes the flute below B4. The passages thin enough for a low flute (the F-major episode 73–88, the trio 153–168, the codetta 225–236, the closing page) all carry protected melodies in other instruments. Adding a low-flute line would be a new colour, not a fix, and it would compete with the harp, the clarinet or the horn. Kept. The low flute stays free for the finale.

### 4. Theme A's first wind statement: Fl 1 and Ob 1 in unison at E5 (29–36). Kept.
Between B4 and A5 the flute is in its "sweet, with little carrying power" register (PDF 185, Ex. VII-2). In unison with the oboe it adds body but little colour, and the oboe leads (PDF 201: the oboe adds "cutting edge to any instrument with which it is combined"). Here that is right: the winds are alone (strings rest on the wind bars), the oboe is the colour that answers the violins, and the flute softens its edge. The movement uses three different woodwind colours for theme A across the statements: the unison call at 29, Fl 1 alone in its brilliant upper octave as heterophony at 253, and Bsn 1 "scherz." at 269. The last is the comic bassoon staccato of PDF 226. This is the "personality" principle of PDF 184 applied to one theme. Kept.

### 5. Contrabassoon above its lowest twelfth at 213–220 (sounding G♯2–C♯3). Kept.
Adler (PDF 230) advises against the contrabassoon's higher register, where it becomes "just another bassoon, a little weaker and paler". At 213–220 it doubles the basses an octave higher under T's forte return (c115), so it adds body to the bass rather than a melody. A weaker, paler doubling is what that job needs, and the "octave above the bass filled in every tutti" is protected. It is not a melodic line, so it is outside this lens. Kept.

### 6. The codetta relay of T's head: Ob 1 225, Cl 1 229, Bsn 1 233. Follows; noted as the model.
The same five-note figure passes down through three woodwinds an octave apart, each in its best register. The oboe is at G♯5–C♯6 p, the "thin, softly piercing" C6 range that Adler says is "sometimes most beautifully scored" (PDF 198). The clarinet is in the clarino (written B4–E5). The bassoon is at G♯3–C4, its "sweet, more subdued but expressive" register (PDF 226, Ex. VII-89), with the C♮ of iv as the last colour. Each time, the strings move out of the soloist's octave (the violas drop to G♯3 under the clarinet and return to G♯4 over the bassoon), and the accompaniment is pp under p. Adler's warning that an accompanied bassoon "gets swallowed" (PDF 226) is answered by both of these. This passage shows best how the score uses the chapter's principle that each instrument should get a line only it could play.

### Memorability verdict (lens)
- **Theme A** is the movement's tune. Its rhythm alone identifies it, as the timpani show at 9. Its colour changes at each wind statement (points 4 and 6), which helps a listener recognise it again, as Beethoven does with the 9/ii subject in the winds.
- **The second idea** is sung by Vln I and Fl 1 in the flute's clear upper register (D6–A6), with Ob 1 an octave lower in its most effective range (PDF 185, 198). That is the right colour for the exposition's loudest lyrical idea. It works by sequence and hemiola, not by a striking contour. It is well made but not as memorable as theme A.
- **T** is singable and well paced, and the clarinet is the right voice for it. Its first phrase is the least striking idea in the movement (point 1).
- **The harp melody 73–80** and **the coda's shared return of T (341–356)** stay as they are. The Ob 1 portion at 345 (D5–E5, F♯5–B5, A5–G♯5–F♯5–D♯5–E5) lies in the oboe's warm middle register (PDF 198).
