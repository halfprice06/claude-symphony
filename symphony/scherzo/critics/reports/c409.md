## What else I studied

They describe the movement as follows:
- a 3/4 scherzo in F;

- a heavy, stamping 2/4 trio;
- the trio appears twice, and the last return of the scherzo is shortened and speeds up.

## Holistic comparison with Beethoven 6/iii

**Shared methods.** Beethoven's humour comes from a tune set against a layer that keeps the bar: the oboe's syncopation over off-beat violins, and the bassoon that knows three notes. This scherzo uses the same means in its own terms:
- theme A over off-beat strings and pizz. downbeats (Rimsky Ex. 1, row 2);
- the second idea's hemiola against horns that restrike each downbeat at mf (45–52);
- the trio tune in a solo clarinet over harp off-beats and a horn drone (153), which is Beethoven's village-band set-up made soft and Rimsky's Ex. 40 almost note for note.

Beethoven passes his tune oboe → clarinet → horn and keeps the accompaniment the same. This score passes T clarinet → cellos → violins in the trio, and horn → oboe → violins in the coda (341–356). It also shortens and speeds up the last scherzo return (305–324, Più mosso at 325), as Beethoven does.

**Where Beethoven is plainer.** Beethoven's tunes are shorter and more square than T and the second idea. That is the open point every holistic critic has raised (c289–c401). The assigned reading is about scoring, not melody, so it gives me no ground to rewrite either tune.

**Would a conductor believe a master wrote this?** For craft and scoring, yes. Every principle in the table is followed apart from one, the background of the cellos' T (row 5), and that one is a matter of colour.

## v399 → v409: did anything get worse?

No. The six changes:
- **81, harp pp (c399).** Better: the harp now accompanies Cl 1's p a level under it.
- **101–104, woodwinds p< (c408).** Better: the build now adds strings, then woodwind, then brass, as at 301–304.
- **169–184, Vln II quaver arpeggio (c407).** Better in rhythm. Its colour is the subject of Finding 1.
- **197–200, call a level under T (c404).** Neutral to better.
- **209, Hn 1·2 mp (c406).** Neutral.
- **193, "unis." removed (c407).** Correct.

## Findings

### 1. Vln II con sord. under the cellos' trio tune, 169–184 (APPLIED)

**Bars:** 169–184 (tA.ily, tAVlnTwo); "senza sord." at 193 (tB.ily, tBVlnTwo).

**Problem.** At 169 the trio tune moves to the cellos (mp espress.). Vln II plays a slurred quaver arpeggio above them (pp dolce, c407), and Fl 1 answers in the held notes. Vln II and the cellos are the same family, both unmuted. Only the level separates the tune from its background: mp against pp, which Rimsky calls the "artificial" means (PDF 54). The Vln II figure lies at B4–G♯5, just above the cellos' climb to A4. The two lines differ in rhythm (c407) but not in colour.

**Evidence.**
- Rimsky PDF 54: a melody is set in relief "naturally" by "selection and contrast of timbres".
- Ex. 7 (PDF 180–181), cited at PDF 56 for the cellos' "broad melody dolce ed espressivo": the cellos are unmuted, and Vln II and violas play a figuration around them pp **con sord.** over harp and muted horns. That is the layout of 169–184, with the mute added.
- PDF 55 (Ex. 6, Antar): the mute gives "a dull, ethereal quality of tone".
- Ex. 13 and 16 (PDF 187, 190): muted strings again stand behind a woodwind or cello line.
- Beethoven's own Pastoral (ii) uses muted solo cellos inside murmuring strings for the same reason, although that is outside the scherzo.

**Effect.** The cellos' warm open tone stands out against a veiled shimmer, so the tune no longer depends on the players holding mp against pp. The strings that follow are unmuted:
- the violas, which join the cellos in unison at 177;
- Vln II itself from 193 (p<, then T f espress. at 197).

So the return to open strings marks the trio's build.

**Time to mute and unmute.**
- Vln II rests from 133 to 168: 36 bars, over 25 seconds.
- It rests 185–192 before it plays again: 8 bars at 2. = 76, about 6.3 seconds. Removing a mute takes 2–3 seconds.

**Cyclic notes.** cyclic-notes.md records muted strings in movement II (15–30) as "do not present as new". Here the mute is a background colour for 16 bars, not a featured effect. No cyclic rule reserves string mutes for the finale.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-409/work):**

        new: tAVlnTwo = { R2.*16 | r8 b'8(\pp^\markup \italic "con sord., dolce" e''8 ...

        new: ... R2. | fis'2(\p\<^\markup \italic "senza sord." gis'4 | ...

I added a two-line c409 comment above tAVlnTwo. I kept pp, as in Rimsky's Ex. 7, so the levels are unchanged.

**Why this is the best candidate and why I keep it.** I looked for the change in this reading that a listener would hear most, and weighed four candidates:
- **The mute (applied).** It is the one place where the score departs from an example's layout that it otherwise matches note for note.
- **Fl 1 off the Ob 1 call at 29–34 (row 20, a solo oboe as in Beethoven's village band).** It is protected (c054, c311, c324, c302) for register reasons that still hold.
- **Vln I an octave over the cellos at 177–184 (the second half of Ex. 7).** It would spend the violins' entry that c344 saves for 193/209 and undo c391.
- **A 3/4 pulse figure under the second idea at 45–52.** Not needed: the horns already restrike each downbeat at mf.

The mute breaks no rule or protection, so I keep it.

## Considered and not changed

- **Theme A's call at 29–34, Fl 1 + Ob 1 in unison.** Rimsky (PDF 66–67) would give a soft solo phrase to one instrument. The protection (c054: no early A6; call and response kept by c007, c302, c311) answers that, and the unison sits in the flute's weaker register where the oboe leads. Kept.
- **The second idea's homorhythmic block, 45–52.** The melody, violas and bass all move in the hemiola. Rimsky's forte cantabile examples (Ex. 3, 8, 29, 32) give the accompaniment its own figure, but the horns already keep the bar. The passage is also protected (c302, c314, c389). Kept.
- **Cb pizz. without cellos at 341–348 (row 6).** c309's reasoning still holds. Kept.
