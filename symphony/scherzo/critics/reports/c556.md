## Findings

Seven passes of this chapter have left the string writing in good order: ranges, stops, skips, clefs, divisi, bass writing and the solo all follow the reading (table). The one place the reading still applies is Ex. III-7: a soft lower violin octave whose top notes cross to the E string under a top octave already on the E string. It is the change a listener would notice most among those the chapter supports, because it changes the colour of a theme's middle octave throughout two whole phrases. I applied it.

### 1. Vln II "sul A" on the soft E-minor second idea, 122–128 and 280–284. Applied.

**Bars.** Exposition 121–128 (sC, rehearsal H): sub. p espress., Vln I B5–F♯6, Vln II an octave lower B4–F♯5, violas and cellos holding below, the passage c308/c453 keep undoubled. Reprise 280 (beat 3½)–284 (dA): p espress., Vln I C6–F♯6, Vln II C5–F♯5, cellos C4–F♯4 in the tenor clef.

**Problem.** Unmarked, a section plays the lower octave in first or third position. The phrase peaks (F♯5 minim at 126 and 282; E5 at 122, 127, 283) then cross to the E string, open E5 included, while the rest of the line is on the A string. The middle octave brightens on exactly the notes where it should stay under Vln I, and it takes the same E-string colour as the top octave, so the two violin octaves stop sounding as two colours. In the reprise the cellos sing the same phrase on their A string an octave lower, so Vln II is the middle of three octaves and the E-string notes stand out between them.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 75: "In soft, lyrical, expressive passages, the composer will often prefer the higher pitches to be performed on the A-string rather than have the player cross to the E-string, where these upper notes take on a different, more brilliant timbre." Ex. III-7 (Brahms 3/iii mm. 13–24, p espress., sul A) on the page image is the same kind of line: dotted figures and held notes in one octave above the open A. PDF 73: timbre is the one reason to name a string, and an open string stands out in an expressive passage (the open E5 is removed). PDF 75 also gives the E string "the most ethereal sounds … when one plays softly", which is the colour Vln I keeps on top. The compass A4–F♯5 is a sixth above the open A, inside c076's octave-or-ninth limit (Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 26) and within third/fourth position.

**Answer to c481.** c481 rejected a mark at 121–124 because the G4 at 121 lies below the A string, so "the mark cannot hold for the phrase". In the reprise the whole phrase, from the 280 pickup D5 to the B4 at the end of 284, lies on the A string, so that reason does not apply there. In the exposition the mark starts on the E5 of 122 beat 2, after the tied G4, the same way c466 ended its own mark before a note below the A string at 180. That is the reason c481 gave, answered; it gave no musical reason against the colour itself. The previous passes applied the same rule to the same compass at 177–180 and 349–352 (c466), so all four soft lower-octave violin statements in the movement are now played alike.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-556/work).** Playing word only; no notes, slurs, bowings or dynamics change.

### Tested and kept (decided; no change)

2. **Sul G for Vln II's T at pitch, 209–212 (f espress.).** Ex. III-5 (PDF 74) supports the G string's intensity for a forte cantabile, but on the page image Tchaikovsky's sul G stays within A3–B4, a tenth above the open G. Vln II's line reaches E5 at 210 and F♯5 at 214, a thirteenth and more above G3, beyond Adler's example and Rimsky-Korsakov's ninth (PDF 26). c481 rejected the same mark at 193–200 for the falling sixth on one string. Kept.
3. **Vln II's two-octave skip A3→A5 at 40–41 (f), and Vln I D4→A5 at 41, B3→A5 at 273.** PDF 82–83 lists skips between extreme ranges as a section hazard. Here both notes lie in first position (first finger on the G string, fourth finger on the E string), so the left hand does not move; only the bow crosses, during a quaver rest, off the string. Moving Vln II's off-beats at 40 up an octave would cross above Vln I's F♯4–D4. Kept.
4. **Cellos' open A3 held at 281–282 in the tenor line.** PDF 73–74: an open string stands out in an expressive passage. Cellists take this A3 with the fourth finger on the D string as a matter of course, and PDF 73 leaves fingering to the player. A mark would add ink only. Kept.
5. **Cello–viola coupling (Beethoven 5/ii, Ex. III-39, PDF 91) for the reprise's tenor line at 281–284.** The violas hold the inner chord there, and adding them would thicken a statement that already sounds in three octaves (Vln I, Vln II, cellos). HISTORY also says not to flip 177–184 between violas and Vln I. Kept.
6. **Double stops on the secco cadences 56, 132, 288 (PDF 76: easy double stops "for secco chordal effects").** The strings play octaves there, and the third is already in the clarinets and horns (barall.py). At 56 the bare G octave leads into the G-minor fugato, so a B♮ in the strings would weaken the change to minor. Kept.
7. **"Change bow at will" for the basses (PDF 108).** The longest bass holds are the E2 at 223–224 (5 beats, mf diminuendo) and the pp E2 tied through 361–364 (11 beats, about 2.9 s at 2. = 76). Both fit one bow. Kept.
