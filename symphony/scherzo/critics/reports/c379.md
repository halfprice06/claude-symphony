## What else I studied

**Saint-Saëns 3, second part of movement I.** I worked from my own knowledge of the score and checked the facts against the Cleveland Orchestra and Boston Symphony programme notes and The Listener's Club essay (2021). I claim no bar numbers.
- The Allegro moderato is a C-minor scherzo built on a transformation of the symphony's cyclic motive, driven by strings and timpani.
- Its Presto is the trio. It is faster, not slower, and its special colour, the piano's rapid scales, is heard only there.
- At the end the trio comes back and, in the composer's words, prevails over the "restless, diabolical" element.
- A short pp contrapuntal passage on the motive then leads straight into the finale.

## Holistic comparison with Saint-Saëns 3

Would a conductor believe a master wrote this? Yes, a master in the Dvořák–Brahms line. The two movements share their large design:
- Saint-Saëns's scherzo exists to prepare the finale, and so does this one. It starts on movement II's A♭ as G♯, keeps C major, string tremolo and the brass chorale back for the finale, and leaves a G♯ for the finale to take up.
- In both, the trio material wins at the end. Here T comes back after the coda's ff stretto and G.P. (341–356), and the motto's tail closes in E major (357–360). That is the same dramatic idea as Saint-Saëns's "struggle for mastery".
- Saint-Saëns keeps his special colour (the piano) for the trio. This score does the same with the harp: the F-major episode, the trio and the coda's T, with the last harmonic at 375.

The main differences are decisions recorded earlier, not faults:
- The trio is slower here, not faster (c129). Saint-Saëns's faster trio is the exception among the models (Dvořák 9, Eroica, Brahms), so it does not answer c129's reason.
- There is no link passage into the finale, because the finale's link is written from the scherzo's last chord.

Where the score falls short of Saint-Saëns is detail, not plan. Saint-Saëns's soft passages are scored so each sound is audible. Here, one coda bar group stacks three layers at the same pitch, and one of them cannot be heard (finding 1).

## Findings

### 1. Coda 349–356: Cl 1 rests, so it is not a third same-octave layer and its entry at 361 is fresh (applied)

**Bars:** 349–356, Cl 1.

**Problem:** At 349 T is in the two solo violins (C♯6–A6). The rest of Vln I, Vln II and Cl 1 (ppp) all play it an octave lower, at the same pitch. At pp, a ppp clarinet inside two full violin sections at the same pitch cannot be heard; it only thickens the line. Cl 1 then holds E5 from 361 in the closing wind chord, so its "new" entry there is the same instrument that has just played without being heard.

**Evidence:**
- Adler PDF 229 (Schoenberg, Ex. XVI-14): doubling at pitch with similar colours makes them "swallow each other up".
- PDF 224 (Ravel): change fore- and background colour with each new entrance.
- PDF 228 (Stravinsky, Ex. XVI-13b): lighten a phrase so that the next solo entrance "is very fresh".
- PDF 213 (Bizet): a ppp chord is kept alive by adding a soft new element, not by thickening it.

The coda's own plan already follows these pages: Hn 3 (341), then Ob 1 (345), then the violins (349), each in a new colour. c377 removed the wind–string unisons at 361–364 for the same reason (Adler I PDF 257). The clarinet at 349–356 is the one place left where the plan has a doubling nobody hears.

**Answering the earlier decision:** c367 marked Cl 1 a level under Vln II here. The balance it set no longer exists, because c371 added the rest of Vln I to that octave. The clarinet's tone does not reach through 26 violins at pp, so nothing is lost by resting it.
