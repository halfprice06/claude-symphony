## What else I studied

I studied Bruckner 4/iii from my knowledge of the 1878/80 score, and checked its character and the composer's own programme on the web: the scherzo "represents the hunt" and the trio is "a dance melody played to the hunters during their meal". The trio is a Ländler in G-flat major (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._4_(Bruckner); https://thelistenersclub.com/2021/05/12/bruckners-fourth-symphony-nature-and-the-call-of-the-horn/). The scherzo opens with horn fanfares answered by trumpets, built on the 2+3 "Bruckner rhythm", and builds to a fortissimo for the full brass.

## Holistic comparison with Bruckner 4/iii

Bruckner builds his scherzo from one horn call. He states it pp, answers it between groups, and builds it in long terraced blocks to a full-brass ff, with general pauses between the blocks. The trio is short and soft, in a remote key, and lightly scored.

This scherzo has its own plan (Dvořák 9/iii, Beethoven 9/ii). The cyclic rules take away Bruckner's main resource, brass melody: trumpets only in tutti rhythm or motive x, and no high horn unison. Within those limits it meets his standard where the two do comparable things:
- **Terraced build.** The coda's motive-x build 325–336 enters from the bottom up at pp. It adds one group per two bars, holds the low brass back until 339, and breaks off with a G.P. at 340. Bruckner builds his peaks this way, and stops them dead in the same way before the trio.
- **One motive as the whole rhythm.** Motive x has the same role as Bruckner's 2+3 rhythm. It is the theme, the timpani's solo (9–12, 89–100), the trumpets' only material, and the coda's ostinato.
- **Soft, lightly scored trio.** 153–168 is a solo clarinet over harp off-beats, a horn drone and pizzicato basses. That is the same kind of Ländler texture as Bruckner's trio.
- **A tonal surprise by block.** The F-major episode 73–88 works like Bruckner's sudden key shifts. It is a whole block in a remote key and colour, with no transition passage.

A conductor who knows the Bruckner would not find anything amateurish in the layout of the climaxes, in the brass writing or in the trio's colour. The one place where the scoring sits below this standard is a local balance fault at 197–200 (finding 1).

## Findings

### 1. Bars 197–200: three winds in unison on Vln I overpower it and the trio tune below (applied)

**Problem.** At the f return of the trio's middle part, the call (E6–F♯6–E6–D♯6–E6, then C♯6–G♯5, A5–B5–C♯6, G♯5) lies in Vln I with Fl 1 and Fl 2 a2 (c084), and from 198 Ob 1 as well (c117). The trio tune's head lies below it in Vla espress. f, Vln II f, Cl 1 f and Hn 1 mf. That puts three high winds in unison on one string section. The call then outweighs the tune that c107 and c117 meant to keep audible underneath ("call above, tune below"). The oboe's own colour, which owns this call from 185, is also absorbed into a flute–oboe–violin blend.

**Evidence.**
- Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 76: "If several wind instruments play in unison with one group of strings, the latter will be overpowered." c111 applied this rule at 41–43.
- PDF 66–67: each added unison timbre costs individuality, and same-kind coupling "will gain in power, but its capacity for expression will be diminished".
- PDF 65 (Ex. 52): Fl + Ob in unison gives a quality "fuller than that of the flute, sweeter than that of the oboe", and in the upper register the oboe leads. This is the right blend for the oboe's call.
- In the examples of strings and wind in unison (Ex. 80–83, PDF 253–256), Rimsky puts one wind on one string group (Vla + Cl, Vln + Ob, Vla + C.a.).

### Points considered and not raised

- **Same-kind tritones at the climaxes** (Ob 1·2 D♯5/A4 at 104/304; Cl 1·2 D♯5/A4 at 336–338; Tpt 1·2 A4/D♯4 at 337–338; Fl 1·2 A6/D♯6 at 337). PDF 90 rule 4 gives discords to different colours, "except when discords are to be emphasised". These are dominant sevenths at ff/fff hemiola and coda peaks. Each resolves inside its own pair (Ob 1 D♯→E at 105). The peak voicing is protected (c030, c034, c096).
- **Hn 3·4 open fifth E3–B3 under the trio solo 153–168, and the codetta at 237.** PDF 97 prefers thirds and sixths for the wind's middle parts when the bass is separate. Here the open fifth is a Ländler drone of the kind Bruckner's trio and Dvořák's use. The harp's off-beats fill in the third, and the first trio statement is protected (c089, c109).
- **51: Hn 2 E3 doubling the bass of A7/E** (PDF 83). It is the held inner pedal of 49–52 (PDF 84), inside c032's protected sequence.
- **The bass-to-next-part gap** at the pizzicato downbeats of theme A and in the second idea. In every case horns, violas or octave doublings fill the gap, as in Rimsky's own scherzo-like examples (Ex. 93, 99, 121).
