## Findings

No finding requires a change. Every principle in Adler ch. 3 that this score uses is met, and the five string changes made since c286 stay inside the chapter's limits. The chapter's main lever for improving a score, the choice of string for colour (PDF 73–76, 86–87, 99), is already used as Adler describes: soft high lines lie on the E string where they cannot fit on the A string, loud lines on the E string, viola melodies on the A string next to woodwinds, and the cellos' lines on D and A. I looked for the largest improvement this reading could support and tested five candidates against it. Each would cost more than it gains, so I decided against them. The decisions follow.

### Tested and kept (decided; not requests)

1. **Two solo violins in unison at C♯6–A6, pp, 349–356 (c371).** Adler PDF 72 warns that control gets tenuous above the 7th position. PDF 82 adds that a soloist shifts more accurately than a section. PDF 81 gives two soloists to the first stand, which the label does. The line reaches A6, the top of the 7th position, by step and by a third, and it never goes above that. A single concertmaster (PDF 81) would avoid any unison beating between two players. Rimsky-Korsakov's Ex. 26 (PDF 60) uses exactly two soli at pp for body, and at pp two players on the E string are the "ethereal" sound of PDF 75. Kept as c371 left it.

2. **Cellos D2→D4 with no gap at 60→61 and 292→293.** These are fugato bars: the subject's last staccato D2 on the C string, then the countersubject's legato D4, pp, on the A string. This is Adler's case 2 (PDF 83, III-23: extreme registers with silent string crossings). The left hand stays in first position (D2 first finger, D4 fourth finger), so only the bow crosses. It has a full crotchet (0.23 s) and the dynamic drops to pp, which makes the crossing easier. c286 did not list this skip, so I decided it here: kept.

3. **Basses D2, not D1, at 53–54 (and Cbsn D2).** PDF 108: notes below E1 need the C extension, and players without one play them an octave up. D2 is the open D string, reached by step from C♯2, and the hemiola cadence then falls a fifth to G1. Writing D1 would make the line leap a seventh, and the part would depend on how many extension players there are. Kept.

4. **Vln II's held E5, pp, 229–232.** PDF 73–74: an open string stands out in a slow passage. A section plays a soft held E5 as a fourth finger on the A string as a matter of course, and PDF 73 says to leave fingering to the player unless timbre requires it. No "sul A" is needed. Kept.

5. **The violas' fugato answer at D5–F5 on the A string, p, 61–64 (and 293–296).** PDF 85–86: higher positions mean more stretch and a heavier bow, so light strokes are harder. The answer lies in the third to fourth position and uses one quaver pair a bar. PDF 87 credits the A string with the carrying power such an entry needs. An octave lower it would run into the cellos' countersubject, D4–C4–B♭3. Kept (the fugato is protected, c007, c017).

6. **Cellos' T in tenor clef, 169–184 (c344).** PDF 96 and 99: tenor clef suits G♯3–A4, and the D and A strings are the cello's lyrical and carrying strings. The only shift above the 4th position is to A4 at 178–179, which is reached by step. No string mark is needed (row 5). Kept.

7. **The pizz./arco timing is unchanged since c366.** pizz.→arco always has 4 beats or more (0.91 s at the least). arco→pizz. in one beat happens only at Cb 205/225, each after a marked up-bow (c006, c096, c366). The timpani use G♯2 only at 188–200, between the muta marks at 145 and 225. Kept.
