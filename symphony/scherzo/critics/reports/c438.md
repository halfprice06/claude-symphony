## Findings

### 1. The coda's top octave of T is played by two solo violins in unison (bars 349–356). Applied.

**Problem.** c371 split Vln I at 349 into "div.: 2 soli / gli altri". The two soli play T's last statement alone in the upper octave, C♯6–A6 pp dolce, over the rest of Vln I, Vln II and Cl 1 an octave lower. Two players in unison on an exposed line at the top of the violin register is the case the assigned reading names as the worst doubling. Each player brings their own vibrato and intonation, so at pp the pair beats and is heard as two violinists rather than as one line. Every other voice in this coda carries T as a single player: Hn 3 at 341, Ob 1 at 345 (with Cl 1 and Bsn 1 in other octaves) and Hn 1's motto tail at 357. The top violin line is the one place where the line belongs to a pair.

**Evidence.** Casanelles PDF 147–148: a doubled line can produce "a sound that seems like two distinct entities playing at the same time. This is a common outcome … [with] solo instruments (e.g., individual elements of the woodwind section or solo string moments). This is a known problem with acoustic instruments as well … you should generally never double a line with two instruments (you either use one instrument or three or more)". PDF 148: combining two sources with different vibrato "tends to be a challenge due to phasing". PDF 151–156: the expression of a lyrical line lies in one performer's note transitions ("listening to how the solo violinist plays the first two notes … is almost an open door to the performer's inner feelings"), and two players cannot share one transition.

**Answer to the recorded reasons for keeping it.** c371 relied on Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 59–60. Its reason is that the top octave should be light, so that it does not pierce, and the lower octave should carry the expression. One player meets that reason better than two: the top becomes lighter still and the section an octave lower is unchanged. Rimsky's own Note to Ex. 24 does not prescribe exactly two players ("one or two desks are sufficient"). Ex. 26 is one instance, from an opera with women's chorus doubling the line, not a rule. c376 and c391 (Adler PDF 81: two soloists are the first stand) checked only that the label was correct, and c436 kept the soli for their thin, cool colour. That colour, the thin E-string sound at pp, stays with one player. Rimsky's "the solo desk will be sufficiently prominent owing to the general pianissimo" applies to one player too: the accompaniment is pp/ppp, with pizz. basses and held violas. The alternative, two desks (four players), would meet Casanelles's three-or-more rule as well. I rejected it because it turns the top octave back into a small section, the brighter and heavier sound Rimsky's note wants to avoid, and because it breaks the coda's chain of single solo voices.

### Checked and left as they are
- **Fl 1 + Ob 1 unison at 29–30 and 33–34.** These are two solo winds on one line, which is also the case in row 3. c054 chose the blend for a rounder tone, citing Adler PDF 236 (Ex. VIII-2). Flute and oboe are unlike instruments, so the combination reads as one mixed colour and not as two copies of one sound. Casanelles's warning is about layering the same sound. Kept.
- **Hn 1 + Hn 3 on G♯4 at 365–369.** A pp overlap of a few bars that covers Hn 1's breath (c006, c130, c338). It is not a doubled line. Kept.
- **Repeated notes (round robin, PDF 150, 152).** The closing timpani motive x is already graded pp–pp–ppp near the rim (363–371). The pedal repetitions at 89–100 and 241–252 are graded (c238). The trumpets' repeated motive x quavers sit inside moving ff tuttis, and Dan's soft-brass rule rules out varying them with accents. No change.
