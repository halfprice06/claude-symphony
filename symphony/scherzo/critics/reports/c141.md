## What else I studied

I read v141's score as images (all 13 pages at 90 dpi) and every section file. I ran four scripts on the parsed parts (files in /tmp/critic-141/scan/):
- `shadow.py`: for each of 20 melody statements, counts the unison contacts, parallel thirds and sixths, and crossings of every other part.
- `dead.py`: lists bars in which no part moves after the downbeat.
- `leaps.py`: lists inner-part leaps.
- `dyn.py` and `velcmp.py`: compare the dynamics and MIDI velocities of melody and accompaniment.

I compared the trio with Dvořák 9/iii, Poco sostenuto 68–90 (reference-corpus/pdf/DvorakA_O95__Mvt3_Scherzo.pdf, PDF p. 6). There the oboe moves in triplets while the flute holds (69–70), which is Sebesky's fill, Ex. 286, in a symphony. The oboe also runs in thirds with the flute: a duet of equals, not the echoing background that Ex. 279 warns against. I checked c051 (the first pass of this reading) and the lens-1 reports c091, c101, c111, c121 and c131 so that I would not repeat their points.

## Judgement of the melodies

The reading is about how an arranger presents a melody, and v141 follows it almost everywhere.
- **Theme A** (motive x and the chromatic tail) is the movement's hook. It comes back in four settings: spiccato over pizz. at 13, tutti at 105, with flute heterophony and a sustained cello line at 253, and tutti again at 305.
- **T** now has a held peak in phrase 2 (c121), the tied climax A5 (c081) and a 4–3 cadence (c061). A listener can sing it back after one hearing, as with the Ländler trios of Bruckner and Dvořák 8.
- **The second idea** is the least singable of the three, because it is an exact sequence a step higher. But its hemiola gives it a profile, and it is protected (c001, c027, c032).
- **The harp head 73–80** is a three-step falling sequence, also protected (c001, c080).

Every place where the reading's rules are not met in full has already been weighed by an earlier critic, or the change it calls for is weaker than the reason that protects the passage. I therefore make no score change. The points below are ranked by how close each came to a change, with my decision on each.

## Findings (ranked): none requires a change

### 1. 209–216: Hn 1 holds B4 and then C♯5 inside the lower octave of T (Ex. 277, 279; PDF 210–211). Not applied.
**Observation.** In T's forte return, Hn 1 holds B4 for four bars and then C♯5 for four bars, in thirds and sixths with Hn 2. The scan finds seven unison contacts with the tune's lower octave (Cl 1 and Vln II) at 210, 211, 214, 215, 216, 220 and 221. The horn lies above that octave for 26 half-beats (209, 212, 213, 216). Sebesky would call this a static lead in the background (Ex. 278) that sits on the solo (Ex. 279).
**Decision.** No change.
- Vln I carries T an octave above, so the tune's outline is never in doubt.
- The horns are marked p under an f string-and-clarinet statement, so they are a soft pad rather than a competing line.
- I tested three alternatives on paper, and each one is worse. Inverting the pair (Hn 1 on Hn 2's line, Hn 2 an octave lower) still meets the tune at 209 and 212. Resting the horns until 217 removes the warmth that c004 and c094 gave this return. Any re-pitching at 220→221 creates new octaves with the bass A→B or with Bsn 1. That is the same impasse c092 and c112 reached.
- The thirds and sixths are protected (c094).

### 2. 45–52 and 121–128: Cl 1 moves with the second idea's lower octave in its own rhythm (Ex. 279; PDF 211). Not applied.
**Observation.** Cl 1 plays the hemiola minims a third or sixth from Ob 1/Vln II, with a voice exchange at 45 and 49.
**Decision.** No change. This is a homorhythmic chorale setting of the hemiola, which c027 asked to keep and c109 and c111 weighed. Ex. 279's problem is an echo that lags the solo and blurs it. Here the clarinet moves exactly with the tune and strengthens the cross-rhythm, in the way Dvořák's oboe runs in thirds with the flute at 9/iii, 68–75.

### 3. 229–232: Vln II holds E5 above Cl 1's statement of T's head (Ex. 277; PDF 210, 219). Not applied.
**Observation.** For four bars the texture's top note is a static pp E5, and the moving line (G♯4–A4–B4–C♯5–B4) lies under it.
**Decision.** No change. c047 moved the violins up deliberately, because the earlier B4–A4 voicing put the held notes on the clarinet's own pitches. Sebesky's Ex. 280 allows a background above the soloist if it supplies chord tones and keeps out of the solo's notes, and the E5 does both.

### 4. 205–209 and 357–360: the motto tail is quoted twice (PDF 224). Not applied.
**Decision.** Sebesky's once-only rule is about borrowed quotations used as colour. Here the motto is the symphony's own cyclic material: the brief requires it, and the first statement prepares the last (c023, c050, c110).

### 5. 357–360: the violas move in parallel sixths under the motto tail (Ex. 279; PDF 211). Not applied.
**Decision.** No change. The violas and Bsn 1 (E–C♯–C–B) form a second chromatic line, which is Ex. 272's "chromatic inner melody changes chord quality" and not an echo. The passage is the protected closing page.

### 6. 341–344: the pizz. cellos and basses are p, the same level as Hn 1's p dolce T (PDF 219). Not applied.
**Decision.** No change. The plucked downbeats lie an octave or more below the horn and stop at once. The same p bass sits under every soft solo in the trio (153, 185, 189), and the harp and violas are already pp. In the MIDI, LilyPond's instrument equalizer plays the horn quieter than the cellos. Movement II's critique rejected switching it off (mvt2/v161), because the listening renders use their own balance.
