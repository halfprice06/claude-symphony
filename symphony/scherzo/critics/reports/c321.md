## Findings

### 1. T's first phrase has no figure of its own (bars 155, 171, 199, 211; coda 343). Applied.
Problem: phrase 1 of T is G♯4–A4–B4–E5 | D♯5–C♯5–B4 | A4–G♯4. Its first two bars fill the horn call's sixth (borrowed, and fixed by movement I). The rest is a stepwise descent of five notes. Phrase 2 (157–160) has its held peak F♯5 on beat 2 (c121) and phrase 3 has the tied A5 (c081), but phrase 1 has nothing a listener can hold on to. Critics 289, 299, 301, 309, 311 and 319 said the same.
Evidence: Sebesky PDF 210 (Ex. 277–278): a long-note lead line moving by step, as in Ex. 278, is "much less interesting" than one with a leap, a tie or a rhythmic figure. PDF 206 (Ex. 270): a recurring figure with its own leap and rhythm gives a score its shape, and figures may be derived from a portion of the tune. PDF 220 (question 6): if the melody is weak, find what will overcome the fault. Dvořák 9/iii's E-major oboe tune gets its profile from a leap on a short note.
Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-321/work): bar 3 of T becomes `dis''4. fis'8 b'4` (was `dis''4. cis''8 b'4`). The quaver now falls a major sixth, D♯5–F♯4. This is the horn call's rising sixth G♯3–E4 (bars 3–4) turned downward. Then comes the fourth up to B4, and the phrase closes on A4–G♯4 as before. The result:
- Phrase 1 has a leap and a rhythm of its own.
- The three phrases are now distinct: a plunge on the quaver, a held peak, and a tied peak.
- The falling sixth leaves a gap that phrase 2's rising A–B–C♯ fills.
- B4–A4–G♯4 stays at the pitch of the coda's motto tail, B4–A♯4–A4–G♯4 at 357–360.
- The only thing lost is the C♯ of the old 6–5–4–3 descent.
I checked all five harmonisations:
- 155: V7, harp A–B–D♯.
- 171: V7, violas A–D♯, cellos F♯–A.
- 199: F♯–A–C♯ with the tune's D♯.
- 211: V, with Bsn 1 and Hn 2 on F♯4, so the quaver meets them in unison.
- 343: V7 of A in Hn 3, `gis'4. b8 e'4`.

In every case D♯ and F♯ are chord tones. The beat-3 B was already there. Bar 4 (A–G♯) is unchanged, so 200's A over G♯ still resolves. In the clarinet, the leap goes from written F♯5 to A4 and back to D5, slurred at p. That crosses the break down and up within one beat. It is routine for a professional and lasts a quaver (Adler PDF 210: for an experienced player the break "presents no difficulties whatsoever"), so I kept it.
Statements changed (every one): Cl 1 155, Vln I and Vln II 171, Cl 1, Vln II and violas 199, Cl 1, Vln I and Vln II 211, Hn 3 343 (A major). Files: sections/tA.ily, tB.ily, tC.ily, coda.ily (plus a c321 comment at the top of tA.ily).

### 2. The second idea (45–56, 121–132, 277–288). Not changed.
I looked for a clearly more memorable version and did not find one. Against Sebesky's test (PDF 210), the line already has what Ex. 278 lacks:
- two rising sixths, B–G (46) and C♯–A (50), the horn call's interval;
- minims across the bar line, which make the hemiola;
- dotted lead-ons in bars 4 and 8 (c031);
- a counter-line under it.
Its last four bars are the hemiola cadence protected since cycle 2 (53–55, 129–131). Any rewrite of bars 1–8 that I tried either moved the rising sixth (the link to the horn call) or gave up the minim hemiola that defines it. Neither is clearly better. The memorability gap of the exposition now rests on T, where finding 1 acts. Decided: no change.
