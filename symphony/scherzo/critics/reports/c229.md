## What else I studied

**Beethoven 5/iii.** The corpus has no score of the Fifth, so I worked from my knowledge of the score and from listening guides: LSO (https://lso.shorthandstories.com/beethoven-symphony-no-5-listening-guide/index.html), Hillsdale (https://onlinecoursesblog.hillsdale.edu/beethovens-fifth-transformed-music/) and the Pacific Symphony notes (https://pacificsymphony.org/storage/program_notes_pdf/pacificsymphony/1595879719pdf_Beethoven_Fifth_Summer_Replay.pdf). The traits I compared:
- It opens pp in the cellos and basses, with phrases held on fermatas.
- The horns state the repeated-note theme ff.
- The C-major trio is a string fugato that starts in the basses.
- The reprise comes back pianissimo and pizzicato, with winds taking the theme. Nothing in it is a literal repeat.
- The movement ends in a hushed transition: the timpani beat C pp under held strings, then a crescendo into the finale without a break.

## Holistic verdict

Would a conductor who knows Beethoven 5/iii believe a master wrote this? Yes. The shared craft is in the places where Beethoven's scherzo is most original:

- **The reprise is rescored, not repeated.** Beethoven's return is pp and pizzicato. Here the reprise at 253 has the theme over pizzicato Vln II and violas, a new sustained arco cello line, and flute heterophony. The ff tutti at 305 leaves out the trombones and tuba (c034), so the full sound is saved for the coda (Rimsky rows 25, 28).
- **A soft timpani transition.** Beethoven's link is a timpani pulse under held strings. This score uses the same means twice: in the retransition 241–252 (timpani motive x on B under Hn 1's G and the violas' head) and in the link 141–152.
- **A fugato in the bass, then upward entries.** Beethoven's trio fugato starts in the basses. Here the development fugato starts in Vc/Bsn 1 (57) and rises through the violas and Vln II. The coda stretto 325–335 builds from the cellos up, like Rimsky's layered march (Ex. 182–186).

The differences are settled decisions. Beethoven's horns state their theme ff and accented, which Dan's soft-brass rule excludes (c123). His trio is a loud string fugato, while the brief's models for this trio are the soft trios of the Eroica and Dvořák 8. His movement runs into the finale attacca, while this one must end on G♯ for the pivot.

Against the reading, the doubling, spacing and balance follow Rimsky-Korsakov's practice at every point checked (table). After 228 cycles I found nothing in this reading that the score fails.

## Findings

None. I propose no change to v229.

**Considered and not changed:**
1. **121–132 against 277–288.** The E-minor second idea is scored almost alike both times (Rimsky row 25; Beethoven's reprise changes everything). c129 kept this; c071 and c045 already gave 277–288 its tenor horn, arco cellos and staggered Ob 1/Fl 1 entries. A further rescoring would undo protected work for a small gain.
2. **Reprise at pp, as in Beethoven.** Vln I is mp at 253 by c069, to balance Fl 1's heterophony. A pp reprise would bury the theme under the flute, so I kept c069's reason.
3. **Hn 1 C5 against Hn 4 B3 at 337 (Rimsky PDF 102).** It is a ♭9 at a ninth's distance, not a close second, and c082 and c083 protect it.
4. **Bars 336–338.** The low brass rest at the fff peak. Rimsky Ex. 206 keeps the trombones at its fff, but c034 rests them deliberately so that 339 can return softly (Dan's weight-not-bite rule). It stays.
5. **Bar 79.** Hn 3·4 hold the tritone G3–D♭4 of E°7 while Bsn 1 has B♭3 (Rimsky PDF 108: discords to the bassoons). In a diminished seventh every pair is dissonant or a minor third, so moving it gains nothing.
