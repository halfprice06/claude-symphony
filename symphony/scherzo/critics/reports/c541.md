## Findings (ranked)

### 1. Bar 48: the second idea's antecedent ends on a triple E; the E-minor statements already use an incomplete neighbour here (APPLIED, theme change)

**Problem.** In G major, bars 47–49 sing F♯6–E6 (tied) | –D6, E6 (quaver) | E6 (dotted crotchet). The quaver anticipates the next downbeat. So the join between antecedent and consequent strikes E6, then the neighbour D6, then E6 twice in a row. This is a forte espressivo line in four octaves (Vln I/Fl 1, Vln II/Ob 1, Vla/C.a./Vc), and the antecedent's last gesture is a repeated note. The same join in E minor (124 and 280) is B5–D6 | C6: an upper incomplete neighbour reached by a leap of a third that falls by step onto the consequent's first note. The theme therefore has two shapes for the same place, and the plainer one is in its first and most important statement.

**Evidence.**
- PDF 334–335, Ex. 20-13 (the Franck excerpt 20-13b): an incomplete neighbour can "decorate … the following tone", approached by leap. F♯6 leaps a third up from D6 and falls a step to the E6 of 49.
- PDF 336: incomplete neighbours "attract a certain amount of attention", because a leap emphasises the dissonance. That is why they belong in the soprano. Here the IN is the top line at the hinge of the theme, the place that should catch the ear.
- PDF 339–340 (Ex. 20-23): figuration choices can prevent "ugly repetitions". The anticipation is what produces the repetition.
- PDF 330 (Ex. 20-3, polyphonic melody): F♯6 resumes the upper voice G6–F♯6 of 46–47 and passes it on to E6 at 49. The upper voice now runs G6–F♯6 … F♯6–E6–D6–C♯6 over the descending lower voice B5–… –D6.
- The E-minor statements already prove the figure in this theme: B5–D6 | C6 at 124 and 280, over the same harmony at 280 (E7/G♯ to A minor). After the change, all three statements share one join.

**Voice leading at 48 beat 3.5.** The harmony is E7/G♯ (bass G♯, Cl 1/Hn 1 B4, Cl 2 D4, Hn 2 E3). F♯ is a ninth over the root and a seventh over the bass, so it is dissonant. Vln II/Ob 1 F♯5–E5 against Cl 1/Hn 1 B4–A4 makes surface fifths on a quaver. The reading allows exactly this: fifths from a dissonant neighbour "occur much more freely … in instrumental style" (PDF 341; PDF 343, point 9b; Brahms, Ex. 20-25). There are no new octaves (PDF 341, point 10). The low octave F♯4 (Vla, C.a., Vc) forms fourths with B4 and thirds with D4. The 7th D6 still reaches C♯6 at 49 beat 3, as before. Rhythm, slurs, bowing (c031's lead-on), dynamics and harmony are unchanged.

### 2. Bars 52→53, 128→129, 284→285: D (or B) sounds three times into the cadence phrase (checked, not changed)

c451's neighbour figure at 53 follows the anticipating quaver of 52, so the line goes E6–D6 | D6–E6–D6–C6 (E minor: C♯6–B5 | B5–C♯6–B5–A5). I tested every figure the reading offers for the quaver. Each one is worse:
- A lower IN (C♯6 in G) doubles the bass C♯ of the V6/5-of-V and moves with it to D: octaves by figuration (PDF 341).
- C♮6 sounds against that C♯ bass as a cross relation.
- An échappée F♯6 (Ex. 20-13a) turns the line into F♯–E–F♯–D–E–D–C, a zigzag around two notes.
- In E minor, the same options clash with the A♯ bass of VII°7 of V (c412, c523).
- Ex. 20-17's escape tone plus accented passing tone needs a beat the hemiola set-up does not have.

The anticipation is the least intrusive, so 52/128/284 stay as they are.
