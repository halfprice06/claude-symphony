## Findings

### 1. T's second phrase is a rhythmic copy of the first, a step higher (158, 174, 214; coda 346). Applied. THEME CHANGE.

**Problem.** Phrases 1 and 2 of T have the same rhythm bar for bar: minim-crotchet, minim-crotchet, dotted figure, crotchet-minim. Bar 6 (`cis''2 fis''4`) repeats bar 2 (`b'2 e''4`) a step higher. In both, the leap to the phrase's top note (E5, then F♯5) falls on the light third beat and is let go at once. Cycle 1 left this open, and c031 and c111 kept it. Only phrase 3 (the tied A, c081) has a rhythm of its own, so for its first eight bars the tune offers one rhythmic pattern twice.

**Evidence.** Most of the lyrical string melodies Adler prints place a long note off the downbeat, by a tie or an upbeat. Tchaikovsky 5/ii, sul G (Ex. III-5, PDF 74), holds its long notes across beats and bars. Brahms 3/iii, sul A (Ex. III-7, PDF 75), the closest model (a lyrical third movement one in a bar), places its long notes after short pickups throughout. The Tristan and Harris cello lines (PDF 99–100) and Don Quixote (PDF 102) do the same. Dvořák's E-major trio tune (corpus line 31) arrives on its long note on beat 2. Putting T's phrase-2 peak on beat 2 and holding it gives that phrase its own stress. It also brings in a hint of the movement's own device, the minim across the beat (the hemiola). With the change the tune's rhythm builds through its phrases: phrase 1 is minim-crotchet, phrase 2 holds its peak, and phrase 3 has crotchets and a tie.

**Fix (applied).** Bar 6 of T, `cis4 fis2` instead of `cis2 fis4`, in every voice carrying it:
- tA.ily `tAClOne` 158: `cis''4 fis''2`; `tAVlnOne` 174: `cis'''4 fis'''2)`; `tAVlnTwo` 174: `cis''4 fis''2)`.
- tC.ily `tCClOne`, `tCVlnTwo` 214: `cis''4 fis''2`; `tCVlnOne` 214: `cis'''4 fis'''2)`.
- coda.ily `codaOboeOne` 346 (A major): `fis''4 b''2` (was `fis''2 b''4`).

The harmony does not change. The held F♯ lies in the ii chord: bass F♯, harp and viola off-beats F♯4, cello A2 on beat 2, and Hn 1·2 C♯5/A4 at 214. At 346 the held B5 lies in B minor (Cb B1, Vla D4, Bsn 1 D3). Slurs and hairpins are unchanged. A c121 comment is in tA.ily.

### 2. The "sul A" at 169 now changes string on the phrase's top note (169–170). Applied.

**Problem.** c031 marked T's first string statement "espress., sul A", following Adler PDF 75. c076 then moved "nat." to the E6 on beat 3 of 170, so the sul A now covers only G♯5–A5–B5. The section plays those three notes in 3rd–4th position on the A string, then crosses to the E string inside the slur for E6, the first phrase's highest note. The brighter timbre falls on the one note the marking was meant to keep soft, and the string crossing falls on that note too.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 75: the reason for sul A is to keep soft high notes off the E string's "different, more brilliant timbre". PDF 73: name a string only for timbre; in expressive passages "all the tones" should have "equal and controlled texture". Brahms's sul A example (Ex. III-7) keeps the whole phrase on one string, and its range is C5–B♭5, the ninth above the open A. That is the same limit c076 took from Rimsky-Korsakov. T's phrase spans G♯5–E6, so it cannot all lie on the A string. Unmarked, the section plays the whole phrase on the E string in one colour (G♯5 2nd finger in 1st position, one shift to E6), and its p–mp dynamic gives Adler's soft, "luminous" E string. This reverses c031 at this bar, but it keeps c031's purpose (one colour for the soft phrase) and c076's range limit.

**Fix (applied).** tA.ily `tAVlnOne`: `gis''2(\mp^\markup \italic "espress." a''4 | b''2 e'''4)` (was `"espress., sul A"` … `e'''4^\markup \italic "nat.")`). The c031 comment is replaced by a c121 comment. No notes change.
