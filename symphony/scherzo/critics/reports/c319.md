## Holistic comparison with Dvořák 5/iii

- **The bridge from the slow movement.** Dvořák opens his scherzo with the slow movement's tempo and theme before the Allegro breaks in. This movement does the same thing by other means. Movement II's last pitch returns as Hn 1's held G♯ (1–2), the horn call follows (3–6), and timpani motive x launches the Allegro (9–10). The single-horn, p, poetic opening is the use Rimsky-Korsakov gives the horn in Ex. 76 (row 4). A conductor who knows Op. 76 would recognise the device and accept it.
- **The trio's key.** Dvořák moves to ♭III (D♭ in B♭), a third-relation colour change. This trio stays in the tonic major and opens out to C♯ minor and B at 180–192. Here the tonic major is the right choice, for three reasons:
  - ♭VI (C) is barred by the C-major rule.
  - ♭III (G) is already the second idea's key at 45.
  - The G♯ frame needs E major at the end.
- **Lightness.** Dvořák lightens his tune with triangle. This score has no percussion, and it gets its lightness from the means Rimsky-Korsakov shows in Ex. 121 and Ex. 93: leggiero strings over pizz. off-beats, wind calls answered by strings, and the timpani's motive x.
- **Proportion.** At 324 and 376 bars the two movements are of the same scale.
- **Verdict.** On orchestration, yes: a conductor would believe a master wrote this. All 27 principles hold, and the doublings at 197–200 (Vla + Cl + Hn with the horn line simplified, Ex. 96) and 217–224 (Vc + Bsn + Hn, Ex. 90–91) are exactly Rimsky-Korsakov's "most natural" combinations. The gap c289–c311 named remains: T and the second idea are well made but not tunes one keeps for life. This reading teaches doubling and spacing, not melodic invention, so it gives no basis for rewriting them. T's head is also tied to movement I (c310).

### Considered and kept (decided, no change)

1. **305–320 (308, 311, 316, 319): Hn 3·4 form sevenths, and at 311 a minor ninth, on their shared staff at ff.** Hn 4's bass B2 (c114) sits under Hn 3's tenor A3, and at 311 under the tied C4 of the 9–8 suspension (c198). Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 102 prefers sevenths and seconds in different colours, and PDF 108 gives concords to the horns. I tested Hn 4 on F♯3, the fifth of V, at the four bars in /tmp/critic-319/work. It turns the sevenths into thirds, but it makes downbeat-to-downbeat octaves with theme A's E–F♯–E in Vln I at 307–309 and 311–312, and PDF 83 forbids consecutive octaves between upper parts. D♯3 would give a tritone with A3, and B3 a second with C4. The discords are the V7's own seventh and the expressive 9–8, which rule 4 (PDF 90) allows "when discords are to be emphasised", and c114's reason (filling the octave above the bass) stands. Reverted; no change.
2. **55 and 216: Hn 1·2 sevenths (D4–C5, D♯4–C♯5).** These are the 4–3 at the second idea's cadence (c172) and the 9–8 at 216 (c298, kept). Both are suspensions meant to be heard (PDF 90 rule 4, exception). Kept.
3. **87: Cl 1 A4 doubles the bass A under D♯°7.** The A is a pedal from 84, and PDF 84 lets pedal passages follow the rules of harmony for pedals. Cl 1 reaches A4 as the end of its dolce phrase and leaves it at once. Kept.
4. **169–184: no bassoon under the cello countermelody (row 12, Ex. 91).** The strings-alone colouring of T's second statement is protected (c108, c257, c304), and 209–224 already has Vc + Bsn 2 (+ Hn 4), which is Rimsky's combination. Kept.
5. **Trio key against Dvořák's ♭III.** Kept for the reasons above.
