## What else I studied

**Bruckner 6/iii.** No copy is in reference-corpus/. I worked from what I know of the score, checked against Deryck Cooke's note for the Haitink recording (https://www.abruckner.com/down/articles/articlesEnglish/cookederyckbruckne2/cooke_bruckner_6.pdf) and the Wikipedia article (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._6_(Bruckner)). The facts I used are these. The scherzo is the only slow one Bruckner wrote. It is in A minor over a continuous pulsation on E: the first twenty bars rest on a dominant pedal, and the root-position tonic arrives only at the end. It has no single striking theme. Three rhythmic motives sound together from the start and are combined and recombined throughout. The development goes flat (D♭, G♭, B♭ minor). The recapitulation starts over the dominant pedal again. The C-major trio ("Langsam") is a dialogue of horn and pizzicato strings on the same figure, and the woodwinds quote the main theme of the Fifth Symphony.

## Holistic comparison with Bruckner 6/iii

Bruckner's scherzo and this one are opposite types. Bruckner's is slow, ambiguous and ostinato-based, and holds back its tonic. This one is a quick, clear Allegro vivace with a theme in four-bar periods, on the model of Dvořák 9/iii. I would not move it toward Bruckner's harmony. Theme A starts on the tonic, and the finale quotes its head and bar 2 at pitch. The plan was written around a clear E minor against the trio's E major. A withheld tonic would undo both.

Two of Bruckner's habits do carry over, and the score already has them in part.
- **The trio as a timbral dialogue.** Bruckner sets horn against pizzicato. This trio sets the clarinet against the harp's off-beats over a horn drone (153), then the cellos against the flute's answers (169), then the oboe/cor anglais calls against the cellos (185). Adler PDF 253 (choirs apart, then together) describes this order. It is at Bruckner's level of craft.
- **Combining the movement's motives.** Bruckner's movement lives on motives that sound together and come back in new combinations. This movement has one such combination: theme A over the tenor countermelody (cellos, Bsn 1, Hn 3·4) at 105–120 and 305–320. But the countermelody is heard only in those two ff tuttis, each time competing with the full orchestra. It never appears at a level where a listener can learn it. Finding 1 fixes that.

Would a conductor believe a master wrote this? On scoring, yes. I checked the 40 applicable principles above bar by bar. The only real breach is the one in Finding 1, and it is a matter of how the form is shaped, not a mistake in the scoring. A master's reprise usually lets a secondary line step forward once. The reprise at 253 now does that.

## Findings

### 1. 253–268: the cellos play the tenor countermelody at pp, so that the tutti at 305 brings back a line already heard alone (applied)

**Problem.** The tenor countermelody (G3–B3–E4–B3–A3–F♯3–G3 …, sC/dC Vc, Bsn 1, Hn 3·4) is the movement's only real second line against theme A. It is heard twice, at 105 and 305, both times ff under a full tutti with theme A in octaves above it. At 253 the reprise brings theme A back over the same bass (E E E B E A B E A G♯ A B E G B E, identical to 105–120), but the cellos hold a separate line of dotted minims (B–G–F♯–G–E–A–G …). That line appears nowhere else.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 142–143 (Ex. V-20, Beethoven 7/ii): the layers stay clear for two reasons. Each has its own rhythm and articulation, and "the foreground and middleground themes were introduced earlier without the background". Adler PDF 143 adds that a line set out by the strings alone is then "developed, colored, or doubled" by the orchestra. Adler PDF 238: orchestrate each thematic element so that the form is clear. In v359 the countermelody is never introduced without its ff background. At 253 the layers already have the right rhythms: theme A in spiccato crotchets and quavers, the flute heterophony in slurred quavers, the pizz. off-beats, and a legato line in minims. The legato layer only needs to be the countermelody. Bruckner 6/iii makes the same point at the scale of a movement: its motives become familiar by recombination. Dan asked for the themes to keep developing (2026-10-12). This gives the countermelody a soft statement of its own, with no change to any recorded theme.

### Considered and not proposed
1. **15–16, 255–256: cellos doubling Vln I's dip (Adler PDF 141, Ex. V-19).** I reject it for the reason c259 gave. Shostakovich's theme is arco with arco accompaniment. Here the theme is spiccato over pizz. downbeats, and doubling would thicken the leggiero. At 255–256 the new countermelody already lies just below the dip (E4/B3, A3/F♯3) and supports it.
2. **77, 85, 86: bass clarinet A2 a minor third under harp C3, pp (Adler PDF 151).** This is the F6 of the episode, with the third doubled near the bottom as Adler PDF 152/257 asks. The setting is pp, plucked over a soft reed, and the spacing of 73–88 is protected (c299). The gain does not justify the change.
3. **Register economy (Adler PDF 238).** A6 occurs often: 49–52, 109, 217, 253–263, 309, 337 and 349. B6 is kept for 336 alone, so the top step of the movement is still saved. Lowering the second idea would undo c301/c321.
4. **A Bruckner-style dominant pedal under the reprise at 253.** It would hide theme A's tonic, which the finale quotes at pitch, and it would weaken 305's return. Not proposed.
