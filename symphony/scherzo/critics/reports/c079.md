## What else I studied

- **Adler part 1, ch. 7, oboe** (adler1 PDF 198): the oboe "loses its pungency as it goes up". Its best range is F4 to C6. The notes from C6 to F6 give "a thin, softly piercing effect … quite hard to control". This passage is the second source for Finding 1.
- **The Mendelssohn Scherzo** (G minor, 3/8, Allegro vivace; 2 fl, 2 ob, 2 cl, 2 bsn, 2 hn, 2 tpt, timp, strings), from my knowledge of the score. The woodwinds open alone, staccato and pianissimo, and the strings take the material over. Choirs alternate in phrase-length units. Almost the whole piece stays below forte. Late on, the flute has a long unbroken staccato solo, and the piece fades out instead of ending in a tutti.
- **v69 against v79.** I diffed every part bar by bar (script in /tmp/critic-079/bars.py) and checked each change from c069 to c078 in the score. The changes were: A♯°7 at 88; Ob 1 at 92–94; horn dynamics 101/105/301/305; Fl 2 in the upper octave 109–120/309–320; contrabassoon rests 121–125/277–281; timpani alone at 135; Vln I sul A at 170/173; Fl 1 rests 177–184; Bsn 2 at 203–205; slurs and the mf entry at 209–224; Vln I mp at 253; Hn 2 and cellos in the tenor at 281–288; the seam at 296; Cl 2 at 331; violas at 336–338; Bsn 1 and the violas at 345–349; the harp at 333/366/375. **None of them made anything worse.** The page count is 13 in both versions. ## Holistic judgement against Mendelssohn

A conductor who knows the Mendelssohn would accept this as the work of a skilled late-Romantic composer. It follows the Dvořák and Brahms tradition, not Mendelssohn's fairy-scherzo manner, and that matches the brief. Several passages already show the Mendelssohn virtues:
- the strings-only leggiero theme over pizzicato downbeats (13–28);
- the winds-alone calls (29–36);
- the flute heterophony over the reprise (253–268), which is the most Mendelssohnian page;
- the string-free F-major episode;
- the close that dies away on one held G♯.

Where it departs from Mendelssohn is its loud blocks. About 52 bars reach MIDI velocity ≥ 100, mostly 41–56, 69–72, 101–120, 301–320 and 333–339. Cycle 59 left this open for a later holistic critic. **I decide it stays as it is:**
1. The models in BRIEF.md are Dvořák 9/iii and Beethoven 9/ii, not Mendelssohn.
2. Cycles 63 and 39 already made the second and third statements of the second idea start sub. p.
3. Adler's own tutti model (Slavonic Dance 8, PDF 209–213) is a loud tutti followed at once by a soft tune, and the score does exactly that at 120→121 and 72→73.

The orchestral mechanics stand up to the reading. 22 of the 23 principles are followed. The one exception is a register problem at two bars.

## Findings

### 1. Bars 72 and 300: the oboes at E6 and C♯6, ff (with a one-bar diminuendo at 72). Applied.

**Problem.** At 72, Ob 1 jumps to E6 and Ob 2 enters cold on C♯6, both ff with a diminuendo to nothing inside the bar. The orchestra then drops to pp for the F-major episode. Bar 300 is the same ff arrival in the reprise (Ob 1 E6, Ob 2 a cold C♯6).

**Evidence.**
- Adler's model ff→p diminuendo (Dvořák, Slavonic Dance 8, m. 33, adler2 PDF 213, score PDF 212): the piccolo is rewritten an octave lower "to enable the player to accomplish the diminuendo to piano; all other instruments are also in controllable ranges."
- Adler on the oboe (adler1 PDF 198): above C6 the oboe is "pinched", "hard to control" and loses its pungency.
- The E6 adds nothing in either bar, because Fl 1 and Vln I already sound it. Ob 2's only note in each passage is an unprepared second-oboe entry on C♯6 at ff.
- Mendelssohn keeps his oboes in the staff in loud passages of the Scherzo; the flutes and violins carry the top.

**Fix** (`sB.ily`, `dB.ily`): Ob 1 holds the C♯6 that ends its rising line at 71/299 (doubling Cl 1), and Ob 2 takes A5 (doubling Fl 2). The top of the chord is unchanged.
