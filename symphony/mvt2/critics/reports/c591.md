# Critic 591: v591, lens 1 (melody and memorability)

Assigned reading: Adler, *The Study of Orchestration*, part 2, ch. 11 "Scoring for Brass": the brass choir as a homophonic unit, and combinations for melodic presentation (adler2 PDF 19-36, printed pp. 332-349). This is the fifth pass on this reading (c071, c161, c321, c451 before). I read the text of every page in the range in full, and rendered all 18 pages at 110 dpi (/tmp/critic-591/pg-019.png to pg-036.png) and read the notation from the images, not the OCR. The examples are Beethoven 5/iv (PDF 19), Hindemith *Nobilissima visione* (20), Bruckner 7/iv (21-22, sideways), Stravinsky's Bach chorale variations (23), Elgar "W.M.B." (24), Mussorgsky-Ravel "Great Gate of Kiev" (25), Strauss *Don Quixote* (27), Weinberger *Polka and Fugue* (28), Schubert *Rosamunde* and Tchaikovsky *1812* (29-30), Berlioz *Roman Carnival* (30-32), Prokofiev 5/iii and Wagner *Siegfried* (33), Liszt *Les Préludes* (34), and *Lohengrin* Act I prelude (35-36).

Two details are visible only in the images. In *Don Quixote* (PDF 27) the horns do not double the whole solo cello line: horns 1·2 and 3·4 take alternate fragments and overlap, which is what Adler means by "staggered" and "four personalities". In *Lohengrin* (PDF 36, bars 56-58) the diminuendo ends with all winds at pp, and the first violins then enter p with a new rising line above them. The "gorgeous pianissimo" on PDF 35 refers to that entry, not only to the winds fading.

## Findings

Every principle in this reading that applies to the melodic lines is followed. I found nothing that the reading would change, and I made no edits to the scratch copy. The points below record what I checked against the reading. Where c451 already recorded a point, I add only what is new.

### 1. Theme A's stressed notes, reduced as PDF 23 asks (recorded, no change)

**Bars:** 7-14, 23-30, 71-82.

**Check against PDF 23.** Adler asks for a reduction to the essential pitches, and then for each stressed note: which note, why, and how it is made to dominate. Reduced, theme A is: a rise to a held 5̂ (E♭) that falls through the motto tail E♭-D-D♭-C to 3̂; then a rise to a held 6̂ (F) that reaches the B♭ appoggiatura and falls to 1̂. The two held notes and the appoggiatura are the stressed notes. Each is made to dominate in the same way at every statement:
- The held E♭ and F are tied over the barline, and the crescendo peaks on the tie (hidden MIDI peaks at 7-8 and 23-24, c338).
- The B♭ appoggiatura gets the bar's dynamic peak, and its resolution is softer: mp> at 12 (horn), f> at 28 (violins), mf> at 76, and ff at 79, where it is the movement's highest note (B♭6, three octaves).
- Nothing in the accompaniment sounds above the horn at its attacks in 7-14 (c019, c057, c361). The counter-melody under 79 falls while the tune holds.

The register of the stressed notes rises over the movement (B♭4 → B♭5 → B♭5 → B♭6), so the listener hears the same hook higher each time. This is the plan the reading asks for, and it is the main reason theme A is the tune a listener keeps.

### 2. Colour kept back for a later statement (recorded, no change)

**Bars:** 31-36, 83-89.

**Check against PDF 20 (Hindemith keeps the tuba for the theme two octaves lower).** Theme A gets a low, fresh colour once: the bass clarinet's head and tail at 31-34 in the chalumeau, about two octaves below the violins' statement at 23. The clarinet, which accompanies nothing in 7-30, is kept back for the motto tail at 85-89. The harp, which accompanies in 7-14, gets the tune itself only at 83. Each instrument that plays theme A plays it in a colour the listener has not yet heard carry it. This is c451's row 3 with the low statement added. It follows the reading.

### 3. Horn with cello at 75-82 follows the Strauss model as the image shows it (recorded, no change)

**Bars:** 57-60, 75-82.

**Check against PDF 27 (Ex. XI-10, image).** The image shows that Strauss's horns enter in overlapping fragments rather than doubling the whole line. In this score, horn 2 joins the cello counter-melody at 75 (p<), and horn 1 joins at 78 b3 (mf<) for the a2 at 79. Each horn colours part of the line and the full doubling arrives at the peak, which is the staggered effect Adler describes. At 57-60 horn 1 plays the counter-line and the cellos double it an octave below from 57 b3, so the horn leads and the cello follows. Both passages follow the reading. The plain first statement at 23-30 (cellos alone) is protected.

### 4. Colour contrast carries the answers (recorded, no change)

**Bars:** 15-22, 37-56.

**Check against PDF 30-32 (Ex. XI-14).** The image shows Berlioz's line in long notes over a constant eighth-note ostinato, first in the bassoon, then in the violins, then in the trombones. Adler credits its effect to colour, not to the counterpoint. Theme B is laid out in the same way: a constant viola triplet ostinato from 37, with the line moving from flute 1 (37-40) to the cor anglais under flute 1 (41-44), oboe 1 with the cellos (45-47), the violins (53-56), and the tutti (57-62). Each of these phrases is a new colour over the same background. At 15-22 the oboe and the solo violin alternate over the muted strings. Both passages follow the reading.

### 5. The release at 81-83 against the *Lohengrin* image (recorded, no change; protected)

**Bars:** 81-83.

**Check against PDF 35-36 (Ex. XI-18, image).** Wagner's order has three stages: the strings drop the doubling, the woodwinds carry it into a diminuendo, and the violins enter p with a new line above the pp winds. In this score the winds and strings fade together from ff at 81 to p at 82. Then the harp enters p at 83 with theme A's head in harmonics, above the cellos and basses' ppp pedal. The last stage (a soft new colour entering on top after the fade) is the same as Wagner's. The first two stages are combined. Separating them, for example by stopping the strings at 81 b3 so that the winds carry the fade alone, would change the staged release 81-83 and the violins' "mettere sord." timing at 82 b3. Both are protected (c063, c585, standing list). The present order also follows Adler's Schubert example (PDF 28-29), where the whole tutti fades together. I do not propose a change.

### 6. Comparison with the models (recorded)

Dvořák's Largo tune (corpus woods.notesL.ily 207-210) repeats a one-bar cell (dotted eighth, sixteenth, quarter) four times within a sixth, and its second phrase repeats a two-bar cell. Theme A repeats its head rhythm (dotted quarter, eighth, tied quarter) three times in eight bars, and ends both halves with a falling line to the tonic. That repetition is what makes it singable after one hearing, as in Dvořák. Theme A's second half rises a sixth to the held F and reaches the B♭ appoggiatura. This is the same move Tchaikovsky makes in the second half of the 5/ii horn tune, which rises higher than the first half to a stressed dissonance. Theme B is a neighbour-note tune with an arpeggio second phrase. It is the weaker tune by design (c401), and its first phrase is protected (c141, c291, c581), so I do not reopen it. The Largo's trombone 1 plays one ff quotation of movement I's head and nothing else (brass.notesL.ily, `tromboneImL`); bars 63-64 here do the same.
