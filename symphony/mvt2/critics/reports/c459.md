## Comparison with Mozart 41/ii

Mozart's slow movement is a sonata form for muted strings. The winds play two roles. In the first theme they are f punctuation: the full wind and horn chord on beat 2 of bars 2 and 4 against the muted violins' p (corpus PDF p. 1). Later they colour the restatements. I asked what a conductor who knows that movement would notice in this one.

1. **Single colour and muting.** Mozart keeps the strings muted throughout, as Adler PDF 203 says the una corda pedal is realised. Movement II mutes only 15-36 and the coda horns, and opens string-free from bar 3. These are the Dvořák 9/ii plan and the cyclic rules, not oversights. The mute changes fall in rests (4, 31-36, 37), so they do not show.
2. **The return.** At 60 Mozart restates his theme with new figuration underneath it. This score's A′ at 71 does the same: viola eighths, cello and horn counter-melody, violins in octaves, flute at pitch from 77. It is the "same colour, different hue" that Adler praises in Dvořák (PDF 209, 213). It is also shorter than Mozart's return (12 bars against 24), which is in proportion for an ABA′ Adagio and matches Dvořák 9/ii (c453).
3. **Contrast within the theme.** Mozart's p/f answer inside the theme gives a contour this movement does not try for. Here the dynamic shape is two long crescendos, 53-61 and 75-79. That is the Romantic idiom of the models named in the brief. A conductor would not count it as a weakness.
4. **The ending.** Mozart closes by recalling the opening material over the opening colour. Here the coda recalls theme A's head in harp harmonics (83), puts the motto tail in the clarinet, and returns to the opening string sound with Vln I's C5 on top. This is Ravel's "return of the exact opening colours" (PDF 224). The one foreign event, the trombone quotation, is not brought back, as Ravel does with his interlude.
5. **Craft details a conductor checks.** Mozart writes no wind note that lacks a job. Every doubling in v459 is assigned a job in the score comments, with a cited source, and no part is idle in a way that could be cut. The full tutti appears only at 61-62 and 79-81. The brass enter once, as in row 26.

Would a conductor believe a master wrote this? On scoring and pacing, yes. In this reading I found no place where the orchestration makes a technical choice that Mozart, Dvořák or Ravel in these examples would not make.

## Findings

No finding rises to a change. Each candidate from the reading is listed with the reason it stays as it is, ranked by how close it came.

1. **71 b2, viola A♭4 under Vln II's held A♭4 (Ravel PDF 217, row 15).** The viola figure `c'8( es' aes') es'( c' aes)` attacks A♭4 on the second eighth of beat 2, inside the second violins' dotted-quarter A♭4. It is the only place in 71-78 where the background strikes the melody's sounding pitch. It lasts one eighth at pp under p, with Vln I an octave above, so it cannot blur the line. The figure was accepted by c137 and recorded by c427. Not changed. If the composer reopens the figure, the one-note fix is `c'8( es' c') es'( c' aes)` at 71. It keeps A♭ major, adds no parallels with the cellos' E♭3-A♭3, and repeats the C4 the bar already uses.
2. **83-86, motion in the ppp chord (Bizet PDF 213, 216, row 13).** I checked whether the held A♭ in the cellos and basses needs Bizet's added motion. It already has it: harp harmonics, then the timpani figure, then Cl 1 and the divided violas, one after another. No change.
3. **62-65, scored diminuendo (Dvořák PDF 213, Ex. XVI-10 mm. 33-42, row 10).** Dvořák lowers the piccolo and lets the strings drop out. Here the winds drop out at the end of 62 and the strings carry the release with sf> tremolo. That is the same method with the choirs reversed, and it suits the trombone quotation, which has to stand alone (protected). No change.
