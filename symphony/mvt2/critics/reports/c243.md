# Critic 243: lens 3 (form, pacing and proportion), v243

Assigned reading: Aldwell & Schachter, *Harmony and Voice Leading*, Unit 14 "V as a Key Area", PDF 211–229 (printed pp. 207–225). I read the OCR text of all nineteen pages. I rendered PDF 211, 212, 214–225, 227–229 at 110 dpi and read every example from the image: Ex. 14-1 (Schubert, *Heidenröslein*), 14-2 (Mendelssohn, Op. 62/1), 14-3 (chords applied to V), 14-4 (Bach, Chorale 234), 14-5 (Freylinghausen), 14-6 (Mozart K. 333/i), 14-7 (Haydn Op. 76/3/i), 14-8 (Haydn Op. 77/2, Menuetto), 14-9 (Chopin, Prelude Op. 28/13), 14-10 (Mozart, "In diesen heil'gen Hallen"), 14-11 (Mozart, German Dance K. 509/6), 14-12 (Schumann, *Stückchen*), 14-13 (model phrase groups), and the exercises on PDF 228–229. The unit is about tonicizing and modulating to V. Most of its techniques are general (applied chords, pivot chords, cadences in the new key, the return), and §11 (PDF 220–225) is about how key areas make form. I used it as a checklist for this movement's key areas and the joins between them.

## Findings

Unit 14 asks five things of a key plan. (1) Every key area is reached through its own dominant or confirmed by it. (2) The joins use a pivot or a clear phrase modulation. (3) The new key is confirmed by a cadence. (4) The return is prepared in proportion to the length of the stay away. (5) The form is shaped by departure and return. v243 does all five. I found nothing in this reading that a master's score would need changed, and I recommend no change. I did not edit the scratch copy, so there was no build. v243's own build-warnings.txt is empty, analysis.txt shows 0 outer-voice parallels, score.pdf has 8 pages and parts-book.pdf has 30. The findings below are what I checked, ranked by how close each came to a recommendation.

### 1. The movement never makes V a key area (checked, no change)

**Point.** A&S PDF 213 (§3), 220 (§11), 223 and 228 (review points b–d) make V the normal contrasting area of a major-key form and the usual key of an ABA middle section. "Because of the special importance of the tonic-dominant relationship, the use of V as a key area is of particular importance in creating musical form" (PDF 220). Here E♭ is never tonicized. The departures go to the subdominant side: f minor at 17–21, IV for the whole of 37–61, D♭ again at the A′ climax (79), and the iv–I close (87–89).

**Why no change.** The unit says its techniques "are easily applicable to other tonicizations and modulations" (PDF 213). It also says departure and return are what matter (PDF 220): "Without the contrast of the tonicized V, there would be no impression of departure and return". This score gets that impression from IV and from the excursions to F♯ minor and A minor. The dominant still gets the large-scale job the unit gives it after a long stay away. At 65–70 it is a seventh chord (E♭7, with D♭ in Vln II at 65–66 and Bsn 2 at 69–70) under the horn's tail. That is A&S's remedy, word for word: "turning the chord into a dominant seventh by adding a minor 7th" so that "the listener is prepared for the return of the tonic" (PDF 220). Dvořák 9/ii also puts its middle section somewhere other than V. The subdominant leaning comes from the cyclic plan (D♭ as a key, plagal D♭–A♭ close; cyclic-notes §3) and is protected.

### 2. Bar 64 b4 is the double-meaning chord A&S asks for at a return (checked, no change)

**Point.** "The last chord in the [foreign] area will have a double meaning" (PDF 219 §10). On 64 b4, F♭–A♭–C♭–D (E2, A♭3, B2, D4, F3 in the low brass and strings) is E7♭9, V of A minor, and also the German sixth of A♭ resolving to E♭7 at 65 (c210, c232). The F♭→E→E♭ thread does the same job at 62–65 that Ex. 14-1 bar 5 does with I = IV of V. A&S's model (PDF 219) introduces the returning chord "in a place of some prominence". Here that is a downbeat, the start of letter H and Tempo I.

**Why no change.** It follows the reading. I record it as one more reason to keep the protected F♭/E–E♭ thread at 62–65.

### 3. 31–36: the tonic is held as a goal, not made active (checked, no change)

**Point.** A&S PDF 217–218 (§8): the 5–6 over the tonic "allows the tonic, itself normally a goal, to become an active element", and Ex. 14-8 shows the tonic losing stability before the new key arrives. After the full close at 30, 31–36 hold the plain A♭ triad for six bars: no 5–6, no seventh, and the bare fifth at 35–36. The only motion is the common-tone diminished seventh at 33 and the bass clarinet's line. The tonic is still a goal when D♭ enters at 37.

**Why no change.** A&S gives a second model: phrase modulation, where "the strong contrasts in rhythm and texture … make it sound as though the section … begins anew, without any transition" (PDF 223, Ex. 14-11). Bar 37 is exactly that: new metre, new tempo, new theme, new accompaniment. The pivot is the A♭ chord itself, I in A♭ and V in D♭ (PDF 216–217, Ex. 14-1 bar 5). I considered a 5–6 (E♭→F in the B.cl. or Bsn 1 at 36). It gives A♭–C–F, which is iii6 in D♭, a weak lead into I. It also replaces the protected bare fifth (c184/c213/c223). A seventh on the A♭ chord was rejected at c020 and c203 because it makes the join V7–I. I do not reopen either.

### 4. The A section's two statements close the same way; A′ differs (checked, no change)

**Point.** A&S PDF 224: in a quatrain the A phrases "need not be identical; in particular the first A and second A usually cadence in different places". Ex. 14-9 shows a semicadence strengthened by a cadence in V (PDF 220–221). In v243, 7–14 and 23–30 have the same bass and harmony bar for bar: semicadence at 10/26, V7–I with 2̂–1̂ at 13–14/29–30. A′ changes both cadences: V4/3 at 73–74 and the ♭VI deceptive cadence at 78 before the climax.

**Why no change.** The form is a–b–a′ (b at 15–22 ends on V via the French sixth), not AABA. The two closes on I sit on either side of a half-cadence phrase. That gives the open–closed contrast A&S asks for (Ex. 14-12: the B phrase prolongs V). The 23–30 accompaniment is protected as the plain first statement (A′ differs on purpose), and 25–26 is where a 4̂–♯4̂–5̂ bass would go. Bar 76 b3 (ii7) could also take one: bass D♮, with the melody's A♭6 as the seventh resolving to G6, as in Ex. 14-2. But the horn/cello counter-melody's protected D♭4 sounds on that beat and would make a false relation against D♮. Keep.

### 5. D♭ is confirmed late, and the consequent at 45–52 modulates (checked, no change)

**Point.** PDF 216 §6 and PDF 219 §9: a long modulation is confirmed by an authentic cadence and a line to the new 1̂. A deceptive cadence may come first. D♭'s dominant comes at 43–44 (cadential 6/4–V, then deceptive into B♭ minor at 45) and at 51–52 (A♭7, then D♭ at 53 with F5 on top). The authentic cadence with 1̂ on top comes only at 60–61, at the ff climax. Between those points, 45–52 is a modulating consequent (PDF 222, Ex. 14-10). It raises the tension (swell to mf on the C♯7 at 47), moves on to F♯ minor for the quotation, and returns to D♭ with no special preparation (PDF 219 §10).

**Why no change.** This follows the reading, and the delay is protected (D♭ cadence held back to 59–61, c076; theme B's tonic withheld until 61, c211). The cadence at 61 uses the unit's characteristic bass 4̂–♯4̂–5̂ (G♭–G♮–A♭ at 59–60, PDF 215). c242 recorded that this is the only such approach to V in the movement, and it is right to save it for the climax.

### 6. ♯4̂ is never doubled (checked, no change)

**Point.** PDF 214: "Because ♯4 functions (temporarily) as a leading tone, it must not be doubled." Every applied chord to V sounds ♯4̂ in one line only. The D♮ at 8 b2 is horn 1 alone. At 24 and 72 b2 it is the theme in violin unison or octaves, with no inner D♮. At 21 b3 (French sixth) D5 is Vln I alone. At 59 b3–4 G♮ is the bass line (Cb, B.cl. and a bassoon in octaves) with no G♮ above it.

**Why no change.** Follows.
