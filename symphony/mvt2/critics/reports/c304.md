## Findings

The movement follows this chapter closely. The scans found no new unison, interlocking or spacing fault outside passages that HISTORY.md already protects with stated reasons. Below I rank the remaining points. None is large enough to justify reopening a protected passage, so I applied nothing.

**F1. Bar 87: the IV6 has its third (F) five times and its root once. No change; recorded.**
Problem. At 87 the sounding notes are Cb F1, Vc F2, Hn 4 and lower violas F3, Hn 3 and upper violas A♭3, C.a. F4, Vln II A♭4, Cl 1 D♭5. The bass note of the first inversion is doubled in four octaves, up to the middle of the texture. The root D♭ sounds only in Cl 1 at pp, at the top.
Evidence. Adler PDF 257, Ex. VIII-25: in a first inversion "the third (bass) should be doubled toward the bottom rather than the top", so that the chord keeps its "open" quality. My scan found 87 to be the only 6-chord in the movement whose third is doubled above the tenor. The others (41, 57, 75, 78 b3) keep the extra Fs, C and E♭s low.
Why I do not recommend a fix. The upper Fs are not chord doublings. They are the first note of the F-F♭-E♭ echo of 61-62, which C.a., Hn 4 and the lower violas play together (c194, c290, c300; the finale quotes it). Adler's rule is about the spacing of a held chord, not about a melodic line that happens to start on the third. The one change that would add the root is c299's option, upper violas D♭4 at 87-88 (`<f des'>2. | <fes des'>2.`, then A♭3 at 89). It would end the violas' doubling of muted Hn 3 (c194) and put a third D♭ against Cl 1's D♭-C, so the plagal top line would be doubled in octaves. The coda is protected. I record the point so that later critics need not raise it again.

**F2. Bars 37-40: theme B's first statement in a single flute; Dvořák's B-section theme uses flute and oboe in unison. No change; recorded.**
Problem. Adler's advice on transcription (PDF 248-249) is to learn the model's favourite wind pairings. In Dvořák 9/ii, the model for this movement, the middle-section tune (bars 46-52, C♯ minor) is played by Fl 1 and Ob 1 in unison at pp. This is Adler's Ex. VIII-2: the flute "will somewhat neutralize the nasal quality of the oboe and give the passage a rounder, richer sound" (PDF 236). Here Fl 1 plays theme B alone at p, from F5 down to B♭4 at 40, which is the weak lower-middle register of the flute.
Why no change. Adler argues the other way for soft solos: a unison of two winds "limits and impedes the expressive quality and prohibits any slight rubato" (PDF 249-250). Dvořák saves his unison for an episode theme that is repeated over a moving accompaniment. Theme B's first statement here is a solo, like Dvořák's own cor anglais tune. The accompaniment under it is pp and sul tasto (c021, c233), so the flute carries. The single flute colour at 37-44 is also protected (c034, c124). If a later version wants more weight at 39-40, the change would be Ob 1 in unison for those two bars only, pp: `bSecOboeOne` 39-40 `ges''4(\pp \tuplet 3/2 4 { f''8 ges'' f'' } es''4 des''4 | c''4 des''8 c''8 bes'2)`. I have not tested it and do not recommend it.

**F3. Bar 14: the horn's last note A♭3 is also in Bsn 1 and is struck by the harp on beat 2. No change.**
Problem. At the end of the horn solo, Bsn 1 resolves G3-A♭3 together with the horn, and the harp RH plays `<aes c' es'>` on beat 2, including the horn's A♭3. Cycles c057 and c219 took the harp and bassoon off the horn's sounding notes at 7-12 (Adler PDF 257: a unison of unlike timbres "robs the timbral characteristics from both").
Why no change. At 14 the solo is over. Bsn 1's A♭3 is the resolution of the leading tone, which the A♭ chord needs. The harp's A♭3 is the root of its own cadence chord. Taking it out would leave the harp's G3 at 13 leaping to C4. Adler's warning is about covering a solo while it speaks, not about its final tonic.

**F4. Oboe-clarinet unisons at 60, 61-62, 79-81 and 81 b2-82. No change.**
Adler PDF 235 (Ex. VIII-1) gives three reasons against this pairing on a melody: the oboe covers, the balance has to be fixed by the conductor, and tuning is hard. In this score it appears only in the crescendo to the first tutti (60) and in the two ff tuttis, where "simple volume is required and the characteristic timbre not crucial" (PDF 250). The last use, 81 b2-82, falls from ff to p. c144 recorded it as a deliberate exception because Ob 1 carries 2̂-1̂ and Cl 2 the only resolving leading tone. I agree with that reasoning.

**F5. Winds doubling strings at pitch. No change.**
Inventory: Fl 1 = Vln I and Ob 1 = Vln II at 57-58 (p against mf); Bsn 1 = Vc at 45-47; Bsn 1 and Hn 2 = Vc at 75-77; Cl 1 = violins on G5 at 77; Cl 1 = Vln I on C5 at 89-90. Adler (PDF 265-266) prefers octave doubling today but shows Berlioz's flute at pitch on a rising violin line, where the doubling adds "warmth" and "richness". 57-58 is that case, with the flute entering under the violins' level as in Schubert (PDF 238). 87-90 is Copland's quiet doubling of muted horns by strings (PDF 270). Each case has a reason. The A′ climax doubles in three octaves (79-81), which is the 8′ + 4′ principle on PDF 250.

**F6. Pedals and their release. No change.**
Adler's Mozart and Strauss examples (PDF 240-245) ask for sustained wind pedals under active strings in a nineteenth-century style, and for the pedal to be lifted before a restatement's pickup. Sustained winds sit under moving strings at 57-59 and 71-74. A♭ (60) and D♭ (79-80) pedals steady both tuttis. The pedal is lifted before the pickups at 10, 26, 44 and 74. c239 covered the three places where only the bass continues (22, 52, 70) and kept them for good reasons. I found nothing to add.

## What must not be touched

The plan for theme A's orchestration (winds only 7-14, strings only 23-30, both together 71-82, then the coda) is Adler's Schumann and Copland "summation" at a master's level and should not be touched. The same holds for the three-octave melody doubling at 79-81 and the dark wind chord over the strings at 87-92.
