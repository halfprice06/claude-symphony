## Holistic judgement against Tchaikovsky 4/ii

Tchaikovsky's Andantino rests on four things:
1. One plain, repeated song tune, first given to a solo reed over a bare accompaniment (oboe and pizzicato strings).
2. A restatement in a lower, warmer colour with a new wind counter-melody (cellos plus flutes).
3. A contrasting middle section that builds to the movement's only real tutti.
4. A return with new decoration, then a coda that breaks the tune into fragments passed between soloists, ending very softly.

This movement does each of these in its own terms:
1. Theme A is first heard in the solo horn over bassoons, bass clarinet and harp, with no strings (7–14). That is as bare as Tchaikovsky's oboe with pizzicato.
2. The restatement at 23 is in muted violins with a cello counter-melody, Tchaikovsky's order with the roles reversed.
3. The D♭ section builds to the climax at 61–64.
4. The coda takes theme A's head into harp harmonics and timpani and gives its tail to clarinet 1 alone (83–89). That is the same dissolving gesture as Tchaikovsky's soloist-by-soloist fade.

Two differences a conductor would notice. First, the return here rises to a second ff peak (79), where Tchaikovsky's subsides. That comes from the Dvořák 9/ii plan and is protected (c171, c173). Second, Tchaikovsky's middle section changes character outright, while this one stays lyrical and changes key and texture. Neither is a craft fault. On orchestral craft I found nothing a conductor would read as amateur: the dynamics name the result (Casanelles PDF 271), every soft entry gets a clear field (Fig. 6.1), and the spacing follows the harmonic series. A conductor would believe a master wrote it.

Compared with v189, one detail got slightly worse, as a side effect of c194 (finding 1). Everything else is neutral or better (finding 2).

## Findings

**1. Bars 89–90, violas: the upper half's new attack at 90 b3 in the morendo. APPLIED.**
- Problem. c194 divided the violas at 87–90, with the upper half on A♭3 doubling muted horn 3. At 90 b3 it notated `<es~ aes>2 es4~^"unis."`, so the upper half has to change from A♭3 to E♭3 at 90 b3. That is a fresh bow attack during the rit. (♩ about 40) and the diminuendo. It is the only attack in 90–92. Everyone else only releases there: the cor anglais, clarinet 1 and the horns all leave at 90 b3 without re-attacking. The notation was also unclear: the tie belongs to the lower half only, and nothing tells the upper half whether to re-bow or stop.
- Evidence.
  - Casanelles PDF 275–276: an attack transient is an inharmonic onset sound and is part of the discourse, so it should come where a new event is meant.
  - PDF 279: a note's envelope is attack, then decay and release.
  - Fig. 6.1 (PDF 272, viewed): the closing field under the distant signal is a static pedal.
  - PDF 278: a doubling that is masked (here by horn 3 and the cellos' resonance) takes room without adding to the sound.
  - v189 held unis. E♭3 from 89 with no attack after 89 b1.
- Fix (applied, coda.ily, codaVla):
  ```
  <f aes>2.\pp^\markup \italic "lower half sul C" | <fes aes>2. | es2.~^\markup \italic "unis., change bow at will" | es2.~ | es2.~\> | es2.\ppp\fermata |
  ```
  Both halves reach E♭3 on 89 b1, together with the chord change to I, the cor anglais' F♭→E♭, clarinet 1's D♭→C and Vln I's entry. Horn 3's A♭3 stays doubled at 87–88, where the F–F♭–E♭ line moves, so c194's purpose is kept for the IV6 and iv chords. **Change to the ending, for the other threads:** the pitches of every chord, the closing tail 85–89 over IV6–iv–I, the last chord (A♭1 A♭2 E♭3 E♭4 A♭4 C5) and Vln I's C5 are all unchanged. **5. Distance by colour, not by dynamic, at 87–90. No change.**
PDF 272–273: a sound moved away changes spectrum, and a softer onstage performance would "invalidate the effect". The muted Hn 3·4 echo of horn 3's open F–F♭–E♭ (79–81), with the cor anglais and violas sul C, makes the distance by timbre. Tchaikovsky does the same at the end of 4/ii, moving the tune to darker solo colours rather than just playing it softer.
