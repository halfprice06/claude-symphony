## What else I studied

**Schumann 2/iii.** No local score, so I used what the web sources state.
- The movement is a C-minor elegy (Wikipedia, Symphony No. 2 (Schumann)).
- Its main theme starts in the strings and is then taken over by the bassoon and the oboe. Schumann wrote that he placed the "melancholy bassoon" there "with special affection" (Cleveland Orchestra programme note).
- The middle section is strongly contrapuntal: a short fugal interlude comes before the melody returns (Wikipedia; Cleveland note).
- It slows at the end, and the final chords are marked molto Adagio (Cleveland note).

**Version comparison.** I diffed every section file of v139 against v149, and checked the climax and final chords from score.midi (chord dump at 37, 41, 53, 57, 61, 75, 77, 79, 82, 87, 92).

**Brass re-entry rule.** Rimsky PDF 136 is just outside the range. The composer already uses it (c024), so I checked its examples in the range to see how strictly Rimsky himself applies it (row 20).

## Holistic judgement

A conductor who knows Schumann's Adagio would accept this movement as professional work in the same tradition.

**Where it matches Schumann's plan:**
- one long-breathed main tune that returns intensified;
- a melancholy low-wind solo as the hinge (Schumann's bassoon, here the bass clarinet at 31–36);
- a middle section that turns two lines against each other (theme A's head against theme B at 53–56, the horn/clarinet counter-line at 53–60);
- a slowing, soft close on a held chord.

**Where it falls short of Schumann:**
- The middle section is lyrical rather than contrapuntal. Schumann's fugato gives his movement a second kind of motion that this one does not have. This is a matter of design and is now settled; I do not propose rewriting 37–60.
- Schumann's theme reaches the bassoon and oboe as a whole line. Here the woodwinds carry theme A only in fragments (the cor anglais alone plays a full theme, c041). HISTORY closes that point, so I only note it.

**Against v139:**
- Most changes are improvements: Vln II off the flute's tune at 37–40, clarinets above the horn at 57, harp fifths removed at 53–54, articulation at 63, 80–81 and 88.
- One thing got worse, at 31–36 (Finding 2).

## Findings

### 1. Bars 78–79: the grace run into B♭6 is unmeasured and played by three groups at once

**Problem.** Fl 1, Vln I and Vln II (an octave lower) all play the run as `\afterGrace c'''4( { f'''16 g''' aes''' }`, under "poco allargando", into the movement's registral peak.

**Evidence.** Rimsky PDF 141 says doubling "is only suitable in tempo; to apply it, in unison or octaves to a passage ad lib. is both ineffective and dangerous". When he doubles a run across groups, he writes it out measured: the sextuplets in piccolo, flute 1 and violins in Ex. 283 (PDF 454–455), and the measured triplets in flute and clarinet in Ex. 304 (PDF 493). Grace notes have no fixed place in the beat. Under an allargando, a flute and two string sections will each place them differently, which smears the approach to the peak.

**Fix (pitches, run and slurs kept).** Write the run out in tempo on the last eighth of 78:
- Vln I: `aes''2\f c'''8( \tuplet 3/2 { f'''16 g''' aes''' } | bes'''2.)~\ff`
- Vln II: `aes'2\f c''8( \tuplet 3/2 { f''16 g'' aes'' } |`
- Fl 1: `aes''2)\f \breathe c'''8( \tuplet 3/2 { f'''16 g''' aes''' } |`

**Protection.** "Grace run into B♭6 at 79" is protected (c008, c123). The reason given is the approach gesture to the peak. That gesture is kept note for note; only its notation changes. **Applied** in /tmp/critic-149/work (aTwo.ily).

### 2. Bars 33–36: the bass clarinet solo now ends in unison with the bassoon pedal (worse than v139)

**Problem.** Since c140 the solo lies an octave lower (E♭2–C3), so its cadence note A♭2 at 35–36 is the same note as Bsn 2's held A♭2 pedal. For a bar and a half the soloist is absorbed into the accompaniment. In v139 it landed on A♭3, an octave above the pedal.

**Evidence.** Rimsky PDF 139: the voice suffers when it meets harmony "in a register similar to [its] own". PDF 140–141: unison doubling "replaces by a mixed timbre the rare characteristic qualities" of the soloist.

**Fix.** Keep the solo's pitch, which is c140's reason (the motto at movement I's pitch), and the A♭ pedal under the diminished seventh at 33. Bassoon 2 leaves after 33 b2, and the contrabassoon, already holding A♭1 from 30, keeps the pedal to 36 with one breath:
- `transBsnTwo = { aes,2.~\pp | aes,2.~ | aes,2 r4 | R2.*3 | }`
- `transCbsn = { aes,,2.~ | aes,,2.~ | aes,,2 \breathe aes,,4~ | aes,,2.~ | aes,,2.~ | aes,,2 r4 | }`

The solo's A♭2 then sits an octave above the pedal, as Rimsky's low pedals under a melody do (Ex. 298, PDF 482; Ex. 292b, PDF 471). Winds stay alone, and the chord at 33 is unchanged in pitch content.

**Protection.** This touches the protected woodwind choir 31–36 "as of v141" only in the pedal instrument. It answers that protection's reason rather than undoing it. **Applied** in /tmp/critic-149/work (trans.ily).

### 3. Bars 87–88: horn 4 doubles the violas' F3–F♭3 under the clarinet's last tail (no change proposed)

**Problem.** Hn 4 (muted, pp) and the violas (sul C) both play F3–F♭3, with the cor anglais an octave above. Rimsky PDF 138–139 warns against strings doubled by brass and wind under a soloist.

**Why no change.** The line is the movement's F♭→E♭ thread. The doubling is soft, muted and two bars long, and c104, c127 and c147 protect the muted-horn close. I record it as a known exception that agrees with Ex. 292 (light scoring for a distant voice).

### 4. Bars 7–14: the horn solo's accompaniment is close four-part harmony in every bar (no change proposed)

**Problem.** Rimsky PDF 139 prefers fewer harmonic parts, pedals and rests under a soloist. Ex. 277 (PDF 444) accompanies an Adagio voice with off-beat string figures, one held horn and pizzicato bass.

**Why no change.** The horn solo and its accompaniment are on the standing list, and earlier cycles settled the voicing (c004, c019, c141). I note that this is the one place in the movement where the reading would ask for a thinner texture.
