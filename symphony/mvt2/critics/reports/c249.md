## Findings

### 1. Bars 23–28: the basses are divided in octaves on a moving bass line (applied)

**Problem.** Since c204 the basses play 23–28 "div." in octaves (`<aes,, aes,>`, `<f, f>`, …), with the lower half on A♭1–E♭2 and the upper half on A♭2–F3. This is the progression Rimsky-Korsakov's Note II rules out. The lower half plays on the E and A strings and the upper half on the D and G strings, so "unity of tone is impaired". The two halves of one section also sound 16′ and 8′ at once, so each half has half the players. At 24 b1 the upper half also lands on the cellos' F3 in unison, inside the counter-melody's register (PDF 54: the melody must stand in relief).

**Evidence.** PDF 62 Note II. Rimsky's own scores divide the basses only on held notes: Ex. 40 (PDF 218), Ex. 43 (PDF 220, a held second, half pizz., half arco) and Ex. 60 (PDF 231, a held octave pedal). None of them moves a bass line in divided octaves. PDF 56 gives the remedy for a bass line without cellos: support from bassoons, not division. Dvořák 9/ii shows the same restraint. Under the muted strings at 7–14 his basses rest (bassmL: `df4 r r2 R1*7`) and the bass is the divided cellos' 8′ line on D♭2–A♭2. He divides the basses in octaves only on the final held chord. His one moving octave divisi, the cellos at about 38–39, lasts two bars in a crescendo to f, with the basses in unison underneath.

**Answer to c204.** c204 had two points: the bass sounded only at 16′ on the duller E and A strings (PDF 27), and it lay more than an octave below the next part (PDF 85). Both are kept answered if the basses play the upper octave in unison. My first test was the reading's other remedy: basses in unison on the lower octave, with Bsn 2 on the upper octave (PDF 56, Ex. 10, PDF 68), as at 71–74. It built with 0 warnings but added a ninth page, because the bassoon staff appears in the 23–30 system. It also adds a wind to the protected string statement. I did not keep it.

**Fix (applied, sections/aOne.ily, aOneCb):**
```
aes,2.\p | f,4 bes,4 es4 | aes,2 des4 | es2. |
aes,4\< c4 des4 | \after 4 \mf \after 4 \> des2 bes,4 | es,2. | \after 2 \pp aes,,2.\p\> |
```
The "div." and "unis." texts are removed. The pitch classes, rhythm and dynamics of the bass do not change. In 23–28 the line is c204's upper half, except that 24 b1 is F2, not F3, so it no longer doubles the cellos' F3. The 16′ comes back at the cadence: E♭2 at 29 and A♭1 at 30, where the contrabassoon joins (c063). This also fits c229's point that a loud bass under muted strings at 28 turns heavy. Build: 0 warnings, 8 pages, same page breaks, 0 outer-voice parallels, allpairs_par.py identical. The parts book is 30 pages and the bass part stays on one page. If the composer prefers to keep 16′ under 23–28, the fallback is Bsn 2 on the upper octave with the basses in unison on the lower octave. That costs a page unless the layout is reworked.

### 2. Bar 18: cellos divided, C4 over C3 (recorded, no change)

The upper cellos' answer starts on C4 over the lower half's held C3, an octave for about a beat and a half. Note II is about progressions in octaves, and here one half holds while the other moves away. c109 and c191 recorded the same. No change.

### 3. Bars 53–56: Hn 1 + Cl 1 unison on the theme-A head at pp (recorded, no change)

PDF 66–67 says a soft, intimate phrase loses expression when doubled in unison, and that doubling belongs mainly to loud passages. Horn alone would be the "simple timbre". The line is protected with its doubling (c019, c201, c241). c019's reason was audibility inside the viola and harp figures, citing Rimsky PDF 74–75, where clarinet with horn softens the horn's attack. That reason still holds, so I make no change. I note the conflict with PDF 66–67 so that a future lens-1 critic can weigh it.

### 4. Holistic comparison with Dvořák 9/ii: would a conductor take this for a master's work? (recorded)

On most pages, yes. Bar by bar:
- **Opening.** Dvořák changes key over four bars of wind chords, then sets the solo over muted strings. This movement does the same with the common-tone link at 1–6 and the horn solo at 7–14 over winds.
- **Middle section.** Dvořák's is "Un poco più mosso" with pizzicato bass, soft string motion and woodwinds in pairs. Theme B at 37–44 matches that pacing: the same quarter-beat speed range (♩=60 against his 68) and a pizzicato bass with harp.
- **Climax and cyclic quotation.** Dvořák's trombones bring in earlier-movement material at the climax. This movement does the same at 63–64, and it is equally short.
- **Tempo words.** Dvořák uses 11 tempo words in 126 bars; this movement uses 11 in 92. The difference is small and c233 already recorded it.
- **Reprise.** This is the one structural difference. Dvořák's reprise (Tempo I) is shorter than its first statement and gets quieter: two solo violins, one solo cello, fermatas, a ppp close. This A′ (71–82) instead builds to a second ff peak at 79. c233 recorded this as two peaks of different kinds, and the A′ octaves and the B♭6 run are protected, so I do not reopen it. A conductor would hear the second peak as a Tchaikovsky or Rachmaninoff choice, not a Dvořák one. That is consistent with the brief's list of models.

On orchestration detail, the score now matches Rimsky's ch. II almost line for line; the only exception was Finding 1. One example: the cellos sul D with the bassoon in D♭ at 44–47 is the *Antar* [63] layout Rimsky cites on PDF 56.
