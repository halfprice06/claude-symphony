## Findings

On this second pass the reading still finds the tonal form sound. Every key area is introduced and confirmed the way PDF 443 and 445–446 describe (rows 1–5). The I–IV–V–I plan with the IV as the middle section is the textbook case of PDF 451–452 and 456. I found one pacing problem that the reading does not cover and the corpus does. It is not on any protected list, and I applied it. The other findings record checks, so later cycles need not repeat them.

### 1. The A section never goes above mf, so the first 200 s have no local peak (applied)

**Bars:** 27–30 (strings).

**Problem.** Intro, A section and transition (1–36) last about 125 s. The loudest moment in them is mf on 28 b2, Vln I B♭5, the high point of theme A's a′ phrase. B1 begins p again. The first f in the movement is at 59, about 205 s in (59% of the duration). So the tension curve is flat through the whole first third and then has two ff peaks close together (61 and 79). The a′ phrase is the A section's only phrase for full strings with the cello counter-melody. It rises to B♭5 over IV (28) and resolves through ii–V to the PAC at 30. Its contour asks for a real local climax, but the dynamics stop it one level short.

**Evidence.** A&S PDF 448–449 (§7): the great composers "convey a sense of purposeful motion over large as well as small musical spans", and the large-scale plan "involves more than key areas". The reading gives the principle but no dynamic rule, so the concrete evidence comes from the model. Dvořák 9/ii (corpus XML) takes its A section to f at 18 and ff at 25 and back to pp at 26. Only after that does the middle section build to the ff at 96–97. The A-section peak gives the listener a first, smaller summit, against which the middle-section climax is measured. In this score the A section's ceiling (mf) is two levels below B's (ff), so B's climax has no earlier reference point. The A section needs a peak that is clearly marked but stays below the later ones: f, one level under the ff at 61 and 79.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-103/work).** The arrival dynamic at 28 changes from mf to f in all five string parts. The hairpins stay as they are, and so does the p at 30, so 28 b2–30 becomes the diminuendo f → p. Nothing else changes: no pitches, scoring or tempo, and the cello counter-melody's line is untouched (c087).

The strings are con sordino except Vln I/II, which are "ord." from 27, so an f here is still well below the brass-supported ff of 61 and 79. If the composer finds f too strong for muted violas and cellos, the alternative is to leave the lower strings at mf and give only the violins f. That would break the five-part block dynamic, and I prefer to change all five.

### 2. The middle section's tonal plan matches the reading's model; keep it (no change)

**Bars:** 31–71. D♭ enters by phrase modulation at 37, with no V7 of D♭ before it. PDF 452 calls this the typical entry of IV in ABA and rondo forms ("right at the beginning of the new section without a transition"). The six-bar woodwind passage 31–36 stays on the tonic, so it is a link and not a modulating transition. IV is confirmed three times: half close at 44, authentic at 52–53, and the main cadence at 59–61 (IV, ♯iv°7, V7, I), which fits PDF 443 ("after intervening material"). The excursion at 47–50 is the transient modulation of PDF 447–448. It reaches F♯ minor through vi and C♯7 (= D♭7), and it is foreshadowed by A♮ at 40 and 46, as the reading's K. 498 example foreshadows its C minor. At 61–65 IV moves to V of the home key, as in Ex. 26-11, through ♭VI and the A minor colour, the chromatic form of the VI upper neighbour in Ex. 26-17. Nothing here needs to change. Bar 45 (vi, c093), bar 61's 5̂ on top (c093) and the stable tonic at 31–36 (c093) remain settled.

### 3. Every arrival in a new key has the melodic and rhythmic resolution of Ex. 26-4 (no change)

**Bars:** 36–37, 49–50, 52–53, 60–61, 70–71, 81–82. At each arrival I checked the line that leads in and the beat it lands on (PDF 445–446). 37: F5 (3̂) on the downbeat, with the bass clarinet's A♭–B♭–C rising to D♭. 50: C♯5 over the F♯ minor tonic, with 3̂ (A4) on beat 4. 53: F5 (3̂) after the violins' C5 pickup. 61: Vln I C6–D♭6. 71: A♭5 after the E♭–F pickup. 82: A♭5. All of them land on a downbeat with the expected accidentals, so none is "shipwrecked". No change.

### 4. The dominant at 67–68 carries its 7th only as a passing note (no change; protected)

**Bars:** 65–70. PDF 456 (§15): when V comes back it "often supports a 7th in order to lead convincingly to I". The 7th D♭ is held in Vln II at 65–66 and Bsn 2 at 69–70. At 67–68 the only D♭ is Hn 1's passing D♭4 on 68 b2, over the E♭ pedal in the basses and timpani. c013 noted the same thing. The passage is protected (dominant pedal with the horn tail, 65–70, c098), and the reason given there still holds: the solo horn sounds over a bare pedal, which is part of the string-free design. The 7th is present at both ends of the six bars, and I see no case for adding a held D♭ under the solo. Record only.

### 5. Coda-to-reprise ratio (no change; confirmed closed)

**Bars:** 71–92. The coda (39 s plus fermata) is almost as long as the reprise (43 s). In Dvořák's movement the coda is shorter in bars (7 against about 19) but is slowed by the Molto Adagio. c013 already cut this coda from 14 to 10 bars, and c023 confirmed the section proportions. The coda is the reading's I–IV–I expansion (PDF 456 footnote), and it frames the movement with the introduction's C–D♭–C over iv, as Dvořák's coda frames his with its opening chords. I do not reopen it.
