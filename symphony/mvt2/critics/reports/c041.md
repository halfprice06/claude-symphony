## What else I studied

I compared the movement with Dvořák 9/ii in the local corpus (`reference-corpus/mutopia/DvorakA_O95/Sym9/Sym9-lys/woods.notesL.ily`, `oboeImL`). In Dvořák the minor-key middle theme at bar 46 (C♯ minor, pp, triplet head) belongs to oboe 1, with the flute. The cor anglais keeps the main theme. So Dvořák gives the two double reeds separate jobs within one movement. I read the full melodic text of every section file, checked HISTORY.md for earlier melody findings (c001, c011, c021, c031), and looked at the engraved pages of the scratch build.

## Melodic verdict in brief

Theme A is singable and well placed. Its horn, violin and A′ statements are protected, and I leave them alone. Theme B has been improved by c031's head rhythm and is now distinct from A. What the movement still lacks is a single tune that belongs to a woodwind. The reading is about exactly this: the oboe's and clarinet's lyric registers and the flute's long-note intensity. The cor anglais is well used. The oboe, which in Adler's examples and in the Dvořák model is the slow movement's second voice, has only answering cells. The findings below are mostly about how the existing tunes are presented. None of them rewrites a theme.

## Findings (ranked)

### 1. Bars 45–47: give theme B's B♭-minor head to oboe 1 instead of the first violins (applied)

**Problem.** Theme B is stated by flute 1 (37), flute and cor anglais (41), the first violins (45, with cellos), the first violins again (53, sul A), and the tutti (57). The statements at 45 and 53 have the same colour in the same octave, eight bars apart. That weakens the violins' entry at 53, which starts the animando build. The oboe also never sings a phrase of either theme. It has two-bar answers at 15–16 and 19–20, and after that it only doubles.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 198: the oboe "is basically a lyrical instrument". His examples (Schumann 2/iii "p cantabile", Ex. VII-34; Beethoven 3/ii, Schubert 9/ii and Tchaikovsky 4/ii cited on PDF 199) are slow-movement themes. The head lies at A♭4–E♭5, which Ex. VII-32 marks "warm, prominent, reedy and poignant", inside the "most effective range … F in the first space to the C above the staff" (PDF 198). PDF 184: give each idea to the instrument it suits best. The B♭-minor turn of a major-key theme suits a poignant reed. At 47 the oboe's C♯5 diminishes into the cor anglais' pickup C♯4 on 47 b4, a hand-off between sister instruments (PDF 204–205: same family and principle, the cor anglais more melancholy). That leads straight into the movement I quotation. Dvořák 9/ii does the same: oboe 1 has the minor middle theme at bar 46.

**Fix (applied), `sections/bSec.ily`:**
```
% bSecOboeOne, replacing R1*12 at 45-56
des''2(\p^\markup \bold "1."^\markup \italic "dolce espr." es''8 des''8 c''4) | bes'4( aes'8 bes'8 des''2) | cis''2.\> r4\! |
R1*9 |
% bSecVlnOne, 45-52 now rests; "senza sord." moves to the entry at 53
R1*4 | R1*4 |
f''2(\p\<^\markup \italic "senza sord., sul A" ges''8 f''8 es''4) | ...
```
The cellos keep the lower octave (c011). Oboe 2 rests, so the paired staff has a single dynamic. The first violins now rest 37–52 and enter fresh at 53. Pitches and harmony are unchanged, and `allpairs_par.py` shows only the cello octave doubling, now under the oboe instead of Vln I. This supersedes the hairpin form of c038's "al niente at 47" in Vln I. The oboe ends with \> on a quarter rest, because an oboe al niente is less safe than a string one.

### 2. Bar 78: a breath for flute 1 before the ff B♭6 (applied)

**Problem.** Flute 1 plays 75–80 without a breath: four bars of theme A rising to the f arrival at 78, the grace run, and then B♭6 ff held four beats into A♭6. At the written tempi (♩=52 easing to 46) that is about 22 seconds, and it ends on the movement's highest note at its loudest. The c036 breath comes after 80, too late. A flutist will snatch air somewhere, most likely just before the B♭6. That weakens the attack on the note that makes the A′ climax.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 188: the flute is "very effective and intense in slower, long-note melodies", but "requires a great deal of breath … give the flutist time to rest after especially difficult or sustained passages". Brahms 1/iv (Ex. VII-9) has flute 2 hold the long notes so flute 1 can breathe. PDF 186: above A6 notes "are difficult to produce, and extreme caution must be used". The slur already breaks between 78 b2 (end of the phrase) and the grace run, so a breath fits the phrasing.

**Fix (applied), `sections/aTwo.ily`, `aTwoFluteOne` bar 78:**
```
aes''2)\f \breathe \afterGrace c'''4( { f'''16 g''' aes''' } |
```
The violins, oboe 1 and clarinet 1 carry the line through the breath. The pitches and the grace run (protected, c036/c039) are unchanged.

### 3. Bar 85: start the last motto tail with the clarinet's niente attack (applied)

**Problem.** The movement's last melodic statement, the tail E♭5–D5–D♭5–C5 in clarinet 1 (85–89), begins with an articulated pp over a ppp string pedal. It is the quietest melodic entry in the movement, and it is given to the one wind that can start a note from nothing.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 195: "One effect that can be accomplished on the clarinet better than on any other woodwind instrument is the niente attack, in which the tone starts from almost complete silence without articulation". Ex. VII-57 notates it as a crescendo from a circled N. E♭5 sounding (written F5) is in the clarion register, "bright, incisive, expressive" (Ex. VII-56, PDF 210), where the clarinet can play "the faintest pianissimo" (PDF 210).

**Fix (applied), `sections/coda.ily`, `codaClOne` bar 85:**
```
\once \override Staff.Hairpin.circled-tip = ##t \after 2 \pp es''2.(\<^\markup \italic "dolce" | d''2.) \breathe | ...
```
Clarinet 2 rests at 85–86, so the shared staff has one dynamic. The override is at Staff level (HISTORY note at c038). The engraving shows the circled-tip crescendo reaching pp on beat 3. The open point "Cl 1 fade at 89–90 (needs matching Cl 2)" is unaffected.

### 4. Bar 41: flute 1's A♭4 sits in its weakest register over the cor anglais' strongest (option, not applied)

**Problem.** At 41 theme B's second phrase starts on A♭4–D♭5 in flute 1 and A♭3–D♭4 in the cor anglais. The flute is in its "weak, but luscious" low octave and its "sweet and with little carrying power" middle (Ex. VII-2, PDF 185). The cor anglais is in its lowest sixth, which Adler calls "richly beautiful, with tremendous expressive carrying power" (PDF 205). For about two beats the lower octave leads, and the flute colour that defined 37–40 drops out. It returns at 42 (A♭5).

**Fix (option).** `bSecFluteOne` bar 41: `r2 f''4.(\< ges''8 | aes''4\mp\> ...`. The cor anglais then starts the phrase alone in its best register and the flute joins on F5. That is an antiphonal entry rather than an octave doubling that starts upside down. I did not apply it because cycle 36 protects "flute over thin strings 37–44 (Eh from 41)". The change keeps both parts of that entry, but the composer should decide.

### 5. Observation: no woodwind except the cor anglais has a phrase of theme A or B (not applied)

The clarinet, "the nightingale of the orchestra" (PDF 210), has only the coda tail. The bass clarinet's melodic moment (31–34) lies in its throat and clarion registers, written F4–F5, not in the chalumeau that Adler calls its "most distinctive, the warmest, and the most effective" (PDF 217; Wagner examples VII-74/75 are in the chalumeau). An octave down would put the solo under bassoon 2's A♭2 pedal, and 31–36 is protected, so I propose no change there. Finding 1 gives the oboe its place. I record the clarinet point for the composer's overall view.
