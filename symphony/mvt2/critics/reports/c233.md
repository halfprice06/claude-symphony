For this lens I took from it what it shows about pacing: how a solo is introduced, how interludes sit between solo statements, how the forces change at section joins, and how a passage closes.

## Findings

Nothing in v233 goes against the pacing principles in this reading. Every section join overlaps, each tutti is followed by a sharp reduction, each solo colour is kept apart from its own family, and the timpani join the coda's two phrases as in Ex. XVII-13. I recommend no change and did not edit a scratch copy. v233's own build-warnings.txt is empty. The points below are what I checked, ranked by how close each came to a recommendation.

### 1. Bar 37: theme B starts on the same downbeat as its accompaniment (checked, no change)

**Point.** Sibelius gives his soloist three bars of muted violins first, and Bartók one bar (PDF 268–272, Ex. XVII-11, XVII-12). Tchaikovsky's Canzonetta does it with eleven bars of winds (Ex. XVII-7). At 37 the flute's F5, the violas' triplets, the harp chord, Vln II and the pizzicato basses all start on beat 1, after the poco rit. at 35–36. The listener hears the new key, tempo, texture and tune at once.

**Why no change.** Adler's examples are first entries of a concerto soloist, where the soloist is new. Theme B is a new section of a symphonic movement, and the main model does what this score does. In Dvořák 9/ii the Un poco più mosso at 46 begins its flute/oboe tune on the first beat, with the accompaniment starting in the same bar (`woods.notesL.ily`, first bar after `\barNumberCheck#TwomL`). The cor anglais' C5 pickup at 36 b3 already hands the tune to the flute on a common note (c139). The dark bare fifth at 35–36 (protected, c184/c213) is what makes 37 sound fresh. A preparatory bar would also need an added bar, and the proportions are protected (c023). If the composer ever wants the effect, the smallest version is in-bar: violas and harp alone on 37 b1–2, flute entering on b3. But that breaks theme B's rising fourth C–F across the bar line, which c071 set up. Not proposed.

### 2. Tempo slowings: seven in 92 bars (checked, no change)

**Point.** The movement slows at 35–36 (poco rit.), 47–48 (poco rit., poco sostenuto), 60 (poco allargando), 61 (Largamente), 64 (allargando), 78–82 (poco allargando) and 89–92 (rit.). Dvořák 9/ii has five tempo drops in 127 bars: poco ritard. into Poco meno mosso at 53–54, Meno at 78, Meno mosso/Tempo I at 101, rit. at 119 and rit./Molto Adagio at 125 (`percussion.notesL.ily`, conductor). The Canzonetta has three in 118 bars: rit. at 38, rallent. at 111 and riten. molto at 116 (`mvmt2/global.ily`). Mozart's interludes in Ex. XVII-10 change the forces, not the tempo (PDF 267).

**Why no change.** Every slowing here falls at a place where Dvořák or Tchaikovsky also slows. 35–36 is the end of an interlude before the contrasting section, like the Canzonetta's rit. at 38 in its five-bar interlude. 47–48 holds back the quotation, as Dvořák's 53–54 does (the c048 comment in global.ily). 60–61 and 64 are the approach to the climax and the quotation's tail (c153 already moved the broadening off the quotation's head, following PDF 267). 78–82 and 89 match Dvořák's rit. / a tempo / rit. shape at 119–125 almost bar for bar, with 6 a-tempo bars at 83–88 against his 5 at 120–124. The density is higher, but the movement is also shorter. None of the slowings sits in the middle of a phrase. I record the count for the next tempo or expression critic, so it is not raised again.

### 3. A′ returns in the second statement's colour (checked, no change)

**Point.** Dialogue fixes the soloist's timbre in the listener's mind (PDF 257). Dvořák 9/ii brings the Largo tune back at 101 in the cor anglais, the colour of its first statement. Here theme A came first in the horn (7) and returns at 71 in the violins, its second colour (23).

**Why no change.** The horn plays the motto's tail alone at 67–70, so the reprise gives the theme's two colours in the order of the first A section (horn, then violins), compressed into one join. The horn then moves to the background counter-melody at 78–81, which is the role exchange of PDF 261–262. The A′ texture is protected (c071, c219 finding 5). This follows the reading by another route.

### 4. Two ff peaks, 61 and 79 (checked, no change)

**Point.** Dvořák's Largo has one ff area (90–100, with the cyclic quotations) and a soft reprise. This score has the B climax at 61–64 and a second ff at 79–81. In analysis.txt the A′ peak is a little denser (15.9 voices against 15.4) and higher (B♭6 against A6).

**Why no change.** Adler's chapter says nothing on the number of climaxes. Cycle 8 set the A′ plan p→ff, and builds 75–79 and releases 80–83 are protected (c213, c218, c228). The two peaks differ in kind. 61–64 is a brass climax with the trombone quotation and an exit through F♭/E. 79–81 is the theme's appoggiatura in three string-and-wind octaves, with no trombones (c024, c209). Their time positions (0.65 and 0.84) leave 68 s between them, with a 21 s pp retransition. Tchaikovsky 5/ii and Rachmaninoff 2/iii also have a full reprise climax. Recorded only.

### 5. Interludes: three six-bar soft solos over a pedal (checked, no change)

**Point.** The link (1–6), the transition (31–36) and the retransition (65–70) are all six bars, all pp–mp, and all a wind or horn line over a held pedal. Mozart's interludes in Ex. XVII-10 contrast with the solo in mass (tutti f against solo p).

**Why no change.** In this movement the interludes contrast with what comes before them, not with what comes after. 31 follows the f at 28 and the cadence at 30, and 65 follows the ff climax. That is the reduction after a tutti that PDF 267 (item 3) describes. They also differ in function (common-tone link, tonic pedal, dominant pedal), in solo colour (horn 3, bass clarinet, horn 1) and in register. All three are protected as held-pedal transitions (c023, c213, c223). Dvořák's equivalents (the opening chorale 1–6 and the quotation block 90–100) are of similar length.
