## Findings (ranked)

### F1. The tonicization curve matches two corpus slow movements (follows; record)

**Reading.** Applied chords make tonicized areas inside the phrase (PDF 419). Chords applied to V "significantly increase their drive to the dominant" (PDF 422 §4).

**Evidence.** I measured the share of running time taken by applied chords (dominant-function chords of a goal other than the section's tonic) in tenths of the movement. I used the same rule for the score and for the MCTL annotations, which mark applied chords as "x/y" degrees.

| Mozart 41/ii (%) | 6 | 0 | 15 | 21 | 11 | 12 | **28** | 8 | 7 | 4 |
| Tchaikovsky 6/iv (%) | 21 | 4 | 4 | 16 | 9 | 12 | **29** | 4 | 16 | 0 |

All three peak in the 60–70 % window. In this score that window is bars 56–64: VII°7/vi at 56, V7/IV at 58 b4, VII°7/V at 59 b3, then E = V of A minor at 62 b3 and its VII°7 at 64 b3. After the peak, all three drop to a few percent. Tchaikovsky's coda (from beat 438) has no applied chords at all, and neither does this score's coda. One difference: Tchaikovsky's recapitulation build (80–90 %) uses applied chords, while this score's second peak (77–81) uses mixture (♭VI at 78, iiø7 with F♭ at 80 b3, V♭9 at 81). So the two climaxes are reached by different harmonic means: the B climax by applied chords to IV and V, and the A′ climax by the protected F♭–E♭ thread. That difference is useful and should stay. Keep.

### F2. Bar 21 is a chromaticized voice exchange with a passing 6/4 into the French sixth (follows; new identification)

**Reading.** PDF 421, Ex. 25-7: a cross relation, even between outer voices, "can be logical and beautiful when it results from a chromaticized voice exchange". PDF 422, Ex. 25-8a: it is softened by a passing chord, "most frequently a 6/4". PDF 426 §7: these exchanges are "particularly frequent in passages that connect IV with V".

**Evidence.** At 21 the outer voices are bass D♭2–E♭2–F♭2 (Cb, aOne.ily `des,4 es,4 fes,4`) and top F5–E♭5–D5 (violins). The chords are IV (b1), a passing I6/4 (b2, C–E♭–A♭) and the French sixth F♭–A♭–B♭–D (b3), which goes to V at 22. The bass's D♭ becomes the soprano's D♮ and the soprano's F becomes the bass's F♭: an exchange with both tones altered and a passing 6/4 between. These are the exact figures of Ex. 25-8a (5/3 – 6/4 – ♯6/♭5). The D♭/D♮ and F/F♭ cross relations between the outer voices come from the exchange, so they are the kind PDF 421 allows. The scan in F7 finds no other outer-voice cross relation in the movement. In form terms, this half cadence is the one point in the A section where the dominant is approached by an altered II/IV with ♯4̂ (D5) in the top voice (PDF 423 §5). That marks the end of the contrasting b phrase more strongly than the cadences of the two a phrases (13–14 and 29–30), which are diatonic. Keep.

### F3. The retransition reaches V of A♭ through a chord with two meanings (follows; new identification)

**Reading.** PDF 422 (Ex. 25-9): one chord can be an applied chord locally and an altered chord of the main key over the long range. PDF 424 §6: ♯4̂ in the bass "emphasize[s] dominant harmony".

**Evidence.** At 64 b3 the bass is D2 (B.tbn, Vc, Cb) with violas A♭3 (G♯3) and Vln II B3 (C♭4). In A minor this is the leading-tone diminished chord over D. In A♭ it is D–A♭–C♭, VII°7 of V (D–F–A♭–C♭) without its F, with ♯4̂ in the bass. At b4, E7 with Tbn 1's F3 (V♭9 of A minor = German sixth of A♭, c210) moves to E♭7 at 65. So the bass approaches 5̂ from both sides: D (♯4̂) and then F♭ (♭6̂). This is the only place where an applied chord to A♭'s dominant appears inside the remote key. The return to the home dominant is therefore prepared in the last bar of the A-minor quotation, before the listener hears it as such. The ♯4̂ is not resolved directly (D goes up to E, then to E♭). The reading's model (Ex. 25-12) resolves ♯4̂–5̂ at once, but here the protected F♭–E–E♭ approach (62–65) and the quotation's E goal take precedence, and 5̂ still arrives in the bass at 65. Keep; do not reopen.

### F4. The doubled A♭ at 62 b3 is a common tone, not a doubled leading tone (follows; record)

**Reading.** PDF 419 and 439 (Point 3): do not double the temporary leading tone.

**Evidence.** At 62 b3 the A♭ (G♯ once the chord turns out to be V of A minor) sounds in seven parts, A♭3–A♭6 (Fl 1, Ob, Cl, Hn 1·2, Tbn 2, violas, Vln I). But while it sounds, the chord is spelled and heard as F♭–A♭–C♭ (♭III of D♭, protected key plan). The A♭ is the held melody note from 61, so in this context it is a stable common tone. The chord takes on its applied meaning only at 63, which is the double meaning of PDF 422. On the resolution, only the top line moves A♭6–A6 (Vln I with Fl 1 in unison), a chromatic step in one voice (Ex. 25-3a "good"). The other winds, Tbn 2 and the violas stop at 63 or take other notes, so there are no octaves on the resolution, which is the problem Ex. 25-14c warns about. The other temporary leading tones (19 b3 and 20 b3 E, 40 and 56 A, 47 E♯, 58 b4 F, 59 b3 G, 64 b3 D) sound in one part or as an octave doubling of one line (harp with the basses at 40; violins in octaves at 72). Keep.

### F5. I♭7 over the coda's tonic pedal: tested at 85 b3, not recommended (record; answers c302's reason, still declines)

**Reading.** PDF 439, Exercise 1b: "A♭: tonic pedal using I♭7 as V of IV". Also Ex. 25-19 and the PDF 428 fragment.

**History.** c302 tested G♭ at 86 and rejected it because it made a diminished third against Cl 1's D♮ (the motto tail's ♯4̂). c203 rejected the same chord at 36.

**Test.** I put the G♭ one beat earlier, before the D♮ enters: in a separate scratch copy, violas upper half at 85 `<c'~ es'>2 <c' ges'>4 | <c' f'>2.` (was `<c' es'>2. |`). The G♭4 resolves to the violas' F4 at 86 and never sounds with D♮. The inner line then descends G♭4–F4–F4 (C.a.)–F♭4–E♭4 in parallel sixths under the clarinet's E♭5–D5–D♭5–C5. Build in /tmp/critic-473/test-gb: 0 warnings, 0 outer-voice parallels, 8 pages. The sonority at 85 b3 is A♭–C–E♭–G♭.

**Why I do not recommend it.** (1) The movement's model does without it. Dvořák 9/ii's coda turns to IV over the tonic pedal (cellos G♭2 under the basses' D♭, 105–109) and to iv (B♭♭ in Vln II, 113) before I, with no ♭7 on the tonic (strings.notesL.ily from bar 101). This score does the same at 87–88. (2) A♭7 points to D♭ on the beat before the clarinet's D♮. The D♮ would then sound like a turn away from the expected D♭ instead of a step in the motto tail over a plain tonic, which is the point of 85–86 (c302). (3) The coda would gain a third chromatic event in four bars (G♭, D♮, F♭) where the plan thins and stills the texture (c199). The objection is no longer the diminished third. It is that the chord adds a harmonic goal the coda does not need. Not applied to /tmp/critic-473/work.

### F6. Deceptive cadences lead to stronger arrivals, without applied chords (follows in function; record)

PDF 427: the applied chord at a deceptive cadence delays the authentic cadence and makes it stronger. The score's two deceptive moves (V7→vi at 44→45; V→♭VI at 77→78, soprano on 1̂) are diatonic or mixture, not applied chords. The delayed cadences arrive at 60–61 (through the one ♯4̂-in-the-bass cadence, 59) and at 79–82. This is the function the reading describes, and c333 recorded it. Nothing new to add, and no reason to change.

### F7. Cross relations, the augmented-second risk, and sequence figuration (follows; record)

An outer-voice scan (bass on one beat against the top note on the next, and the reverse) finds 24 semitone pairs. All of them are leading-tone steps between different letter names, the chromatic step in one voice at 62–63, or the voice exchange at 21 (F2). None is a forbidden outer-voice cross relation (PDF 420). The falling-third sequence 37–40 (PDF 437–438) has its applied VII°7/vi at 40 with the A♮ only in the bass (Cb and harp). The viola figure at 40 is C–E♭–G♭, so no melodic G♭–A♮ augmented second arises (the PDF 438 note). The root-position chords are figured by the triplets, so they do not sound choppy (PDF 437).
