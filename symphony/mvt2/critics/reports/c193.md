## Findings

The movement's modulations and key plan agree with the reading at every point I checked. I propose no change to the music. The findings below record the checks that earlier passes (c103, c183) did not make, so later critics need not repeat them. Findings 1 and 2 answer the task's question whether the key plan reads as one shape: it does.

### 1. The same descending-third plan, I–vi–IV–V–I, runs at two levels (no change)

**Bars:** 15–22 and 44–53.

**Reading.** PDF 452–454 §12: "A most important function of tonicized VI is to lead down in 3rds from I to IV." Ex. 26-13 shows this inside one phrase of a Beethoven Adagio: I, (VI) at bar 3, IV at bar 5, V at bar 7. Ex. 26-14 and the Beethoven plan on PDF 454 (I–VI–IV–V–I) show it across a whole section.

**Phrase level, 15–22.** I6 at 15, IV at 16, then F minor is tonicized: vi at 17 and 19, vii°6 of vi at 19 b3, V7 of vi at 20 b3. The V7 resolves deceptively to D♭ (IV) at 21, and the French sixth leads to V at 22. That is Ex. 26-13's I–(VI)–IV–V in eight bars, with IV reached as the deceptive resolution of vi's dominant.

**Section level, 44–53, in D♭.** The half cadence on A♭ at 44 is followed by B♭ minor (vi) at 45–46, then G♭ major (IV) at 46 b3. D♭7, the applied V7 of IV, follows at 47, and G♭ turns minor at 48–50 (spelled F♯ minor). A♭7 (V7) at 51–52 leads to D♭ at 53. That is I–(vi)–IV/iv–V7–I, the plan of Ex. 26-14 and PDF 454, with the IV darkened by mixture. Exercise 1f on PDF 457 asks for exactly the first step: from D♭ to B♭ minor with V of D♭ as the pivot. At 44–45 the A♭ chord is V of D♭ and VII of B♭ minor (natural form).

**Foreshadowing.** PDF 448: in K. 498 a small progression "foreshadows the larger tonicization". Here A°7–B♭ minor at 40 (vii°7/vi inside theme B's first phrase) foreshadows the B♭ minor of 45. The same progression at 56 recalls it. PDF 452 calls VI "the easiest minor triad to tonicize" because only its leading tone is needed. That is what the score does: A♮ is the only accidental at 40, 46 and 56.

**Why keep it.** The A section's b phrase and the B section's middle group use one tonal gesture at two sizes. The large plan (Finding 2) then carries it through the whole movement as I–IV–V–I. This is the "purposeful motion over large as well as small musical spans" of PDF 448 §7, and it is the main reason the key plan reads as one shape. Nothing in it should be changed.

### 3. The F♯-minor area is a mixture key with a two-stage pivot (no change)

**Bars:** 46–53.

**Reading.** PDF 443 §2: remote keys come in degrees, and a key that relates "indirectly … through mixture" is the nearer kind. PDF 443 §3: the pivot "can be any chord that belongs to both keys". PDF 444: an applied chord can intensify a modulation.

**What the score does.** F♯ minor is G♭ minor, iv of D♭ by mixture, so it is the nearer kind of remote key. The pivot works in two stages:
- G♭ major at 46 b3 is IV of D♭. It is also the major form of the new tonic.
- D♭7 at 47 is V7 of IV in D♭ and V7 in F♯ minor. Held for a whole bar under the poco rit., it is the applied chord that, in PDF 444's words, intensifies the modulation.

The cadence iv–N6–V–i at 49–50 contains II and IV forms (Points 2, PDF 456). The way back is A♭7 at 51–52, which is V7 of V in F♯ minor and V7 in D♭.

**The one risk the reading names, and why it is covered.** PDF 451: after emphasis on IV, I can sound like V of IV. At 53 the D♭ chord is also C♯, the dominant of the F♯ minor just left, so it could be heard as V. Two things prevent that. First, two bars of A♭7 (51–52) fix the D♭ meaning before 53, with the departing tonic F♯ heard as G♭, the chord's 7th (c183). Second, the full IV–V6/5 of V–V7–I cadence at 59–61 confirms D♭ before the climax. No change.

### 4. The way out of IV: ♭VI as upper neighbour, then ♭ii before V (no change; protected)

**Bars:** 61–65.

**Reading.** PDF 456 §15 and Points 12 (PDF 457): VI is the upper neighbour of V, and when V comes back it "is often preceded by a II or IV and often supports a 7th".

**Score.** The bass runs D♭ (61), G♭ (62), F♭/E (62 b3), A (63), D (64 b3), E (64 b4), E♭ (65). F♭, the flat sixth degree, is the upper neighbour, and it resolves to E♭ through the A-minor chord, whose fifth E is the same pitch. A minor is the enharmonic B♭♭ minor, a lowered II, so the returning V is preceded by a form of II and that II's °7 (64 b3). V carries its 7th from its first chord (65). c180 recorded this as ♭VI–♭ii–applied °7–V. This pass adds the reading's own wording and the Beethoven 9/iii parallel (Finding 2). The passage is protected, and the reading gives a further reason to keep it.

### 5. The subdominant weight of the whole movement does not undermine the tonic (no change)

**Bars:** 5, 37–61, 79–80, 87–89.

**Point.** D♭ appears at every level of this movement: iv6/4 in the link (5), the IV key area (37–61), the first ff climax on D♭ (61), and the second ff climax over a D♭ bass (79–80, ii6/5 with B♭6 on top). The coda's IV6–iv–I (87–89) repeats it. PDF 451 warns that "too much emphasis on IV … can upset our sense of tonality".

**Why it is not a fault here.** The warning concerns IV as a key early in the piece. In this score the IV key starts at 125 s (36%). The A section treats D♭ only as a predominant chord (9, 11–12, 16, 21, 25, 27–28), and no G♭ sounds in 1–36. Every tonic section closes with V(7)–I: 13–14, 29–30, 70–71 after six bars of V7, and 81–82. The coda's plagal I–IV–I is the case PDF 456's footnote calls "the most important of these possibilities". The coherence files also depend on it: D♭ as the symphony's Neapolitan, resolved in the finale (c180). No change.
