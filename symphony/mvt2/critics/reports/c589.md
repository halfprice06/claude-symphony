## Findings (ranked)

**F1. Would a conductor believe a master wrote this? Compared with Brahms Op. 15/ii (recorded, no change).**
Both movements open in a dark, low wind colour over a held string or wind chord. Brahms gives the theme to the bassoons with the strings; this score gives theme A to Hn 1 over bassoons, bass clarinet and harp (7-14). Both pass the line to the upper strings for the second statement (here 23-30, violins in unison with the cello counter-melody). Both keep a hushed, devotional tone, and both end on a long tonic pedal with the motion running down to nothing (here 83-92: A♭ in the basses under the harp, timpani and the plagal close).

They differ in how they build intensity. In Brahms the orchestra seldom goes past f and never uses the brass for a climax; the soloist supplies the peaks. This score has two ff tuttis (61-62, 79-80) and a brass intrusion at 63-64. That comes from the Dvořák 9/ii model in the brief, and Adler's examples support it: Schubert 9 (Ex. XI-19, PDF 37) holds the trombones back for one event, and Franck (Ex. XI-20, PDF 38) builds the second climax from horns with the woodwinds and no heavy brass, as 79-81 does. A conductor would read the scoring as professional work in the Brahms-Dvořák line. The distance from a master that remains is in the length and growth of the themes, not in the orchestral craft. Those lines are protected (horn solo 7-14; c441 theme record) and I do not reopen them.

**F3. Colour held back for the climax and the cyclic event, 1-64 (recorded, no change).**
Adler's first climax technique is to save a colour for one event (PDF 37; Ex. XI-19). His Hindemith example saves the tuba for a later variation (PDF 20). Here the trombones and tuba are silent for 59 bars. Tbn 2, B.tbn and tuba enter pp< at 60, under the theme as the d'Indy page advises (PDF 147). Tbn 1 has its only melodic entry at 63, ff marcato, for the movement I quotation. The diminuendo out of the tutti follows Schubert's Rosamunde (Ex. XI-12, PDF 28). At 63 Tbn 2 and the tuba stop, and the bass trombone alone goes sf> p with the strings, so the quotation sits over one low brass voice. This matches the reading and is protected (quotation 63-64; lean climax 61-62).

**F4. Theme A rescored three times with one recognisable texture, 7-14, 23-30, 71-78 (recorded, no change).**
Adler's Mahler 4 page (Ex. XV-20 to XV-22, PDF 169-171) shows a passage that comes back three times. Each time it is scored differently, but the texture and chord structure stay recognisable, which unifies the form. Theme A follows that plan. First it is Hn 1 over bassoons, bass clarinet and harp, with no strings. Then it is the violins in unison with the cello counter-melody and plain accompaniment (protected, cycle 7). Last it is the violins in octaves, with the eighth-note figure in the violas, the cello counter-line, and Hn 2 and Bsn 1 joining it at 75. The Schubert 8 page (Ex. XV-19, PDF 168-169) adds that unison melody against harmony in other timbres keeps foreground and background apart. That is how 71-78 works.

**F5. Foreground, middleground and background at 37-44 and 57-60 (recorded, no change).**
At 37-44 the theme is Fl 1 alone. It sits over viola triplets, Vln II sustained sul tasto chords, pizzicato basses and harp. This is Adler's foreground-background model (Francesca, Ex. XV-6, PDF 154-156): a contrasting accompaniment colour, pizzicato to set off the solo, and sustained strings as the adhesive (Smetana, PDF 154). At 41 the cor anglais enters after a rest, which is Adler's way of making a new colour heard (PDF 156).

At 57-60 each element is mixed from different choirs, as in the Meistersinger and Scheherazade examples (PDF 177-179). The melody is in Fl 1, Ob 1 and Vln I. The counter-line is in Hn 1 and the cellos, the horn-and-cello doubling of Ex. XI-10 (PDF 27). The figure is in the violas, and the bass in Cb, Bsn 2 and B.cl. The rhythms are distinct: half notes, dotted figures, triplets and then sextuplets.

One check under Schoenberg's rule (PDF 184): at 57 Fl 1 and Ob 1 are marked p while Vln I, on the same line, carries over mf and crescendos to f at 58 b4. The winds double the principal voice; they are not a separate subsidiary line. Marking them a step under the strings adds sheen without weight. That is Mozart's use of flute and oboe in Ex. XV-3 (PDF 150-151). All three reach f together at 59. I record this as consistent and propose no change.

**F6. Soft and controlled chord ends, 63, 81-82, 92 (recorded, no change).**
The La Mer and Brahms 3 pages (Ex. XV-13, XV-16, PDF 162-165) warn that a ff-to-pp chord needs its top notes in instruments that can play soft there. They also warn that a high soft wind chord sounds louder than written. At 63 the top is Vln I divided E6/A6 tremolo sf> p, and the Fl 1 A6 is a single sf quarter, released at once. At 81-82 the highest sustained notes are violins and Fl 1·2 in the middle of their range, p> pp. The last chord (92) is muted strings ppp with Vln I C5 on top and no high winds. All three follow the reading.

**F7. Climax percussion and the second climax, 60-61 and 78-81 (recorded, no change).**
Bloch marks his rolls p then crescendo so they do not swamp the themes (PDF 184). The timpani here do the same: pp< f into 61, pp< into the f roll at 79, ppp "near the rim" under the horn at 67-70. The second climax at 79 uses horns a2 with full winds and strings and no trombones. That is Franck's way (Ex. XI-20, PDF 38), and the opposite of the brass-led first climax, so the two peaks sound different. This was settled by c024, c060 and c454, and the reading confirms it.
