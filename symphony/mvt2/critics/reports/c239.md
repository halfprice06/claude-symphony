## What else I studied

Mozart 41/ii in the corpus PDF (pages 1–3, bars 1–10): muted violins over unmuted winds and basses; tutti f chords on beat 2 in bars 2 and 4 as relief colour; in bars 7–10, when oboes and bassoons take the line at the octave, the cellos and basses change from quarters to continuous eighths and the violas to sixteenths. The harmony changes no faster; the motion comes from the bass. ## Findings

### 1. Bars 41–43: the pizzicato bass should move on every beat through the cor anglais phrase (applied in the scratch copy)

Problem. In 37–44 the double basses (with the harp) play on beats 1 and 3 only, with rests on 2 and 4, except bar 42, which alone has four quarters (B♭–E♭–A♭–D♭). The "Poco più mosso" therefore has the same bass rate as the Adagio, as c238 found, and bar 42 sounds like an isolated burst rather than part of a moving line.

Evidence. Adler's woodwind-string examples put rhythmic pizzicato basses under sustained or slurred upper parts: Schubert 8/i (Ex. VIII-1, PDF 235–236, basses in quarter-and-eighth figures under the Ob/Cl tune), Strauss (Ex. VIII-12, PDF 246–247), Berlioz (Ex. VIII-37, PDF 266), Mozart 38 (Ex. VIII-42, PDF 271–272). The two masters both add the motion at the second phrase, not the first: Dvořák 9/ii keeps the basses silent in 46–53 and starts pizz. eighths at 54; Mozart 41/ii starts the bass eighths at bar 7. Adler's rule that a repeated orchestration must change (PDF 255) also argues for 41–44 differing from 37–40 in the bass, not only in the melody instrument.

I tested two versions in /tmp/critic-239/work:
- (a) quarters in 37–39, 41 and 43 (`des,4 aes,,4 des,4 aes,,4 | bes,,4 des,4 ges,,4 bes,,4 | es,4 bes,,4 c,2 | ...`): clean build, 0 parallels. It makes 37–40 busy under the flute's first statement, which already has the viola triplets for motion, and it puts arpeggiated 6/4s on weak beats (38 b2, 41 b2).
- (b) quarters in 41 and 43 only, joining bar 42 (applied). 37–40 keep the plain halves with the harp, as Dvořák's first eight bars keep the bass quiet; the motion grows from 41 with the cor anglais and the pizz. violas, and stops at the half cadence 44. A first try with D♭2 on 41 b2 gave an analyser octave F–D♭ with the viola triplets' top note; the version below has none.

Fix (bSec.ily, `bSecCb`, second line):

```
  f,4 f,4 ges,4 ges,,4 | bes,,4 es,4 aes,,4 \once \stemUp des,4 | ges,,4 bes,,4 aes,,4 aes,,4 | aes,,4 r4 r2 |
```

(was `f,4 r4 ges,4 r4 | ... | ges,,4 r4 aes,,4 r4 | ...`). Chord tones only: 41 I6 repeated, IV with its root dropped an octave into 42's B♭1; 43 IV–IV6–V (A♭ held through the cadential 6/4 and V7). The harp LH is unchanged, so the harp and basses sound beats 1 and 3 together and the basses alone add beats 2 and 4 at p. If the conductor finds the added attacks too present, mark the basses pp at 41 (Dvořák's eighths are pp). Bar 40 (vii°7/vi, c233) and the metronome mark (c238) are not touched.

Result: 0 build warnings, 8 pages, 0 outer-voice parallels in analysis.txt, parts book 30 pages with every part except the harp on one page (double-bass part checked by eye).

### 2. Comparison with Mozart 41/ii (no change)

Mozart's slow movement and this one share the muted violins over unmuted winds and basses (Mozart bar 1; here 15–30), the three-statement reprise logic and the woodwind relief colour (Mozart bars 2, 4, 7; here 7–14, 31–36). Where Mozart is plainly ahead is the rhythmic life of the accompaniment in the continuation of a phrase (bars 7–10: bass eighths, viola sixteenths); finding 1 is the place where this score most lacked it. Elsewhere the score already does what Mozart does: the reprise is rescored rather than repeated (71: violin octaves, viola eighths; Adler PDF 238, principle 4), and the woodwinds double strings in octaves, not unisons, when the line rises (75–79; Adler PDF 265–266). A conductor reading 37–44 in v239 would see a correct but static accompaniment under a pretty tune; with the change the B section's second phrase moves the way Dvořák's and Mozart's second phrases move. I found nothing in 1–36, 45–92 that a conductor would take for a student's work.

### 4. Pedal suspended before each anacrusis (no change)

Adler PDF 245 (Strauss, Fledermaus m. 83): the pedal is lifted so the pickup to the second statement is heard. The score thins the accompaniment before most restatements: 36→37 (the woodwind choir holds, the C.a. pickup passes to Fl 1), 44 b4 (violas stop after b2, basses after b1; the Ob 1/Vc pickup is alone over the flute's last note), 70 b3 (horn and timpani have stopped; only the basses' E♭ pedal remains under the violin pickup). At 22 b3 and 52 b4 the bass of the dominant continues under the violins' pickup, but in both places the pickup lies two or more octaves above it, the violas rest (22) or hold one note (52), and the pedal leads into the arrival. Strauss lifts the pedal because his tune and pedal share a register; here they do not. No change.

### 5. Like-instrument unisons only in tutti (no change)

The score's unisons are Fl 1·2 at 61–62 (ff, protected c021), Hn 1·2 a2 at 78 b3–82 (ff counter-melody with Vc and Bsn 1) and Ob 1 + Cl 1 on B♭5 at 79–81 (Ex. ### 6. Register held back for the climax (no change)

Adler PDF 237–238: Ex. VIII-4/5 are rejected because their spread anticipates the register expansion Schubert saves for the cadence. The score keeps theme B under G♭5 at 37–44, the B section's ceiling at A♭6/A6 (58, 61–63) and B♭6 for 76 and 79 only (c110). This is the right order.
