## Findings

None of these needs a score change. Every principle in the reading that applies to a symphonic slow movement is met. I rank the findings by how close each comes to a weakness and say why I would not change the text.

**1. Introduction and coda, bars 1-6 and 83-92: proportion against Adler's two models (PDF 263-264).** Adler's point is that a woodwind introduction makes the solo colour fresh after the previous movement. Here the first six bars do this job: the strings hold movement I's chord and fade (1-3), the winds take over with no strings from bar 4 or 5, and Hn 1 enters at 6-7 in a colour not heard since bar 1's horns 3·4. The Canzonetta gives its introduction 12 of 119 bars (10 percent) and brings it back in the same instruments as the frame at 96-103. This score's introduction is 6 percent and its frame is the coda's close (89-92: Vln I C5 sul D held over A♭, the sound of bar 1). The shorter introduction is right here, because the link continues movement I's last chord and does not state a new idea. The coda (11 percent, about 40 s plus the fermata) is shorter than the Canzonetta's 20 percent, but the Canzonetta's last 16 bars are a modulating link to its finale. Here the scherzo's opening does that work (Hn 1 G♯4 = the last A♭). No change. Nothing at the ending, in the exported themes or in the quotations is affected.

**2. Reductions after each tutti, bars 30-31, 62-67 and 82-83 (PDF 267, Ex. XVII-10 at C; PDF 264).** Mozart introduces the flute with a tutti and then cuts the orchestra back as soon as the soft solo enters, with a sustained tonic in horns and low strings. The movement does this three times, each time differently. At 30-31 the strings close p > pp and the bass clarinet enters over the bassoons' and contrabassoon's A♭ pedal. At 62-63 the ff tutti stops and Tbn 1 states the quotation over sf-p string tremolo. At 64-67 the strings fade and Hn 1 plays the motto's tail over the basses' E♭ and the timpani roll. At 82-83 the harp's harmonics play over the low A♭ alone. The Mozart rule about horns (PDF 264) holds as well: horns 3·4 play only in the introduction and at 87-90, after Hn 1's last solo, and no other horn sounds under Hn 1 at 7-14 or 67-70. The three soft interludes (1-6, 31-36, 65-70) are all six bars long and last 21-22 s each. That could make the form sound blocked out. It does not here, because each one has a different soloist (horn pickup, B.cl. and C.a., Hn 1), a different pedal (C → A♭, A♭, E♭) and a different job (link, modulation to D♭, retransition). c233 recorded this; I agree. No change.

**3. Entry preparation, bars 37 and 53 (PDF 268-272, Ex. XVII-11, XVII-12).** In Adler's two textural examples the background is set up before the solo: three bars of muted violins in Sibelius, two bars of weaving strings and timpani in Bartók. The movement prepares its entries this way at 7 (six bars), 67 (two bars) and 83 (the A♭ pedal is tied from 82). At 37, theme B and its whole accompaniment start on the same beat after the bare fifth of 35-36. This was settled by c233 (Dvořák 9/ii does the same at 46; the 35-36 fifth and the section proportions are protected), and I do not reopen it. At 53 the violins' pickup on 52 b4 comes out of the C.a.'s close and the timpani roll at 52, and the harp triplets start under the held F5. The background is therefore in place before the line moves on. Both entries follow the textural principle (a transparent pp background) if not the "bars ahead" timing. No change.

**4. The middle section's build and climax, bars 53-64 (PDF 267, Ex. XVII-10 at C, read in reverse; PDF 274).** The build follows Adler's rhythmic-contrast and registral guidelines while it thickens. At 53-56 the theme is in the violins with the harp triplets and the Cl 1·Hn 1 counter-line, and the contrast keeps three layers clear (Ex. XVII-15). At 57-60 Fl and Ob are added in octaves with viola sextuplets, the dynamic goes from mp to f, and the MIDI tempo goes from 60 to 64. Then come 61-62 ff and 63-64 (the quotation). The ff plateau is only two bars, but Adler's reduction rule explains why it works: the tutti introduces the trombone's quotation and then drops away on its first beat (63, sf-p), as Mozart's tutti hands over to the solo flute. A longer ff plateau would either cover the quotation or postpone it past 65 percent of the movement. The 61 peak is at 65 percent and the higher 79 peak (B♭6) at 84 percent. The reprise peak is thus the main one. No change.

**5. Dialogue as a formal marker, bars 15-22, 37-52 and 71-82 (PDF 257-258).** Adler says dialogue "aids in a clearer exposition of the form". Theme A changes colour at each of its three statements (Hn 1 with winds and harp; violins in unison with the Vc counter-melody; violins in octaves with Fl/Ob, viola eighths and harp). Theme B passes Fl 1 → C.a. → Ob 1 → violins → violins with Fl/Ob. At 41 the violas change to pizz. when the C.a. takes over, which is Beethoven's Romance device in Ex. XVII-1 (pizz. strings in the answering colour so the solo is not duplicated). The listener can follow the form by colour alone. No change.

**6. Pacing of the ending, bars 83-92 (PDF 267 item 1; PDF 273).** The coda's three solo ideas (harp head at 83, timpani mirror at 84-85, Cl 1's tail at 85-89) are each heard over a single held pedal. That is Mozart's "sparse accompaniment ... to give the performer freedom". The timpani join the two phrases exactly as in Ex. XVII-13. The rit. starts at 89, the bar where the last solo hands C5 to Vln I. The tempo therefore slows only after the last melodic event, as Adler's Bizet example later in the chapter (PDF 279-281) slows only on the phrase's last notes. No change.
