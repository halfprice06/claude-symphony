## What else I studied

**Mendelssohn, Symphony No. 3, iii.** No local score (the corpus has only Psalm 42), so I rely on published descriptions. The movement is the work's Adagio in A major (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._3_(Mendelssohn)), played without a break into the finale. Programme notes describe two unequal ideas: "a broad, flowing melody heard at the beginning in the violins" and a second idea of "somber, ominous chords from the wind instruments", a "pungently accented march in the minor" with dotted rhythms (https://research.allclassical.org/?p=9177, https://theclassicreview.com/beginners-guides/mendelssohn-symphony-no-3-scottish-a-beginners-guide/). Wikipedia describes the movement as "an apparent struggle between love and fate".

## Holistic judgement

A conductor who knows Mendelssohn's Adagio would accept this movement as the work of a professional in the same tradition. The two movements share the essential plan: a long violin cantilena as the main idea, a darker wind-and-brass element as its opposite, and a soft close that leads straight into what follows.

**Where it matches or exceeds the model:**
- The main theme gets three different settings: horn over low reeds (7), strings alone (23), and strings plus winds (71). Mendelssohn's song keeps essentially one colour. This is Adler's "each major thematic element differently" (PDF 238) and "alter something the third time" (PDF 255).
- Each section has its own accompaniment pattern from Adler's Mendelssohn list (PDF 158–159): louré sustain at 15–22, sustain plus arpeggio at 37–40, off-beat figure at 48–51, and sustain plus a new arpeggio at 71–78.
- The climaxes are built by adding octaves and colours, not by louder dynamics alone (PDF 137, 144), and released by thinning the scoring (PDF 154).

**Where it falls short of Mendelssohn:**
- Mendelssohn's opposite idea is a different kind of music: a dotted-rhythm march in the winds. Here the B section is a second song (theme B), and the "fate" element is only the six bars 59–64, ending in the trombone quotation. The design is settled and protected (key plan, quotations, c124 rule for 37–60), so I record this and propose nothing.

**Against v149:** nothing got worse. The changes are:
- the written-out run at 78 (c149);
- the contrabassoon pedal at 33–36 (c149);
- the cor anglais F♭→E♭ in the coda (c150);
- the G♭ anticipation at 58 (c151): checked, the G♭ is the seventh of A♭7 on 58 b3;
- the French sixth at 21 (c152): checked, F♭–A♭–B♭–D resolving to V;
- the allargando moved to 64 (c153);
- the bass trombone sf>p at 63 (c154);
- engraving and wording (c155–c158).

## Findings

None of these needs a change to the score. I list them so later critics do not raise them again without new reasons.

### 1. 37–64: the contrasting idea is a second song, not a contrasting character (design; no change)
- **Problem.** In the Mendelssohn, the minor-key wind march sets a new rhythm and a new choir against the violin song. Here the D♭ section starts as another cantabile tune: flute, then cor anglais, then violins. A sharply different character appears only at 59–64.
- **Evidence.** Adler PDF 236–238 (Schubert 8): each thematic element is given its own scoring to clarify the form. The B section does have its own colours: flute and cor anglais over pizzicato and harp, after horn and strings in A. So the scoring follows the reading. The difference from Mendelssohn is one of compositional character, not orchestration.
- **Why no change.** The key plan, the quotations at 47–53 and 63–64, theme B's line and the c124 rule for 37–60 are all protected. A rewrite would undo about forty cycles of settled work. This is recorded as the main design difference from this master.

### 2. 62 b3–4: Vln I divided (A♭6 held over F♭6) at the end of the climax (no change)
- **Problem.** Adler PDF 152 (Tchaikovsky, Serenade): "divisi would weaken the first violin line". c059 removed a two-bar Vln I divisi at 61–62 for this reason. A two-beat divisi has since returned at 62 b3 (c091).
- **Evidence from the dump.** At 62 b3 the line note F♭6 is also played at pitch by flute 2 (E6). Vln II, oboe 2, bassoon 1 and the cellos double it at E5/E4. The held A♭6 is doubled by flute 1. The line is therefore not carried by half the firsts alone, and the divisi lasts two beats before the "non div." tremolo at 63.
- **Why no change.** Adler's concern, a weakened line, does not arise here. Keep it as c091 left it.

### 3. 77 b1–2: the leading tone G is sounded in five octaves (no change)
- **Problem.** On V of A♭ at 77, G sounds in flute 1 and Vln I (G6), Vln II and clarinet 1 (G5), clarinet 2 (G4) and the violas (G3). The root E♭ sounds in the bass and bassoon 2, and the fifth B♭ only in the counter-melody (Bsn 1, Hn 2, Vc). Clarinet 2's G4 is a held harmonic doubling, not part of the melody, and it stops without resolving.
- **Evidence.** Adler PDF 255–257: "the melody must come through … and not be obscured by the harmony". The melody does come through: four of the six Gs are the melody itself in octaves (Rossini's 8-foot/4-foot principle, PDF 250–251). The viola G3 is one note of an arpeggio. Clarinet 2 at mp< under a p–f crescendo is the only extra doubling.
- **Why no change.** The cadence 77–82 and the clarinets' entry at 77 are protected (c079, c124, c144). If clarinet 2 moved to B♭4, it would double the counter-melody's B♭3, which c127 removed elsewhere for the same reason. The cure would be worse than the fault.

### 4. 41 b1–2: the pizzicato violas touch the cor anglais' A♭3 and D♭4 (follows the reading; no change)
- **Problem.** The viola arpeggio starts on the solo's first two pitches in the same octave.
- **Evidence.** In Bizet's Habanera (Ex. V-36, PDF 160–161), the pizzicato upper strings "support the singer both melodically and harmonically", and the singer still "can execute her part without orchestral interference". A plucked note under a held reed note does not mask the reed.
- **Why no change.** It follows the reading, and the c124 rule protects the accompaniment.
