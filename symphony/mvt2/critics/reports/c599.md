### 3. Against Beethoven 5/ii: the reprise varies the scoring, not the line (no change)
Beethoven returns to his A♭ theme several times and changes the melody's own surface each time (faster figuration). Here theme A at 71-78 keeps its pitches and rhythm (an octave up) and gets its variety from the viola eighths, the cello 9-8 and the run into B♭6 at 78-79. Both are master methods. Rimsky PDF 130 and c413 support growth by scoring, and the theme is exported and protected (c547, A′ texture plan). No change is proposed. I raise it only so the record shows the comparison was made.

### 4. Scale of the movement (observation for Dan, no change)
At 345 s the movement is shorter than every model slow movement (Beethoven 5/ii runs about 7-11 minutes, Dvořák 9/ii about 12). A conductor would accept it as a compact Adagio between a large first movement and a scherzo. It would not be heard as unfinished, because the proportions hold (climax at 65 percent, return and coda 24 percent; c413, c423, c453). Making it longer would reopen protected sections. Record only.

## Holistic verdict

Against Beethoven 5/ii the movement holds on the points that matter most. It opens softly in one colour. Its common-tone moves between A♭ and C work the way Beethoven's own A♭/C shifts do: here C-A♭ at 1-2, and A♭ to E via F♭ to the scherzo. It saves loud brass for one event. It ends with a coda that recalls rather than develops. Beethoven gets his contrast from sudden ff breaks. This score uses graded builds instead (Rimsky PDF 130), with one sf break at 63. That suits a neo-Romantic Adagio. A conductor would believe a professional master of this style wrote it. Theme A is the movement's memorable idea; theme B serves well as a middle-section tune (c401). Nothing got worse between v589 and v599 apart from finding 1, and v599 is fit to release.
