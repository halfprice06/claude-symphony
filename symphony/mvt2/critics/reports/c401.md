## Findings

No finding needs a score change. Every principle in this chapter that applies to the movement is followed, and the places where the score looks as if it departs from the book (nos. 1, 2 and 3 below) have reasons that the book itself gives. I list them so that the next melody critic does not have to check them again.

**1. Theme A in the horn at 7-14 and the motto tail at 67-70: chromatic tail on brass. Recorded, no change.**
Rimsky PDF 73 says chromatic melody suits the brass less and that the brass "lack the capacity to express passion or geniality". The tail E♭-D-D♭-C (8, 68) is chromatic. PDF 72 gives the exception: "the genial and poetic tone of the horn in piano passages affords greater scope in the choice of melodies". Ex. 76 (PDF 247) shows the same thing: horns pp, Molto andante, with single woodwind notes above and no strings, which is how 7-14 is scored (bassoons, bass clarinet and harp under the horn). The tail is four semitones at p inside a diatonic arpeggio. That is not the sustained chromatic writing of the "modern Italian realists" that PDF 73 warns against. On memorability, the horn statement carries the tune well: the rising arpeggio to a held E♭ and the falling tail are the two features a listener keeps, as with the opening of Tchaikovsky 5/ii. This is protected (c021) and should stay.

**2. 41-44: the melody is the lower voice of the Fl/C.a. pair. Recorded, no change.**
PDF 70 assumes the upper part of a pair in thirds or sixths is the tune. Here the C.a. has theme B and flute 1 has a descant a tenth or sixth above, marked one level softer (c101). The order is still the natural one (flute above a double reed, PDF 65, 70), and sixths between different colours are what PDF 70 recommends. Rimsky's own Ex. 57 (*Snegourotchka*, PDF 229, flute over cor anglais, "p dolce e legato assai") is the model, in octaves rather than sixths. Leave it.

**3. 61-62: theme B's augmented head in Fl 2, Vln II and Vc under held ff chords. Recorded, no change.**
PDF 76 warns that it is "difficult to achieve perfect balance" when a melody is spread over three or four octaves of mixed groups. At 61-62 the head A♭-D♭-F-F♭ is in the middle register, in three octaves (Fl 2 + Vln II above Vc), and Fl 1 and Vln I hold A♭6 over it. Each octave has a string and a wind (no. 18, Ex. 87 *Kashchei*, PDF 261), the head moves in half notes while everything else holds (c037's principle), and the trumpets are tacet. This has been balanced across c021, c161 and c321. A further check would need someone to hear it, and nobody in this loop can. No change.

**4. Memorability overall. No change.**
Theme A works as a tune. Its range is a ninth (A♭3-B♭4 in the horn), it has one held peak note in each half (E♭ at 7-8, F at 11-12), and its tail is stepwise. Its second phrase rises higher than the first (F above E♭) before the fall, which is the shape of the Dvořák Largo tune and of Nimrod. The tune comes back four times, each in a new colour (horn; violins with cello counter-melody; violins in octaves with the B♭6 peak; the harp head in the coda). Rimsky PDF 64 ties the expressive strength of a melody to the choice of instrument, and each of those colours fits its register. Theme B is less distinctive. It is mostly stepwise around F-G♭-E♭-D♭, and its rhythm (2 8 8 4) is close to theme A's dotted figure. It is a contrasting middle-section tune, though, not a second main theme, and in Dvořák 9/ii the middle-section melody is also the less memorable one. The peak at 58 (A♭6) and the combination of the themes at 53-56 and 79-81 give it enough profile. I do not recommend changing either theme so late in the series. Theme rhythms are protected (cycle 171 do-not-touch: theme A's tied syncopations, theme B's two head placements).
