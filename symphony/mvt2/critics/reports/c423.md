## Findings

None of these needs a change in the score. They are ranked by how useful the record is to the composer and to later critics.

### 1. Bar 43 b1–b3: two 6/4s in a row. The record's reason is wrong; the book gives a better one (no change)

**Bars:** 43 b1–b3.

**Problem raised.** Unit 19 allows two consecutive 6/4s only in the incomplete-neighbour case (19 §8, PDF 314, Ex. 19-17b). Here, 43 b2 (B♭1 under E♭–G♭–B♭) is followed directly by the cadential 6/4 at b3. c283 kept it because the violas' D♭4 on b1.67 makes the b2 chord "heard as ii4/3".

**Evidence.** The v423 MIDI shows that the D♭4 is a pizzicato triplet eighth that stops on the beat (violas 1.67–2.00). On b2 itself the sound is basses B♭1, cellos B♭2 (held from b1), violas B♭3, cor anglais E♭4 (held from b1) and flute G♭5 (held from b1). That is a pure E♭-minor 6/4, so c283's reason does not hold. The passage is still correct, for two reasons from the reading:
- From b1 to b2 only the bass moves, G♭1 to B♭1, inside the subdominant (G♭–B♭–D♭ with the cor anglais's E♭ as added 6th). Every upper voice is held. A&S say that a 6/4 whose upper voices do not move has "little vertical identity and hardly count[s] as a chord" (19 §5, PDF 311, Ex. 19-12). It is also an arpeggio 6/4 that depends on the 5/3 before it (19 §13–14, PDF 319).
- The pattern is the one in Ex. 19-28 (19 §16, PDF 321): a 6/4 moving inside an extended IV, then the cadential V 6/4–5/3. Mozart puts a II6 between them. Here the bass goes straight from B♭ to the cadential A♭.

So the b2 6/4 is consonant and the b3 6/4 is dissonant. That is the "double nature" of 19 §1 (PDF 304), not a doubled cadential gesture.

**Variant tested on paper and rejected.** Violas `\tuplet 3/2 4 { bes8 des' bes }` on b2 (in place of `bes8 ges bes`) would make b2 a real ii4/3, with the 7th D♭ held over as the 4th of the cadential 6/4. But it would pluck D♭4 one beat before the cor anglais attacks D♭4 at b3, in the same octave. That is the clash c057 and c267 removed. Keep the bar as it is. For HISTORY: "43 b2 6/4: arpeggio/held-voice 6/4 inside IV (A&S 19 §5 Ex. 19-12, §16 Ex. 19-28), not ii4/3; replaces c283's reason."

### 2. The 6/4 inventory is unchanged since c362; c422's change follows Unit 19 (record, no change)

A scan of v423's score.midi finds these 6/4s and no others: 5 (neighbouring, iv over A♭), 21 b2 (passing, with voice exchange), 38 b4.5 (arpeggio, in the gap of the pizzicato bass), 43 b2 (Finding 1), 43 b3 (accented cadential), 68 b3 (passing over the dominant pedal) and 74 b3 (one eighth inside V4/3). This is c362's list.

c422 changed the violas at 43 b3 to A♭3–E♭3–A♭3, so the cadential 6/4 now doubles its bass on the beat: A♭1 in the basses and harp, A♭2 in the cellos and harp, A♭3 in the violas. A&S say the accented 6/4 follows the doubling of the cadential type (19 §3, PDF 306). The 6th (Fl F5) falls by step to E♭5. The single 4th (C.a. D♭4) leaps away, and the cellos' C3 takes the resolution in another voice and octave (19 §9, PDF 315, Ex. 19-19). Because the 4th is not doubled, the rule of PDF 316 about doubled 4ths does not arise. The B♭–A♭ octave between violas and cellos into b3 doubles the bass's own step. That is acceptable.

### 4. The build 53–61 holds back the "full voicing" of its opening chord on purpose (record, no change)

A&S say the chain's opening chord is marked by "full voicing" and its cadential goal by low register (16 §1, PDF 250). At 53 the I of B3 has only the cellos' D♭3 as bass. The basses rest from 53 to 56 (c164), and the low register enters at 57 (Cb F2, mf). The movement's lowest and loudest bass then comes at the goal D♭1 at 61. Under 16 §8 (PDF 257), 53–60 is one long passing motion, iii–IV–♯iv°7–V into I, with subordinate chords between the main ones. Only 61 is the main chord, so taking weight from 53 and giving it to 61 is right. The 58 fifth chain (B♭–E♭–A♭–D♭–G♭) is a chain inside the passing motion, as in Ex. 16-1, where the inner chords "do not function on an equal level of importance".

### 5. 77–81: ♭VI as upper neighbour to V, with the return held back (record, no change)

16 §10 (PDF 258) gives VI as upper neighbour to V as the main long-span bass-neighbour idiom. At 62–65 the score uses it directly: F♭/E, A minor between, then E to E♭. At 77–78 V7 goes to ♭VI (F♭, the deceptive move). The neighbour does not go straight back to V. It passes through E♭–G–C (78 b3, a passing 6/3 that supports the falling line, 16 §7) and II6/5 (79–80) before V7 returns at 81. This applies the interpolation principle of 19 §11 (PDF 317, Ex. 19-23) to V itself: the expected V is delayed by a neighbour chord and a subdominant, and the delay creates the second peak. In the form this keeps A′ from repeating the B section's exit (♭VI to A minor to V), and the two uses of the same neighbour are told apart. Protected; no change.

### 6. Points checked and not raised

- 21 b2: the passing 6/4 (Ex. 19-11, PDF 311) leads into a French sixth on F♭ instead of a diatonic IV6. It keeps the voice exchange between the outer voices. This chromatic variant is within the book's type.
- 31–36: no V7 of D♭ before 37 (c020). This is 16 §4 exactly: the A♭ is heard first as I and afterwards as the upper 5th of D♭. An A♭7 pivot would turn the upper-5th relation into a plain V–I.
- 49: G major over B (the Neapolitan of F♯ minor) as a 6/3 between F♯ minor and C♯7. This is a short-span 6/3, as 16 §10 (PDF 258) prefers.
- 12–14, 28–30, 80–82: no cadential 6/4. c283's do-not-re-raise is kept.
- Minor v (16 §11–14) does not appear, and no passage needs it. Each minor-key area is reached through its own major V.
