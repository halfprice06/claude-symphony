## What else I studied

I read BRIEF.md, LENSES.md, movement-2-plan.md and HISTORY.md, with every entry on the harp, theme A and theme B. I also read the two earlier reports on this reading with this lens (c241, c361) and the harp-playability report c266, so that I do not repeat closed points. Those are: the harp chord at 14 (fixed c361), harp dynamics at 53–55 and 77 (c241, c244), the pickup at pitch at 82 b3 (c116), the A♭1 tie into 83 (c214), the score-only mid-movement pedal marks (c075, MOLA PDF 5), and theme B's narrow first phrase (c131, c141, c361). I rendered all eight score pages of v381 and both pages of the harp part, and read the harp, theme and counter-line source in aOne.ily, bSec.ily, aTwo.ily and coda.ily. The reference corpus has no work with harp. Of the lens's model composers, Dvořák (9/ii), Brahms, Tchaikovsky (5/ii, 6/iv) and Rachmaninoff (2/iii) write their great slow-movement tunes without harp. Elgar (Symphony No. 1/iii) is the one who uses harps in the Adagio, as colour under the strings. A light harp part that rarely carries the tune is therefore normal for this style.

On the lens question: theme A is the tune a listener will remember. It has a clear head (rising A♭–C–E♭ to a held note, then the chromatic E♭–D–D♭–C), an eight-bar period with its peak on the B♭ appoggiatura (12, 28, 76), and three full statements in three colours (horn 7, violins 23, violins and winds 71 with the B♭6 climax at 79). After that the coda takes it apart: harp head 82–83, timpani mirror 84–85, clarinet tail 85–89. Dvořák's Largo coda handles its tune the same way. Theme B is more ornamental (stepwise lower-neighbour turns), and its second phrase with the rising A♭–D♭–F is the part that sticks; that phrase is what the climax at 61 augments. The reading supports the harp's role in this design and does not call for a change in either theme.

## Findings

All five are recorded without a score change. I found no violation of the reading that earlier critics have not already fixed or ruled on.

### 1. Bars 82–83: the harp's melodic moment is short but correct (no change)

**What I checked.** The cyclic rule asks for a melodic moment for the harp. The harp plays theme A's pickup E♭4–F4 at pitch (82 b3, pp), then the head A♭4–C5–E♭5 as LH octave harmonics (83, p dolce). That is five notes, and the first three bars of the tune are spread over harp, timpani and clarinet.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 117 names the harp as a melodic instrument. Ex. IV-10 (Ravel) gives harp 1 a full melodic phrase in octaves, with harp 2 accompanying. Every condition for harmonics on PDF 116 holds here: written range C3–A5, three notes within a fifth in one hand, "o" notation with "sound 8va", and an accompaniment of only the ppp cello and bass A♭ pedal. The phrase could be longer. The tail E♭–D–D♭–C would need D♮ and D♭ together, which needs the enharmonic setting E♭–D♮–C♯–B♯ that PDF 113 describes (C♯ for D♭, B♯ for C). That works for harmonics, but it would take the tail away from clarinet 1 at 85–89, which is protected (harp–timpani–clarinet chain), and c354 recorded that the scherzo gives the harp its longer melody. No change.

### 2. Bars 53–56: the triplet figure is narrower than Adler's arpeggio models (no change)

**What I checked.** The RH triplets stay inside A♭3–A♭4, which is the octave of the horn 1/clarinet 1 counter-line built from theme A's head (A♭3–B♭4). This is the only place where both themes sound together.

**Evidence.** Ex. IV-8 (Debussy, PDF 116) and Ex. IV-7 (Brahms, PDF 115) spread their arpeggios over two octaves or more, and PDF 114 calls wide-spread chords the most effective. A wider figure would cross the counter-line or the violins' theme B (F5 at 53), or drop into the "sombre, dark" low register (PDF 113) on top of the cellos. c137 cleared every attack on a held counter-line note, c241 set the harp one level under the counter-line (pp, then p at 55), and c359 kept the figure under Adler's exceptions. The narrow figure is the right choice for the melody. No change.

### 3. Bars 84–92: the harp does not close the movement (no change)

**What I checked.** After the harmonics at 83 the harp is silent to the end. In Ex. IV-7 (Brahms, PDF 115) the harp's arpeggios close the movement on the final chord.

**Evidence.** c050 removed a harp chord at 91 because a new attack inside the closing diminuendo repeated movement I's last scoring (Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 127). The plagal close 87–92 with C5 on top is on the standing list. The harp's last sound is theme A's head, and that serves memorability better than a chord. No change.

### 4. Bars 7–14 and 75–78: close RH dyads instead of wide three-note chords (no change)

PDF 114 says wide three-note chords per hand are the most effective harp chords. The RH uses close dyads under the horn's theme (7–13) and outside the counter-melody (75–78). At 7–14 the wide spacing comes from the LH octaves A♭1–E♭2. Wider RH chords would strike the solo's pitches or the counter-melody's (c057, c127, c241), and Rimsky-Korsakov PDF 139 says harmony in a melody's own register covers it. The melody rule outranks the idiom rule here.

### 5. Bars 37–44: harp doubles the pizzicato bass under the flute's theme B (no change)

Ex. IV-4 (Mozart, PDF 114) shows flute and harp in dialogue. Here the harp gives solid low octaves with the pizzicato (PDF 114–115: solid unless a wavy line is written), three octaves under the flute. The tune keeps its register to itself, and the pedal changes at 39 b4 and 41 b4 give one beat each (PDF 113). A harp answer between the flute's phrases at 40 and 44 would compete with the cor anglais' entry at 41 (c051, c139). No change.
