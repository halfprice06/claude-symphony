## Findings

### 1. The ff tutti at 61-62 matches the appendix's loud chords (recorded, no change)
- **Bars:** 61, 62 b1, 62 b3.
- **Evidence:** At 61 the sounding chord is C♯1 C♯2 C♯3 F3 A♭3 C♯4 F4 A♭4 C♯5 F5 A♭5 C♯6 A♭6. The bottom two octaves hold the root alone (Cbsn, Cb, Tba), which is what chords 1-12 show (PDF 505-506). Each group is complete by itself: woodwinds (B.cl. C♯3 up to Fl A♭6), brass (Tba C♯2, B.tbn C♯3, Tbn 2 F3, Hn C♯4-F4-A♭4) and strings (Cb C♯2, Vc A♭3, Vla C♯4/F4, Vln II A♭4, Vln I C♯6). Brass and timpani are one degree under the ff of winds and strings, as in chords 2 and 9. Strings use a double stop, divisi and tremolo, as in chords 3, 4, 11 and 12. At 62 b3 the F♭ chord keeps the root alone in E1/E2.
- **Not reopened:** the F4 (Bsn 1, Vc) against the horns' G♭4 at 62 b1 is the major seventh of G♭maj7, held over from 61 and resolving down; c197 rejected a change because it would remove c014's colour. The appendix's chords are consonant triads and give no reason to reopen it. The leading tone in four octaves at 62 b3 was recorded in c182.

### 2. The A′ peak at 79-81 is a "partial tutti" in Rimsky's sense (recorded, no change)
- **Bars:** 79-81.
- **Evidence:** PDF 120 defines a partial tutti as one where only part of the brass plays and says flutes or brass may be left out according to register and context. At 79 the horns are the only brass, marked f under the ff of woodwinds and strings (principle 5). The melody B♭ is in four octaves (Vla B♭4, Vln II/Ob 1/Cl 1 B♭5, Vln I/Fl 1 B♭6), the root D♭ in four (C♯1-C♯4), and the third and fifth fill the middle (A♭3, F4, F5, F6). This is a melodic tutti, the type Rimsky gives in Ex. 209 (c404), not a block chord like the appendix's, so principles 1 and 7 apply only to the accompaniment, and it follows them. The choice of no trombones at 79 was recorded in c024 and c209.

### 3. The soft chords at 3-6, 31-36 and 87-92 follow chords 13-16 (recorded, no change)
- **Bars:** 3-6, 31-36, 87-92.
- **Evidence:** Chords 13 (*Mlada*, pp), 14 (*Tsar's Bride*, pp), 15 (*Kitezh*, f dim.) and 16 (*Christmas Night*, pp) are woodwind and horn chords in close position, one note per horn, with strings silent or reduced to a bass pedal (PDF 506-507). Bars 3-6 have Cl 2, B.cl. and Hn 3·4 pp with the strings out (A♭2 E♭3/F♭3 A♭3 C4/D♭4). 31-36 is the woodwind choir over the bassoon and contrabassoon A♭ pedal. At 87-90 the muted horns hold F3-A♭3 then E♭3-A♭3, one note each, with C.a. and Cl 1. Muted strings are added, a colour Rimsky's operatic chords do not use but Dvořák 9/ii does at its close (c459, c489). PDF 120 says soft tutti is rare; the score uses it only at the joins and the end, which agrees.

### 4. The sf attack at 63 follows chord 8 (recorded, no change)
- **Bars:** 63.
- **Evidence:** Chord 8 (*Ivan the Terrible*, PDF 506) shows strings striking a short sff and stopping while the winds hold. At 63 the bass trombone holds sf> under the Tbn 1 quotation, the basses pluck sf and stop, and the upper strings play a tremolo sf> that clears the texture for the quotation. The principle is the same: a short loud attack, then one held line. Protected (sf>p tremolo and Cb pizz. sf at 63, c424).

### 5. Comparison with Elgar 2/ii: what a conductor would accept, and where the distance remains (recorded, no change)
- **Shape.** Elgar's Larghetto opens with quiet sustained string chords before the march theme, brings the main theme back with an oboe countersubject laid over it, and builds terraced climaxes to a final peroration before a quiet close. This movement does the same three things: a soft introduction (1-6, the common-tone link), a reprise with a new counter-line (71-78, cellos and Hn 2 under the violins' theme A, c007 and c020), and two climaxes, the second higher and later (61 at ff on D♭6, then 79 at ff on B♭6), before a pp close. The proportions (A 36%, B 34%, A′ 19%, coda 11%; c523) fit the funeral-march plan as well as the Dvořák model.
- **Scoring.** At his peaks Elgar uses full brass, trumpets included. This movement keeps trumpets tacet and uses trombones only at 60-64. That is a recorded cyclic decision (c060, c454): the brass is saved for the finale. A conductor would read it as restraint in a slow movement inside a four-movement plan, not as a lack of means.
- **Where the distance is.** Elgar's march theme and his oboe countersubject are memorable on their own. Within this lens and this reading I find no orchestration or spacing fault that stands between this score and Elgar's craft. Any remaining distance lies in the themes themselves (lens 1), which are protected and which 528 cycles have shaped; I do not have a better tune to propose and do not invent a change.
