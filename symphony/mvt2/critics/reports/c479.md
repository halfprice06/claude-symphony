## Comparison with Mahler 1/iii

Points where the score stands up to Mahler's movement:

- **Start and end.** Mahler builds his opening layer by layer over a quiet timpani ostinato, starting with one low, unexpected soloist (muted double bass, bar 3), then muted cellos (11), then four muted horns with harp (29). Bars 1-14 here do the same in their own terms: the link's held C5, timpani ppp, horns 3-4, then horn 1 alone with bass clarinet, bassoons and harp, and no strings. The coda reverses the process, again with muted horns 3-4 and harp. That is the same use of the orchestra as Casanelles' Fig. 6.1 (principle 3).
- **Self-quotation in its own colour.** Mahler's trio quotes his own song in a separate sound world: muted strings, harp triplets, a different key. Here the B section has harp and viola triplets (37-40, 53-56), and the quotation of movement I (47-50) gets the cor anglais, p over pp strings, F sharp minor and a poco sostenuto. The quotation is set apart the way Mahler sets apart his song: by colour, key and tempo, not by volume.
- **Dynamics as results.** Mahler writes his dynamics for the balance he wants, so his solos are often marked above their accompaniment. This score does the same everywhere I checked (table row 2).

Points where it falls short: Mahler's movement contains irony and abrupt cuts (the "Mit Parodie" band music). This movement has none, and the plan does not ask for it. A Dvořák-model Adagio does not need it, and I do not recommend adding it. A conductor would accept this score as the work of a skilled late-Romantic composer. What separates it from Mahler is character, not craft, so I make no change on that ground.

## Findings

### 1. Bar 74 b1 (reprise only): the cellos' 9-8 suspension sounds a minor second under a held D flat4. This got worse since v469. Applied in scratch.

**Problem.** c469 (v470) held the cellos' C4 from 73 b3 into 74 as a 9-8 over the V4/3's bass B flat1. At 74 b1, clarinet 2 holds D flat4 for two beats and the violas attack D flat4 on the same eighth. So the suspension C4 sits a semitone below the chord's 7th, in the same octave and with nothing between them, for a whole beat (about 1.15 s at crotchet 52). The sonority is B flat1, B flat2, C4, D flat4 (twice), G4, G5. It falls on the theme's half cadence, where the melody holds G and the inner parts are the most exposed sound. In v469 bar 74 b1 had no such clash (the cellos played B flat3).

**Evidence.** Casanelles PDF 277-278: the orchestrator's real problem is the ensemble timbre, the sound that results when instruments combine, including the way frequencies reinforce or cancel each other. A cello on the D string and a low clarinet a minor second apart in the same register beat against each other. That is not a suspension's "accent" (A&S PDF 348); it is a cluster. c469's report (line 57) calls the D flat4 "the tenth above" the 9-8 and cites Ex. 21-5. My answer to that reason: in Ex. 21-5 the tenth is the consonant third of a triad. Here D flat is the dissonant 7th of V, and it lies directly above the suspension. A&S's ninth chords (Unit 27, PDF 469-470, the reason c472 gave for moving Vln II at 39) put the 9th above the 7th and keep the note of resolution out of an inner voice. At 74 b1 neither holds.

**Fix (keeps c469's suspension, c073's complete V4/3 and c060's D flat-C in the violas).** Clarinet 2 ties its E flat4 (the 7th of vi7 at 73 b3) into 74 as a common tone and takes D flat4 on b2, as the cellos resolve to B flat3. This is the interlocking-sevenths motion c442 used at 72 (A&S PDF 400, Ex. 24-3). The violas' first eighth at 74 becomes E flat3. That puts it below the suspension and avoids A flat3-G3, which would make octaves with Vln II's A flat4-G4. 74 b1 is now an E flat triad over B flat with C suspended (B flat1, B flat2, E flat3, C4, E flat4, G4, G5). D flat4 enters at b2 and the violas still have it at b3.5, resolving to C4 at 75.
