# Critic 371: v371, lens 1 (melody and memorability)

Assigned reading: Sebesky, *The Contemporary Arranger*, ch. 5 "Voices" (PDF 197-203, printed pp. 188-194), third series. This is the same chapter c231 read. I read all seven text pages in full and rendered all seven pages at 110 dpi (Ex. 259-268) and read the images. The OCR of the examples is unusable; the notes below come from the images. The chapter is about choral and commercial vocal-group writing. I apply it to instrumental melody through four questions: does each line move as a singer's line would, does it stay in one voice's normal range, does it breathe where a singer would, and how are the lines doubled and held?

### 1. Both themes fit one voice's normal range; the notes outside it are kept for the peaks (recorded, no change)

Ex. 260 (PDF 198) gives each voice a normal span of about a thirteenth and brackets the notes beyond it. Measured at concert pitch:

- Theme A, E♭ pickup to the B♭ appoggiatura: a twelfth in every statement (Hn 1 E♭3-B♭4 at 6-14; violins E♭4-B♭5 at 22-30).
- Theme B: first phrase B♭4-G♭5 (Fl 1, 37-40); second phrase A♭3-B♭4 (C.a., 41-44); together an octave and a step.
- The notes that lie outside a theme's sung compass are the climaxes: A♭6 at 58 and 61, A6 at 63, B♭6 at 79. This matches the reading's treatment of the bracketed notes as exceptions.

So a listener can hum either theme in one voice, as with Dvořák's twelfth-wide tune. No change.

### 2. Breaths fall where a singer's would, before the pickups (recorded, no change)

The reading prints a breath at each phrase end (Ex. 259, PDF 197) and puts it before the pickup (Ex. 261, PDF 199; Ex. 267, PDF 203). Every wind melody follows this: Hn 1 breathes at 10 before `es8( f8`; B.cl. after `c2 bes,4)` at 34; Fl 1 at 40 before its counter-line; Fl 1 and Ob 1 at the end of 58 and 60; Fl 1 at 78 b2 before the triplet run, which is Ex. 267's shape. The longest unbroken wind phrase I timed is Fl 1's A♭6-A6 at 61-63 b1: nine beats at ♩=54, about 10 s at ff, which is playable. The one breath that splits a motive, Cl 1 after D5 at 86, is protected (c226) and was recorded by c231. No change.

### 3. Something moves under each held melody note (recorded, no change)

Ex. 261 (PDF 199) moves the alto under the soprano's whole note. Here: Bsn 1 (`aes4-- aes4-- g4`) and B.cl. move under Hn 1's tied E♭ at 8 and F at 12; the cello counter-melody moves under the violins' tied notes at 23-24, 27-28, 71-72 and 75-76; at 79-80 the cellos and horns move `des'4 c'4 bes4 | aes2 bes4` under the held B♭6. The exception is 54 b3-4, where melody and counter-line hold together; c231 (finding 3) explained why the tie stays (theme A's head rhythm in the counter-line, protected c201). No change.

### 4. The register drop at 74 b3 is the only place a sung line could not follow (recorded, no change)

Vln I ends the first half of A′ on G5 at 74 and takes the pickup `es'8( f'8` on E♭4, a drop of an octave and a sixth, to restate the second phrase in unison with Vln II and climb to B♭6 at 79. By Ex. 260 a single voice would not make this jump. But the line a listener follows is the melody's pitch-class line, which stays intact, and Vln II carries the same E♭4 pickup from G4. The plan was settled by c299, c309 and c341 ("reopen only with a new book reason"). Ex. 260 is a range table for singers. It is not a reason that outweighs the build to 79, so I do not reopen it.

### 5. Memorability: the head cells return often enough (recorded, no change)

Sebesky's soul-trio example (Ex. 268, PDF 203) gets its individuality from rhythmic inflection of a simple line, and the Dvořák tune repeats its head cell four times in twelve bars. Theme A's head (`es8 f8 | aes4. c8 es4~`) is heard at 7, 11, 23, 27, 71, 75 and in the harp at 83. Its tail is the movement's motto (E♭-D-D♭-C), heard in every A statement, in the B.cl. at 32-34, the horn at 67-68 and Cl 1 at 85-87. Theme B's turn figure (`ges''4 \tuplet 3/2 4 { f''8 ges'' f'' }`) returns at 39 and 55, and its A♭-D♭-F rise at 41, 57 and in the augmented head at 61. The weaker point, theme B's narrow stepwise first phrase (37-40), is recorded and protected (c031, c151, c201, c361 finding 5). No change.

### 6. Doubling for body follows the reading (recorded, no change)

PDF 200 and 203 say that more singers on a line, or an instrument in unison with it, add warmth. The score keeps solos single for the soft statements (Hn 1 7-14, C.a. 41-52) and doubles only toward the peaks: unison violins at 53-56, octaves with flute and oboe at 57-60 (Ex. 263's lead doubled an octave lower), and three octaves at 79-81. This is the order the reading implies: one voice for intimacy, doubled voices for weight. No change.
