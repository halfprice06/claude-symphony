## Findings

All findings are recorded with no change to the score. I found nothing that a change in this cycle would improve without undoing a protected decision.

### 1. Against Tchaikovsky's Serenade: the string-choir handling holds up (recorded)

- **Doubling hand-offs.** Tchaikovsky doubles every melody note, but "not necessarily by the same instrument at all times" (PDF 154): Vln II in mm. 1-2, the violas in mm. 4-5. Here theme B moves Fl 1 (37) → C.a. (41) → Ob 1 with Bsn 1 and Vc (45) → violins (52-53), and each hand-off overlaps by at least a beat. Theme A is scored three ways: horn (7), violins in unison (23), violins in octaves (71). That is the same practice.
- **The big chord after pale harmony.** Adler praises "the big chord in measure fifteen ... after this pale, subdued harmony" (PDF 154; Ex. V-29, m. 15, ff on a single tonic in the basses). Bars 75-78 keep the violins in unison around E♭4-B♭5 at p-mp. Bar 79 then puts B♭ in three octaves (Vln I B♭6, Vln II B♭5, Vla B♭4) over a single D♭ in the basses. This is the same plan.
- **Diminuendo by scoring.** Tchaikovsky thins from (C) to (D) and again to the end (PDF 154). Bars 80-83 do this: the winds fall away at 82, the strings drop to p, and 83 leaves only the harp harmonics, the low strings and the timpani. Bars 87-92 then add the muted strings one section at a time (Vln II and Vla at 87, Vln I at 89) at pp-ppp.
- **The Élégie's frame.** In the Élégie, Tchaikovsky's quiet string introduction comes back at the end of the movement. Here the held strings of 1-3 (C5 in Vln I over a soft, slowly changing chord) come back as the held, muted strings of 89-92, also with C5 on top. A conductor would hear the frame. It is the protected cyclic link (bars 1-2) and the plagal close, so it stays.

A conductor who knows Op. 48 would not find a beginner's sign in the string writing.

### 2. Theme A is never given to the cellos (recorded; no change recommended)

**Problem.** Tchaikovsky gives the second statement of his tune to the low strings, "this time in the bass", with high double basses and a thin, non-brilliant accompaniment above (PDF 154). In the Élégie, too, the tune passes from violins to cellos. Haydn restates his theme "an octave lower to allow the woodwinds to be heard" (PDF 139). Here the string statements of theme A go upward, never down: violins at the A♭4 level (23), then Vln I at the A♭5 level (71), then three octaves (79). The cellos only ever have the counter-melody.

**Why no change.** The low statement exists already: horn 1 at 7-14 sounds the tune at A♭3-B♭4 (aOneHnOne, concert pitch). That register is the Tchaikovsky cello statement's register, given the string-free colour that the cyclic plan requires. Moving theme A to the cellos at 71 would undo the protected octave reprise (71-82 octaves, cycle 9) and lose the cello counter-melody (plan: "a melodic bass counterline, cellos 23-30, 71-82"). The upward course 7 → 23 → 71 → 79 is Adler's octave-adding crescendo (PDF 137) stretched over the whole movement. Recorded so that a later critic does not reopen it without answering this.

### 3. Bars 23-30 versus 71-78: foreground, middleground and background (recorded, follows)

Adler's Beethoven 7/ii example works because each layer has "its own characteristic rhythm and special articulation" and because the layers were heard before they were combined (PDF 142-143). Bars 71-74 meet both tests. The tune is in dotted quarters and eighths. The cello line is in halves with slurred eighth pairs (`es2 aes8( g8 | f2) g4(`). The violas play slurred eighths (`c'8( es' aes') es'( c' aes)`). The tune and the cello line were introduced together at 23, and the viola figure grows out of the triplets at 37-44. Bars 23-30 have quarter-note viola chords that move with the tune at 24 and 28. That is the protected plain first statement (c007, c046, c477, c489). I have no new answer to its reason.

### 4. Bar 92: the octave gap A♭3-A♭4 in the final chord (recorded; do not touch)

Adler's spacing rule is wider gaps low and closer gaps high, as in the overtone series (PDF 151, Ex. V-26). The final chord runs, from the bottom, A♭1 (Cb), A♭2 (Vc), E♭3-A♭3 (Vla div.), A♭4 (Vln II), C5 (Vln I). The gaps are 12, 7, 5, 12 and 4 semitones, so there is an octave above a fifth and a fourth. These are partials 1, 2, 3, 4, 8 and 10 of A♭1 with 5-7 left out. Ex. V-26's first chord also leaves its widest upper gap below the first violins. At ppp, with mutes on, the hole does not sound thin; it lets C5 stand alone as the cyclic top note. c497 ruled the same way, and the last chord stays plain (c210, c480). No change.

### 5. Divisi in the first violins at 62 b3-63 (recorded, follows)

Adler, on Ex. V-29: "divisi would weaken the first violin line" (PDF 152). Vln I is non div. through the arrival at 61-62 b2 (c059). It divides only for the F♭6/A♭6 pair at 62 b3 and for the tremolo chord at 63. At 63 the foreground is the trombone quotation, and the violins are tremolo background, which is the Bizet device of Ex. V-37 (PDF 161). The firsts carry no line there, so the divisi weakens nothing. This agrees with c159's recorded exception.
