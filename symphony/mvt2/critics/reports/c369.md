## What else I studied

**The master work.** I could not get the score of Chausson's Op. 20. IMSLP has it (#05405 and #82933, Baudoux plate E.B. 338; https://imslp.org/wiki/Symphony_in_B-flat_major,_Op.20_(Chausson,_Ernest)), but every download from this container hit IMSLP's bot check. The comparison below therefore uses only descriptions I could cite, and I make no bar-level claims about Chausson's text:
- Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_in_B-flat_(Chausson)): A-B-A′; starts darkly in D minor; the middle section has a more hopeful second subject in B♭; it builds to a double climax whose second peak is a bold return of the first subject; the movement ends in D major.
- Boston Symphony programme note (https://www.bso.org/works/chausson-symphony-in-b-flat): the opening phrase is three rising notes taken from the first movement's motto; in the slightly faster middle section the English horn has a long solo over delicate string figuration; the violins then take the material high and build to the climax; Chausson cut and revised a passage of this movement he disliked.
- https://fugueforthought.de/2018/06/11/ernest-chausson-symphony-in-b-flat-op-20/: a soft overall sound, with woodwind colour added here and there.

## Findings

None of the following needs a score change. Measured against Adler ch. 5, the string writing follows every applicable principle. Where a principle is met only through its exception, earlier critics have already settled the point with reasons that still hold. The findings are ranked by how much each bears on the question "would a conductor believe a master wrote this?". I made no edit in /tmp/critic-369/work, so it is identical to v369, whose build is clean (0 warnings, 8 pages, 0 outer-voice parallels, parts book 30 pages).

**1. The overall plan matches Chausson's (bars 37-82). No change.**
Chausson's slow movement has a faster middle section with an English-horn solo over light string figuration. The violins then take the material up high to a climax, and the first subject comes back boldly as a second peak. This movement does the same:
- Poco più mosso at 37.
- The C.a. solo at 41-44 over pizzicato viola triplets and pizzicato basses (Adler PDF 160-161, Ex. V-36).
- Theme B moves to the violins at 52-60 and rises to D♭6-A♭6 at 61-62.
- The second ff peak, at 79, is theme A's return, with B♭6 in Vln I and Fl 1.

The string doubling at the second peak is different from the first (unison then octaves, Grieg principle, PDF 154-155). The crescendo into 61 is built by adding octaves (PDF 137, Ex. V-14). A conductor who knows Chausson's movement would recognise the plan and its execution.

**2. Chausson's slow movement is minor turning to major; this one is major throughout. No change.**
Chausson begins darkly in D minor and ends in D major, so the major ending is the goal. This movement is in A♭ major from bar 6. Its darkness comes in episodes: F minor at 19-20, B♭ minor at 45, F♯ minor at 47-51, A minor at 63-64. The plan is protected: the key plan, the F♭→E♭ thread, and theme A's harmony and motto tail (cyclic-notes §3; HISTORY standing list). Movement I already supplies the minor-to-major arc (C minor ending on a C-major chord). A minor opening here would repeat it. I record the difference and recommend nothing.

**3. Length: about 6 minutes against Chausson's roughly 9-10. No change.**
Chausson's movement is longer because he develops the first subject at length before the middle section. This one states theme A three times in 92 bars. Proportions were settled at c253, c303, c319 and c329, and nothing in Adler ch. 5 bears on length. Recorded only.

**4. 47-51: the dynamic separation is a last resort. Settled, no change.**
Vln II plays a counter-line (F♯5-A5-G5-E♯5) above the C.a.'s protected quotation. The two are separated mainly by dynamic, pp against p-mp. Adler says that separating lines by marking different dynamics "is not always as effective as the other ways" (PDF 144). The other ways are register, thinning and rhythm. Rhythm is already used: Vln II plays half notes against the C.a.'s held notes and pickups, and the violas play off-beat eighths (Elijah pattern, PDF 158, Ex. V-32). c263, c294, c324 and c343 placed and spaced this passage. Moving Vln II below the C.a. would put it among the violas' off-beat A3-E♭4. I tried that on paper and it is worse than the current text. No change.

**5. 62-63: Vln I divided at the climax. Settled, no change.**
Adler says divisi would weaken the first-violin line (PDF 152). Vln I divides only at 62 b3 (F♭6/A♭6) and 63 (E6/A6 tremolo). This is the top of the climax, and Fl 1-2 double it. c324 recorded it. No change.

**6. 87: IV6 with the bass F tripled. Follows the reading.**
Adler's rule is to double the third of a first-inversion chord "near the bottom" and keep the open sound (PDF 152, Ex. V-27). At 87 the F is in the basses (F1), cellos (F2) and violas (F3), all at the bottom of the chord. The upper voices (A♭4, C.a., Cl 1) keep the chord open. This agrees with the c207 and c347 decisions. Recorded so that the point is not reopened.

**7. 15-19: Vln II as the "melodized" inner part. Follows the reading.**
The Haydn example (PDF 139) gives the second violins a little melodic movement between two statements of the theme. Here Vln II holds A♭4 with lower neighbours G4 and F4 under the Ob 1 and Vln I solo dialogue, then descends E4-F4-C4-B♭3 at 20-21. It is a pedal with neighbour notes, not a static drone, and it does not compete with the solo violin (an octave and more below). No change.
