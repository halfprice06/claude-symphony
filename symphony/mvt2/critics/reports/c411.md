## Findings

### 1. Theme A in the strings: colour and register follow the chapter, and the tune is memorable (recorded, no change)

**Bars.** 22 b3-30, 70 b3-82.

The first string statement of theme A (22 b3-26) is in both violin sections in unison, sul D, p to mp. That is the warm, subdued colour of Ex. III-6 (Scheherazade iii, sul D, p), and E♭5, a minor ninth above the open D, is about 5th position, where the D string "mellows even more" (PDF 75). The tune moves to ord. at 27 for its f phrase. At the reprise (71) Vln I takes the tune on the E string at p, which Adler calls "clear and luminous when one plays softly" (PDF 75), over Vln II sul D an octave lower. The peak B♭6 at 79 is the only note above the 7th-position A6. It comes after a stepwise run (78 b3) and is doubled by flute 1, so the section does not have to find it after a leap (PDF 72). Tchaikovsky's sul G reprise (Ex. III-5) is not usable here: theme A's E♭5 would lie about two octaves above the open G, well past the 7th position.

On the lens question: theme A is the tune a listener will hum. It has a rising arpeggio to a held, syncopated E♭, the chromatic motto tail E♭-D-D♭-C and the B♭ appoggiatura at 12/28/76/79. It is heard three times in full, each time in a higher register (horn A♭3, violins A♭4, violins A♭5 with the B♭6 peak), and its head comes back once more in the coda harp. The Dvořák Largo tune is built the same way: a short, simple tune that is stated, contrasted and then returned to. Nothing in the reading argues for changing it.

### 2. Theme B follows the reading's own model for a soft A-string tune (recorded, no change)

**Bars.** 37-44 (flute, then cor anglais), 52 b4-56 (violins).

Theme B's antecedent moves mostly by step and repeats one figure in every bar: a long note, two neighbour eighths, a step (2 8 8 4, with the triplet turn at 39/55). Earlier critics (c251, c261, c271) asked whether a tune this narrow could be remembered. Ex. III-7 on PDF 75 answers this. It is Brahms 3/iii, the violins' statement of a famous slow-movement tune, and Adler prints it as his model of soft, lyrical writing on the A string. The Brahms tune also has a narrow range and repeats a one-bar cell, with a hairpin on nearly every bar. The score sets theme B the same way at 52 b4-56: "sul A, cantabile", p, a swell in 53 and 55, rising p-mp-mf. The consequent (57-60) then adds the leap (A♭-D♭) and the range (A♭6 at 58), and the augmented head at 61-62 is the version a listener carries away. In Dvořák 9/ii and in Brahms 3/iii the middle-section tune is also less striking than the main one. c141's closure stands, and this reading gives one more reason for it.

### 3. Bar 52, cellos: the D3 of the C3-D3-E♭3 line falls on the open D string (applied)

**Bars.** 51-52 (Vc `c1 | d2\< es2`), at pp with a crescendo, over the basses' A♭ pedal.

**Problem.** The scan of v411's score.midi found 17 string notes sounding an open-string pitch. Fifteen are covered by a sul mark (Vln II 62-64 sul A/sul G; Vla G3 at 65 sul C; Vc A3 at 46 sul D), are short figuration (Vla D4 at 50, G3 at 77-78), or are unavoidable bass notes in a tutti or a pizzicato (Vc C2 at 55; Cb at 40, 62-64). The remaining one is the cellos' D3 at 52 b1-2. The note is held for two beats, it is the ♯4 that gives bar 52 its colour (c322: with Vln II's F5 it makes the A♭-C-D-F sonority of bar 86), and it carries a crescendo into the violins' sul A entry of theme B. A section reading C3 on the G string in first position will most easily take D3 as the open string.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 73-74: open strings "stand out peculiarly"; "in slow, expressive passages, one usually wants all the tones to be of equal and controlled texture". The open string is not "under the controlling influence provided by the finger which oscillates a stopped note", so it cannot share the vibrato of the crescendo. PDF 73: timbre is the reason to name a string; PDF 76: mark sul G when a passage is to stay on one string. On the G string the line is C3 (first position, 4th finger), then D3 and E♭3 in 4th position (fingers 1 and 2). These are stopped notes on the cello's sonorous lower string, and the hand is already placed for D♭3-C3 at 53. c281 made the same correction for Vln II at 62.

**Fix (applied in /tmp/critic-411/work/sections/bSec.ily, bSecVc).** A dashed "sul G" spanner over 51-52, in the same style as Vln II's "sul A" at 62:

```
| \once \override TextSpanner.bound-details.left.text = \markup \italic "sul G"
  \once \override TextSpanner.bound-details.right.text = \markup \draw-line #'(0 . -1)
  c1\startTextSpan | d2\< << es2 { s4 s8. s16\stopTextSpan } >> |
```

No pitch, rhythm, dynamic or MIDI change. The hook ends the spanner before 53, so no "ord." is needed.

### 4. The cellos as a singing voice (recorded, no change)

Adler says the cello's beauty shows best in section solos and "where the cello has a voice within the contrapuntal texture" (PDF 99). The score uses the second option throughout. The counter-melody at 23-30 and 71-77 is sul D, the string Adler calls "the most musically captivating" (PDF 99), and lies E♭3-D♭4 without crossing to the A string (c386). It moves to the brilliant A string ("ord.", 78) only for the f-ff climb C♭4-C4-D♭4 into 79. The cellos sing theme B's B♭-minor head with oboe 1 at 44-47, sul D, and the augmented head at 61-62 ff on the A string. The couplings in Ex. III-64 (cello with horn, with bassoon) are those of 57-60 and 75-80. c074 closed the question of the cellos taking theme A itself, because the cellos already carry an independent counter-melody and a fourth statement would be needed. This reading adds nothing against that reason.

### 5. Solo violin at 17-22 (recorded, no change)

The concertmaster plays senza sord. while the section is muted and answers oboe 1 in two-bar phrases (A♭5-B♭5-G5-F5 | E♭5-D♭5; F5-G5-E♭5-D5 | E♭5). Brahms 1/ii (Ex. III-20, PDF 81) gives the solo violin a longer line that rises over the winds. Here the two answers are short, but the passage is the contrasting b phrase of theme A, not a second tune. The short answers keep theme A the only long melody in the A section, which is good for memorability. The passage is protected (c121), and the reading gives no reason to lengthen it.

### 6. Skips and chromatic steps in the string melodies (recorded, no change)

Widest legato skip in a section melody: Vln I G5-E♭6 at 77 b1-2 (c341), a minor sixth on the E string at mp<, inside a slur. This is the "same string" case of Ex. III-22, but it is small, slow and soft, and flute 1 doubles the E♭6. The other skips are B♭4→A♭5 across 56→57 under a new slur and "ord." (an easy crossing), and the descending A♭5 at 76 b3 → G5 at 77 b1, a step. None is the hard legato case of Ex. III-24. The motto tail E♭-D-D♭-C (24 sul D in about 5th position; 72 on the E string) falls one finger per note, so it needs no same-finger slide (PDF 84, Ex. III-25). Adler leaves the choice of portamento to the player.

### 7. Violas and basses (recorded, no change)

The violas' figures (37-60, 71-78) lie on the G and D strings, which Adler calls the accompaniment strings (PDF 87). The viola uses its sombre C string for the held G3 at 64-65 and the F3-F♭3-E♭3 line at 87-90 (PDF 86). At the peak 79-81 the violas carry theme A's third octave with the cor anglais, as violins and violas do in octaves in Ex. III-38. The basses play soli where Adler calls a solo bass "thin and distant" (PDF 105): under horn 1's motto tail at 65-70 and in the coda. There the thin sound is wanted. Every long tenuto has "change bow at will" (PDF 108), and there are no double stops (PDF 107).
