## Findings (ranked)

### 1. Bar 25: the half cadence of the string statement is reached straight from I. Applied.
**Problem.** Bar 25 is A♭ major for the whole bar. The basses hold A♭ (cycle 52), the violas hold A♭3–E♭4, and the cellos go A♭3–C4. Bar 26 is V. The violins' melody on beat 3 is A♭ (1̂), falling to G (7̂). The horn statement of the same phrase (bar 9) puts IV under that beat (cycle 12's fix). The string statement, the louder and fuller one, does not.
**Evidence.** A&S PDF 135 §1: IV and II "lead into and intensify dominant harmony"; I–IV–V is "more intense" than a bass that only touches the tonic triad. PDF 136 §3: over IV, "1̂ (moving to 7̂)" is one of "the most usable" soprano tones at cadences. That is exactly the violins' A♭→G here. The cellos already move to C4 on beat 3. Over IV that C becomes a 7th. It is prepared because the violins sounded C on beats 1–2 (PDF 140, Ex. 9-9: a 7th "can enter by leap, the preparation occurring in another voice"), and it falls by step to B♭ at 26. Cycle 12 did not make this change because the violas' D♭ would have clashed with the cellos' C. The fix below has no D♭ in the violas.
**Fix (aOne.ily).**
- `aOneCb` bar 25: `aes,,2.` → `aes,,2 des,4`
- `aOneVla` bar 25: `<aes es'>2.` → `<aes es'>2 f'4` (both players on F4 for the beat)

Beat 3 becomes IV7: D♭2–C4–F4–A♭4–A♭5. Into 26 the bass rises while the cellos (C→B♭), violas (F→E♭/B♭) and violins (A♭→G) fall, as in Ex. 9-3a. There are no 5ths: the violas' A♭3 is not held over the D♭, which is what avoids A♭/D♭→B♭/E♭. The cello counter-melody's pitches do not change.

### 2. Bar 78 beat 3: a 6/4 on the dominant falls on a weak beat and moves to IV. Applied.
**Problem.** After the deceptive cadence on ♭VI, beat 3 of bar 78 has E♭ in the bass (basses, Bsn 2, harp) and A♭–C above it (cellos A♭3, Cl 2 A♭4, violas C4–A♭3, harp A♭3–C4–E♭4, Hn 1·2 C4, violins C). That is a cadential-position 6/4. It goes to IV (D♭) at 79, not to V.
**Evidence.** Unit 10 §1 (PDF 152) and Points 1 (PDF 160): a 6/4 over 5̂ "is not an inversion of I; it is an embellishment and intensification of V". A 6/4 that goes on to IV is therefore a V–IV move, which the box on PDF 135 and syntax rule 2 on PDF 148 exclude. It also sits on beat 3 (PDF 154 §6; Points 3). It is not a passing 6/4 in A&S's sense either: that chord prolongs one harmony, I–I6 or IV–IV6 (Unit 19, PDF 309). ♭VI and IV are two different chords. Cycle 70 brought in the E♭ bass to keep the F♭→E♭ thread, and the 6/4 came with it. Before that, b3 was F–A♭–C (vi), which led correctly to IV.
**Fix (aTwo.ily).** Keep the F♭→E♭ bass and the C on top, and replace each A♭ on the beat with G. Beat 3 becomes E♭–G–C (iii6), a passing chord in the falling bass F♭–E♭–D♭. The A♭→G and C♭→C moves are half steps out of ♭VI.
- `aTwoVc` bar 78: `ces'2\f aes4` → `ces'2\f c'4` (then C4→D♭4 at 79)
- `aTwoClTwo` bar 78: `aes'2\f aes'4` → `aes'2\f g'4` (G4→A♭4 at 79)
- `aTwoVla` bar 78: last eighth `aes` → `g`
- `aTwoHarpR` bar 78: `<aes c' es'>4` → `<g c' es'>4` (G is natural in the 71 pedal setting)

The violins' grace run F–G–A♭ now starts from a chord tone. The ♭VI at 78 b1, the bass of 78–82 and the B♭6 at 79 are unchanged.

### 3. Bar 39 (and 55): ii7 moves to vi6 over the tonic bass, so the II harmony never reaches a dominant. Applied at 39 only.
**Problem.** Bars 38 b3–39 b2 expand II (ii6 → ii7, bass G♭–E♭). On 39 b3 the bass falls to D♭ under D♭–F–B♭ (vi6), and only then does vii°7/vi (40) lead to vi. In B♭ minor, the key the phrase moves into, this is iv7 → i6 → vii°7 → i.
**Evidence.** PDF 135 box and syntax rule 3 (PDF 148): "Intermediate harmonies do not lead to I or I6." Points 1 (PDF 151): they "lead from I to V, not from V to I." The flute's E♭ on 39 b3 is also an accented dissonance against the D♭ bass that resolves on the weak beat 4.
**Fix (bSec.ily), bar 39 beats 3–4.**
- `bSecCb`: `es,4 r4 des,4 r4` → `es,4 r4 c,4 r4`
- `bSecHarpL`: `<des, des>4` → `<c, c>4` (the "A♮" pedal mark stays)
- `bSecVla`: second triplet group `bes8( des' f' des' bes des')` → `bes8( es' ges' es' bes es')`
- `bSecVlnTwo`: `<des' f'>2` → `<es' ges'>2`

The chord is now C–E♭–G♭–B♭ (viiø7 of D♭), a dominant-function chord after ii7, as rule 2 asks. It shares C–E♭–G♭ with the vii°7/vi at 40: the bass falls C→A and B♭ in the violas moves to C. The flute's E♭ on beat 3 is now a chord tone, and its D♭ on beat 4 is an unaccented passing tone to C. I did not change 55. There the Hn 1/Cl 1 counter-line (protected since cycle 97) has F4 on 55 b3, which needs vi6 under it. The second statement can keep vi6 as a variant, or the composer can treat both bars the same way by changing that counter-line note to G♭ (tied from b1).

### 4. Bars 11→12, 27→28, 75→76: IV is repeated from beat 3 onto the downbeat under a consonant tie. Not applied.
A&S Ex. 9-24 (PDF 149) separates a weak-to-strong repetition that is "poor" (9-24a) from one that is "good because of dissonant suspension" (9-24b). The theme's first half is the good case: at 8, 24 and 72 the tied E♭ becomes the 7th of vi7 on the downbeat. The second half is the poor case: at 12, 28 and 76 the tied F is the 3rd of the same IV that arrived on beat 3, so the downbeat brings no new harmony. Cycle 12 left this as a deliberate syncopation. The horn bars 11–12 are protected. The string bars have some new attacks on the downbeat (the violas and cellos at 28, harp and violas at 76). I tested on paper the one book-based fix, vi on beat 3 (bass F: I–I6–vi–IV–ii7–V7). It gives a direct octave between the cellos' F3 and the bass, and it gives up cycle 12's rising bass. I recommend no change. I list it so the next harmony critic does not reopen it without new grounds.
