## Findings

### 1. Bars 49 b4–50 b1: the Neapolitan goes to a V with no 7th and a tonic with no third (applied)

**Problem.** At 49 b3 the Neapolitan sixth of F♯ minor (G–B–D over B; c062's protected chord) has its bass doubled in octaves: Vc B2 and Cb B1. On b4 both basses step to C♯ (Vc `b,4 cis4`). V is then C♯2, C♯3, C♯5 (C.a.) and E♯5 (Vln II), with G♯3 only on the violas' off-beat eighth and no 7th. On 50 b1 the cellos leap back to F♯3, so the tonic arrives as F♯–C♯–F♯ with no third until the violas' off-beat A3 and Vln II's A5 on b2. The movement's only real Neapolitan therefore resolves into two hollow chords.

**Evidence.** A&S PDF 484 (§3, Ex. 28-2b): "In general, the bass (4̂) is the best tone to double. This doubling allows one to prepare the 7th of V7". In Ex. 28-2b the doubled 4̂ is held as the 7th of V7 and falls to 3̂. Points for Review 4 (PDF 499) says the same. PDF 483 (§2): the intensity of ♭II comes from the pull of ♭2̂ against the arrival of V, and this voicing makes the most of it. Here the doubling is already in place, and the cellos simply fail to use it.

**Fix (applied).** bSec.ily, bSecVc, bars 49–50 (line 255). Was `fis2 b,4 cis4 | fis2 b,2 |`. Now:

```lilypond
fis2 b,2 | a,2 b,2 |
```

The cellos hold B2 as the 7th of V7 under the basses' C♯2 at 49 b4. They resolve it down to A2, the third of i, at 50 b1, then return to B2 at 50 b3 as before. 49 b4 is now a complete V7 (C♯ E♯ G♯ B), and 50 b1 is a complete i. The violas' G♯3–A3 and the cellos' B2–A2 converge to an octave by contrary motion. Nothing else in the texture changes. The C.a. quotation, the Neapolitan chord at 49 b3, Vln II and the basses are unchanged. The change is pitch only, as c182 allowed under the c124 rule, and no dynamic or orchestration changes.

**Test build.** /tmp/critic-312/work: build-warnings.txt is empty, analysis.txt shows 0 outer-voice parallels, and the score is 8 pages. The all-pairs output differs from v312 only by removing the Vc/Cb octave doublings at 49–50; there are no new fifths or octaves. The parts book was regenerated (`genparts.py`, `lilypond`, no warnings). It is 30 pages, each part except the harp is on one page, and the cello part was checked by eye.

### 2. Bar 64 b3–b4: chords spelled as vii°7 and V7 of A minor (recorded, no change)

The vii°7 of V (D–F–A♭–C♭) and the German sixth (F♭–A♭–C♭–D) are written D–F–G♯–B and E–G♯–B–D (Vla `gis2`, Vc `b,8`, Cb `e,4`, Vln II `b4 d'4`). They resolve to E♭7 at 65 by four augmented unisons. A&S PDF 524–525 (§24 and its Note) allows an augmented sixth written as a dominant seventh when the context explains it, "ease in reading and motivic connections" being the usual reasons. Here the two bars before are A minor (♭ii). Written this way, the chord is first heard as V7 of A minor and only then as the German sixth of A♭. That is exactly the double meaning of the Chopin examples (Ex. 29-39). A♭-key spelling would need F♭ and C♭ in four string parts that have just played A minor. The old open point "spelling E–G♯–B" (c080–c088) can stay closed. **Fix.** None.

### 3. Bars 61–65: ♭VI–♭ii–V7, as in the reading's Verdi and Beethoven examples (recorded, no change)

The climax follows two of the unit's models closely. Ex. 28-14 (PDF 489–490, Verdi, at the end of a major movement) has ♭VI acting as the dominant of ♭II, then V7–I. Here F♭ major at 62 b3 acts as V of A minor (= B𝄫 minor) at 63–64, which goes on to V7 at 65 and I at 71. Ex. 28-17 (PDF 492) restates a theme on ♭II. Here movement I's P head is stated in the ♭ii region. §12 (PDF 493) adds that figuration over ♭II should follow the tonic scale when V comes soon. The quotation's B♭3 at 63 b4 is 2̂ of A♭, not a note of A minor, and it points to the coming E♭7. This is another reason to keep 61–65 as it stands. **Fix.** None.

### 4. Chromatic spelling across the movement (recorded, no change)

I scanned every part for chromatic neighbours, chromatic passing tones and melodic augmented 2nds, and checked each against the .ily spelling. Descending passing tones are lowered notes (PDF 497): Cl 2 C♭ at 58 b4, Hn 3 F♭ at 80 b3, C.a. F♭ at 88, C.a. C♭ at 33 (c152). The motto tail's D♮ descending to D♭ (8, 24, 42, 58, 68, 72, 85–89) is the ♯4̂ the reading allows in both directions in major (PDF 498, Ex. 28-24). The enharmonic changes at 46→47, 50→51 and 62→63 come at harmonic pivots, as §9 (PDF 490) allows. **Fix.** None.

### 5. Augmented sixths: where and how many (recorded, no change)

The movement has two augmented sixths. A French sixth from IV by chromatic voice exchange closes the b phrase at 21 (c152). A German sixth from vii°7 of V, with the 2̂ anticipation, ends the B section at 64 (c210, c232). Each comes "just before important structural points" (PDF 503). The all-pairs scan finds no consecutive fifths anywhere in v312, so the German sixth's usual problem (PDF 505–506, 514–515) is solved. I looked again for a third place and found none that a protection or the melody does not block: 43 (Fl 1 G♭/A♭), 59 b4 (melody B♭ blocks B𝄫), 68 (c089), 73–74 (cello counter-melody), 78 (79 is not V). Adding more would weaken the two that mark the form. **Fix.** None.

### 6. Coda 87–89 (recorded, not recommended)

§15 of Unit 28 (PDF 495, Ex. 28-20) and §22 of Unit 29 (PDF 522, Ex. 29-36) show a "♭II" or an augmented sixth on ♭2̂ as a neighbour of the final tonic over a pedal, working like a plagal IV. The coda already does the plagal version: IV6 (87), iv with F♭ (88), I (89), over the A♭ pedal. A ♭II colour (B𝄫) there would weaken the D♭–C on top that the cyclic rules require. **Fix.** None.
