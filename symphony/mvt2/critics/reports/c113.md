## Findings

Ranked by how much each stands between the score and a master's work. On this reading, nothing does. None of the findings below calls for a change.

### 1. The middle section's chromatic route is Schumann's Widmung route ♭VI → ♭II → V7. Keep it (no change)

**Bars:** 61–65.

**Analysis.** 61 IV (D♭, the B section's tonic, authentic arrival). 62 b1–2 G♭, 62 b3–4 F♭ (♭VI of A♭), with flute 1's A♭6 held over both as the common tone, as Widmung holds A♭/G♯ over A♭→F♭ (PDF 586, Ex. 32-3). 63–64 A minor, which in A♭ is B♭♭ minor, the minor form of ♭II. 64 b3 is the diminished seventh G♯–B–D–F. 64 b4 is E7. 65 is E♭7.

**Evidence.**
- PDF 593: "the usual goal of ♭VI is a cadential V ... the half-step progression in the bass helps to intensify the V ... In the Schumann song, ♭VI moves to V7 through ♭II." The bass here is F♭(E) 62 – A 63 – D–E 64 – E♭ 65. The ♭6–5 half step is kept as the last bass move.
- PDF 596–597: the ♭II is minor, a double-mixture product. The reading calls this the hardest kind of area to integrate, and Haydn's answer is to plunge in through its V7. The score does the same: the F♭ chord at 62 b3 is the V of A minor.
- PDF 591–592: the large plan is IV → [stop] → V. That is the K. 421 idea, where E♭ is "a stop on the way" from F to A minor.
- c103 recorded the same plan in Unit 26 terms. This reading adds the specific model.

**Fix.** None. This passage is already protected ("F♭–E–E♭ approach 62–65", c010; "chromatic bass 61–65", c083), and the reading confirms the protection.

### 2. 64 b4–65: E7 → E♭7 is heard as a chromatic slide, not as a German sixth. Acceptable; record only (no change)

**Bars:** 64 b3–65.

**Problem considered.** PDF 591–592 (Ex. 32-10a): a V7 reread as a German sixth gives a modulation down a half step. E7 is enharmonically F♭–A♭–C♭–D, the Ger6 of A♭, and as a Ger6 its D should rise to E♭. In the score Vln II moves D4 → D♭4 (64 b4 → 65), Vla G♯3 → G3 and the bass E → E♭. Every voice falls a semitone, so the ear hears V7 of A minor sliding to V7 of A♭ (modulation by chromatic inflection, PDF 584–585), not an augmented sixth resolving.

**Why I do not propose a change.**
- The D♭4 in Vln II at 65–66 is the 7th of the dominant. c103 and c093 protect the E♭7 with its D♭ at 65–70.
- To voice it as a German sixth, Vln II would rise to E♭4. The 7th would then need another voice, and none can take it without a bad leap: Vc E2 → D♭3 is a diminished seventh, and Vla must keep G♯→G.
- Both readings are in the unit. The descending slide fits the movement's motive better: E/F♭ → E♭ is the thread, and the D → D♭ step repeats the motto tail's D–D♭ two bars before horn 1 states it (67–68).

**Fix.** None.

### 3. The remote pitches are planted early and resolved in the top voice, as in Haydn's Creation example. Keep (no change)

**Bars:** 1, 5, 19–20, 50–51, 63–64, 71.

**Evidence.** PDF 597–598 (Ex. 32-17): Haydn integrates C minor by (a) taking its main top-voice tone E♭ from E♮, (b) returning through the enharmonic D♯, and (c) stressing D♯–E as a leading tone in the first section. The score does the same three things:
- (c) E♮ appears as a leading tone to F at 19–20 (Vla F4–E4, Vln II E4 at 20 b3) and as the violas' E♮ in bar 1. F♭ is planted at bar 5 (Cl 2, iv).
- (a) The A-minor area's top voice is A6 (63, Fl 1 and Vln I) and E6 (64, Vln I).
- (b) The top-voice E6 of 64 resolves to E♭6 when theme A returns at 71 b3 (Vln I, upper octave), in the same instrument and octave.
- A♮, the other remote pitch (the third of F♯ minor), is resolved by the cor anglais A4 → A♭4 at 50–51.
- A♮ of 63–64 recurs nowhere after 64. That is consistent with PDF 593: the area is the dominant-side expansion of F♭/E, and the pitch that has to be worked out is E/F♭, which comes back at 78, 80–81 and 87–89.

**Fix.** None.

### 4. The reprise answers ♭VI as Mozart's K. 542 does. Keep (no change)

**Bars:** 77–82.

**Evidence.** PDF 601–602 (Ex. 32-21): after the deceptive cadence on ♭VI, "the G major is not followed by a quick relaxation of tension; ... it is inflected", and only then come V and a strong authentic cadence. In the score:
- the deceptive V → ♭VI at 77–78 keeps the melody's A♭ as the common tone;
- ii6/5 arrives ff at 79;
- at 80 b3 ii is inflected to ii°7 by F♭ (Hn 3, Vla, mixture);
- F♭ resolves to E♭ inside V at 81 (V♭9 → V7);
- the PAC with 2̂–1̂ on top comes at 82.

This is also PDF 593's Waldstein point: the chromatic pitch of the middle section (F♭) is resolved inside the home key's cadence.

**Fix.** None. The F♭→E♭ bass at 78 (c070) and the V♭9 at 80–81 (c082) stay.

### 5. Proportions and the tension curve match the corpus. Keep (no change)

**Bars:** whole movement.

**Evidence.**
- Durations from the MIDI: A 83 s, B 117 s, A′ 44 s, coda 39 s. Dvořák 9/ii has the same ratios: A′/A ≈ 0.5, B/A ≈ 1.4.
- This score has two peaks: B at 61 (64% of the duration) and A′ at 79 (84%), the higher one with B♭6. Dvořák has one ff in B. Tchaikovsky 6/iv and Beethoven 9/iii put the main peak late, so both layouts are master models.
- The B-section peak resolves to IV and then turns into the chromatic digression. The A′ peak sits on the predominant after ♭VI. They therefore differ in function, as PDF 592 asks of chromatic areas within one diatonic plan.
- The coda (39 s) is about as long as A′. That was settled at c013, c023 and c103, and Dvořák's Molto adagio coda is proportionally similar.

**Fix.** None.

### 6. Enharmonic spellings are the readable ones. Keep (no change)

**Bars:** 47–50, 62–64.

**Evidence.**
- PDF 588: Widmung writes E for F♭ because notating F♭ "would certainly be possible, though unkind to the performers".
- 47–50 is G♭ minor (iv of D♭ by mixture) written as F♯ minor. 62 keeps F♭ where the chord comes from the climax head's G♭→F♭, and 63–64 switch to A minor where the trombone quotation needs plain notation. This is notational, not structural, enharmonics.
- c083 closed the F♭/E spelling at 62. I agree.

**Fix.** None.
