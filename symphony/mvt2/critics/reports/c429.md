## Findings

They are ranked by how much each matters to the question "would a conductor believe a master wrote this?" Only Finding 2 proposes a change.

**1. Comparison with Rachmaninoff PC2/ii: the plan holds up (1-14, 37-56, 61-64, 83-92). No change.**
Rachmaninoff's slow movement and this one solve the same problem: a slow movement in a remote major key after a C-minor first movement. Both start with a few soft bars that move out of C, and both give the first theme to one solo wind over a plucked or broken-chord accompaniment, not to the strings. Here it is bars 1-6, then horn 1 over bassoons, bass clarinet and harp. Both keep the strings back for a later statement and save the loudest moment for the middle (61-62 here). The theme goes back to the violins with a new accompaniment (71-78 here, with the viola eighth-note figure, which matches PDF 133's ornamental basis, Ex. 265). Both end very soft on a held tonic. Two differences are worth noting, but neither calls for a change. First, Rachmaninoff's accompaniment is a nearly continuous broken-chord figure, while this movement changes texture at each phrase. That follows Rimsky's call for each return to be scored afresh (PDF 116-118; c413, c419). Second, Rachmaninoff passes one long theme from flute to clarinet without a break. The closest thing here is C.a. (41-44) to Ob 1 (45), which starts a new phrase, so PDF 125-126 on shared notes does not apply. A conductor would accept the shape and the scoring as a professional's work.

**2. Bar 28 (and 76): the violins' B flat5 appoggiatura sounds over the cellos' held A flat3, its own resolution note, in the same string colour (applied at 28 only in /tmp/critic-429/work).**
- Problem: at 28 b2 the violins in unison play B flat5, an appoggiatura that resolves to A flat5 on b3. The cellos' counter-melody holds A flat3 (`aes2( des'4)`) from b1 through b2. The whole texture is strings, so the dissonance sounds against its own resolution in the same timbre.
- Evidence: Rimsky PDF 132-133 says that a florid line with non-harmonic notes must not run against a basis that sounds the essential notes. Displacing the line by an octave or giving it a different timbre lessens the clash. "The greater the dissimilarity in timbre ... the less discordant." Here the line and the basis are both strings, so the softening from a different timbre is absent. c279 raised this point, and it was left open as optional ("counter-melody protected, clash mild").
- Answer to the stated reason: the fix keeps the counter-melody's two structural notes, A flat3 on b1 and D flat4 on b3, along with its rhythm in the protected plain first statement (23-30, c007/c009). It moves only the held half of b1-2 to a chord tone, F3, the third of IV, which is what the violas hold. The clash is mild, but it falls on the movement's first f, on the theme's peak note.
- Fix (sections/aOne.ily, aOneVc, bar 28):
  `\after 4 \f \after 4 \> aes2( des'4)` becomes `\after 4 \f \after 4 \> aes4( f4 des'4)`.
  The violas repeat F4 on b2 rather than move to it, so the F3 forms no parallel octave. F3 to D flat4 under the bass's D flat2 to B flat2 gives a compound sixth then a compound third.
- Bar 76: do not copy the fix there. The violas' eighths move A flat4 to F4 into 76 b2 (`f'( aes' f') bes(...)`), so a cello A flat3 to F3 would make parallel octaves. At 76 the harp sounds D flat-F on b1, which adds a different timbre to the basis, so the case is weaker. Leave 76 as it is.

**3. v419 to v429: nothing got worse. No change.**
The four changes are small. The rehearsal letter at 31 fixes a real gap and costs no layout (still 8 pages). **4. Brass and timpani re-entries checked against PDF 136. No change.**
Every return after a rest is pp, ppp or ff (Hn 1 53, low brass 60, Tbn 1 63, timpani 67 and 84, Hn 3-4 87), or has a recorded reason: Hn 4 p at 60 (one degree above the trombones, PDF 103), Hn 3-4 and timpani f at 79, and Hn 1-2 mf< at 78 b3 (c088/c218/c273, not to be re-raised). Two p entries are not on that list: Hn 1's solo at 67 (four bars after the climax) and Hn 2's counter-melody at 75. Both are melodic lines that must be heard, not colour returns, and Rimsky's remark is about colour returns. The piece starts ppp and ends ppp, as the same page asks.

**5. Alternating groups at 62-63 keep smooth voice leading (PDF 126-127, Ex. 239-243). No change.**
At 62 b3 the winds and brass hold F flat major (spelled F flat-A flat-C flat). The strings take over at 63 on A minor with E on top, and every voice moves by step or holds: Fl 1 and Vln I G sharp6 (A flat6) to A6, F flat to E as a common tone, C flat (B) to C. There is no false relation across the groups, which is the danger Ex. 242-243 show. This is the protected quotation entry, recorded only so that later critics need not test it again.

**6. The 57-61 build is a diverging progression of the kind in Ex. 256-258 (PDF 131, 419-423). No change.**
The bass goes down from F2 (57) through G flat1-G1 (59) to A flat1 (60) and D flat at 61. The top goes up from A flat5 to A flat6. The middle fills in the order the book gives: clarinets and bassoons at 57, horn 4, trombone 2, bass trombone and tuba pp at 60, all horns at 61. The viola sextuplets at 59-60 are the stationary middle that holds it together. Ex. 258 uses non-staccato divided violins for the same purpose.

**7. The coda's release follows Ex. 249 (PDF 415) and PDF 130 on clarinets. No change.**
Ex. 249 ends a Larghetto with the winds marked mor. one after another over divided strings con sord. Here the horns stop at 90 and the clarinet goes morendo at 91, which PDF 130 names the clarinet's speciality. The strings alone end ppp at 92.
