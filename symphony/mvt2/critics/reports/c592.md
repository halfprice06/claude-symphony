## Findings

No change to the score is needed from this reading. The points below are ranked by importance; each records what the book teaches and how the score meets it, so that later critics do not reopen settled questions.

### 1. Bar 49: the Neapolitan 6th is set as in Example 28-2b. No change.
Bars: 48-50. Evidence: 49 b3 is G–B–D over B1/B2 (Cb, Vc) with the viola also on B3, so 4̂ is the doubled note (U28 §3, PDF 484: "the bass (4̂) is the best tone to double"). The top string voice, Vln II, has ♭2̂ G5 and moves straight to E♯5 on b4, the melodic diminished 3rd that A&S give for ♭II6 → V7 (PDF 483-484, Ex. 28-2a). The cellos keep B2 on b4 as V's seventh and take it down to A2 at 50 (Ex. 28-2b, "this doubling allows one to prepare the 7th of V7"). The approach is a 5–6 over i at 49 b1-2 (U28 §6, PDF 485). G♮ never moves straight to G♯ in any voice (U28 §4, PDF 484). Fix: none. Keep the c312 cello B2–A2.

### 2. Bars 61-65: the return follows the ♭VI–♭II–V7 model and the IV → sixth → V plan. No change.
Evidence: the D♭ middle section is IV of A♭. It leaves through G♭ (62 b1-2) and F♭ = E (62 b3, ♭VI) to A minor (63-64, ♭ii of A♭). A vii°7 over D follows (64 b3, movement I's stop chord), then the German sixth on F♭ (64 b4) and E♭7 (65-70). That is Verdi's ♭VI–♭II–V7–I close (Ex. 28-14, PDF 490). The book also says ♭II in major is "most easily approached through ♭VI" (Points 8, PDF 499). The whole span is the "tonicized IV through an augmented 6th to the home dominant" return (U29 §17, PDF 516-517, Ex. 29-28). The top voice drops ♭2̂ before 2̂ arrives: Fl 1 leaves A6 at 63 b2, Vln I drops A6 at 64 b1 (E6 only) and rests from 64 b3. So the book's warning against ♭2̂–♮2̂ in the soprano (PDF 484, 488) does not arise. Fix: none. This passage is protected (c076, c124, c262).

### 3. Bar 64 b4 → 65: the German sixth goes straight to V7. The 5ths are broken; the 7th is on top. Record only.
Evidence: cellos B2 → B♭2 on the last eighth anticipate 2̂ (B♭) and make a momentary French sixth, which is the book's "very frequent way of avoiding 5ths with the German 6th" (U29 §6, PDF 507, Ex. 29-11; c233). The all-pairs check finds no parallel 5ths. The augmented sixth F♭2–D4 contracts to the 7th: D4 → D♭4 in Vln II. A&S allow this "rather often, especially if the 7th is in an inner voice" (§15, PDF 515). Here D♭4 is the top note of the pp chord at 65, so the preferred case is not met. I do not propose a change. The D–D♭ step is the motto tail's ♯4̂–♮4̂ that horn 1 completes at 67-68 (E♭–D–D♭–C). The book accepts contraction because "the resolution is so strongly implied by context" (PDF 515). Bars 64 b4 and 65-70 are on the do-not-touch list (c232, c262). Fix: none.

### 4. Bar 64 b4 spelling: a V7 of ♭ii that functions as a German sixth. No change.
Evidence: under the A minor signature the chord reads E–G♯–B–D (+F from the trombone), V7 of A minor. In A♭ it works as F♭–A♭–C♭–D. A&S call the German 6/5 "enharmonically equivalent to ... V7 of ♭II". They note that composers sometimes notate the sixth as a dominant seventh for reading or for motivic links (§24, PDF 524-525, Ex. 29-39, 29-40). Here the motivic link is movement I's stop chord with G♯. The viola's G♯3 → G3 at 65 reads as a chromatic unison on one staff degree, which §19 (PDF 497) would avoid for passing tones. But that rule is about passing tones, and this is a chord tone in an enharmonic pivot. Respelling it A♭ would break the A minor chord's spelling. Fix: none (c262: respellings at 63 and 65 are for reading).

### 5. Bar 21: a French sixth reached by a chromatic voice exchange from IV, as in Example 29-14. No change.
Evidence: 21 b1 D♭ (IV) with Vln I F5. A passing I6/4 on b2 (bass E♭) leads to b3 F♭ A♭ B♭ D with Vln I D5: F and D♭ have exchanged places as D and F♭ (U29 §8, PDF 508-509, "frequently the voice exchange is filled in by a passing 6/4"). B♭ is held into 22 as the common tone (§6, PDF 507). D5 → E♭5 and F♭2 → E♭2 open to the octave (§1, PDF 503). The sixth stands just before the reprise at 23, which matches "just before important structural points" (PDF 503). Fix: none (protected by c152, c211, c233).

### 6. Bar 78: ♭VI after the deceptive cadence has no augmented sixth, correctly. No change.
Evidence: A&S say sixths "frequently follow deceptive cadences to VI (in major, to ♭VI), because they form a convincing transition to V" (§11, PDF 513). At 78 the music does not go to V. It falls F♭–E♭–D♭ to IV at the 79 peak, and V comes only at 81 after iiø7 (80 b3). A ♯4̂ (D♮) on 78 b1-2 would point at an E♭ chord that never comes as a goal. It would also take the F♭–E♭ semitone that the movement saves for its returns (21-22, 64-65, 78). The point stays rejected for the reason c232 gave. Fix: none.

### 7. Coda 83-92: no Neapolitan or ♭2̂ sixth. The plagal iv does this job. No change.
Evidence: A&S describe "♭II" as a neighbour chord over a tonic pedal in a coda "functions much like a IV used at a plagal cadence" (U28 §15, PDF 495, Ex. 28-20). They also describe a sixth on ♭2̂ as neighbour of a final tonic (U29 §22, PDF 522, Ex. 29-36). The coda already has the tonic pedal (83-87) and the minor iv (88: D♭ F♭ A♭) with D♭–C on top. That is the plagal close the cyclic rules require. A ♭2̂ (B♭♭) chord would add a second chromatic colour beside the F♭, and the last chord must stay a plain triad for movements III and IV (c262, c310, c312). Fix: none.

### 8. Chromatic spelling across the movement follows §17-21. No change.
Evidence: in A♭ the score uses ♭6̂ F♭ and ♭3̂ C♭ for mixture and E♮ only as a leading tone (20 b3, V of vi). Under the D♭ signature at 51 the cor anglais' seventh is G♭4 → F4, not F♯ (§20, PDF 498: "G♭, not F♯" when F is the goal). Sharps appear only under the F♯ minor and A minor signatures. Fix: none.
