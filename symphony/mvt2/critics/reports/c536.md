## Findings

The woodwind writing follows these two chapters almost everywhere. Five earlier passes on this reading fixed the larger points. One small change remains, and three points are recorded so the reasons are on file.

### 1. Bars 57-60: bassoon 2 has no breath for 15.7 s, p to f, before the ff at 61 (applied)

**Problem.** Bassoon 2 enters at 57 p (F3-G♭3 | B♭2-E♭3-A♭2-D♭3 | G♭2-G2 | A♭2) and crescendos to f at 59 and on through 60. Its only comma is at the end of 60. That is 15.7 s of growing low-register tone, followed by 8.9 s of ff at 61-62 with no rest.

**Evidence.** Adler says the oboe is the one woodwind that needs less breath than the others (PDF 175). He says nothing that exempts the bassoon. For the low double reed he warns that the low notes need a "firmer attack" (PDF 226) and that the lowest notes take "considerable" breath, so "periodic rests" are needed (PDF 230). His remedy for a line that must not stop is the dovetail: Brahms gives the held note to flute 2 "so that the first flute may breathe without diminishing the intensity" (PDF 188, Ex. VII-9). Here the bass clarinet plays the same line at the same pitch and breathes at the end of 58 (c456). c456 left bassoon 2 without a comma so that the two players do not breathe together. c476 kept that and added that "a bassoon uses less air than a bass clarinet in this register". The first reason is good, but it argues for a comma in a different place, not for none. The second reason is not supported by Adler, who names only the oboe as using less breath. The end of 57 is the natural seam: the line falls a major sixth, G♭3 to B♭2, and the bass clarinet holds the line across it. So the two players breathe one bar apart, and each breath is covered by the other player, as in Ex. VII-9. The basses also hold the bass line.

**Fix (applied).** In sections/bSec.ily, bSecBsnTwo:

```
\partCombineSoloII f2\p\< ges2 \breathe | bes,4 es4 aes,4 \tweak Stem.direction #UP des4 |
```

The comma prints at the end of 57 on the shared bassoon staff (bassoon 2 is alone there in solo mode) and in the Bassoon 2 part. The comma at the end of 60 stays. score.midi does not change.

### 2. Bar 77: flute 1 enters cold on G6 at mp (recorded, no change)

Flute 1 returns at 77 after resting since 63 and enters on G6 at mp, then falls by step. Adler calls the top fourth "most problematical" and says to approach high notes "by scale" (PDF 186). G6 is the lowest note of that top fourth, below the A6 where Adler says real difficulty starts. Clarinet 1 doubles the line an octave lower at the same mp. c417 kept the G6 on beat 1 for c341's violin voice leading. A professional flautist starts G6 at mp without trouble. No change.

### 3. Bars 82 and 87-92: soft releases by the double reeds and the contrabassoon (recorded, no change)

Oboe 1 and the cor anglais fade to niente at 82, the contrabassoon fades to niente on A♭1 at 82, and the cor anglais fades to niente on E♭4 at 90. Adler names the niente as the clarinet's speciality (PDF 211) and warns that the double reeds are hardest to control at the soft extreme (PDF 174-175, 198). None of these notes is in an instrument's lowest fifth. Each one is covered by another player on the same pitch or the same bass, which is Brahms's dovetail (PDF 188). At 82 the basses hold A♭1 into 83. At 89-90 clarinet 2 takes the cor anglais's E♭4 from niente while the cor anglais fades. c418 closed the 82 point. No change.

### 4. Bar 37: cor anglais written C6 (F5) for one eighth (recorded, no change)

The cor anglais's pickup at 36 runs to F5 (written C6) at 37 b1, an eighth, where flute 1 takes the theme. Adler's chart puts written C6 in the "thin, pinched" band where the cor anglais "loses its personality" (PDF 205). The note lasts an eighth and is the hand-over point, not a sustained tone (c271). No change.
