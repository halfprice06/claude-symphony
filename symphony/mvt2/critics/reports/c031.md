## What else I studied

I compared the movement with the Dvořák 9/ii Largo in the local corpus (`reference-corpus/mutopia/DvorakA_O95/Sym9/Sym9-lys/`, `woods.notesL.ily` and `strings.notesL.ily`). Two points from it matter here.

First, the reprise thins the violins step by step to "4 violins" (bar 101), then "2 violins con sordini" (105), then "1 violin" (110), and comes back to "tutti" at 112. Dvořák uses the solo violin as a stage in the theme's return.

Second, the middle theme (Fl/Ob, bar 46) opens with a triplet turn and a tied syncopation. The main theme (cor anglais, bar 7) opens with dotted eighth and sixteenth. The two themes are told apart first by their rhythm.

I also checked the full melodic text of every section file and looked at the engraved pages.

## Melodic verdict in brief

Theme A is the movement's strength. It has a rising arpeggio to a held fifth, the chromatic motto tail E♭–D–D♭–C, and an answering phrase that peaks on the B♭ appoggiatura. Its rhythm repeats enough to be learnt, and a listener could hum it. Theme B is weaker. Its head circles F–G♭–F–E♭ | D♭–C–D♭–E♭ in the dotted-quarter rhythm that also opens theme A and the b-phrase. Only its second phrase (A♭–D♭–F–G♭–A♭–B♭, 41–42) has a profile of its own. Most of the findings below improve the presentation of the tunes rather than rewrite them.

## Findings (ranked)

### 1. Bars 17–22: make the violin side of the b-phrase a solo violin, with "tutti" for the theme's return at 22 b3 (applied)

**Problem.** At 15–22 a single oboe trades two-bar cells with the whole first-violin section. A soloist answered by sixteen players does not read as a dialogue: the violins sound like an accompanying group that happens to have the tune. The section also enters with the a′ return at 22 b3, so the arrival of theme A in the violins (23) gets no contrast from what came just before. Every cell here is two bars long and ends on a half note plus a quarter, so the b-phrase relies on its colours to be remembered.

**Evidence.** Adler, p. 1 PDF 81: "When one solo violin is called for, the concertmaster will play the part". The book also says the solo violin "has also been greatly exploited as an occasional solo instrument in the orchestra". The example it gives is a slow movement: Brahms 1/ii mm. 91–105 (Ex. III-20, PDF 81–82), where the solo violin sings with oboe and horn above a light accompaniment. Dvořák 9/ii does the same thing in the passage this score is modelled on ("1 violin" at 110, "tutti" at 112). On a section the violins' first cell (A♭5–B♭5–G5–F5–E♭5–D♭5) is safe either way. Adler PDF 82–83 notes that a soloist is more exact than a section on shifts, and the cells sit in 4th–5th position on the A and E strings.

**Fix (applied in the scratch copy), `sections/aOne.ily`, `aOneVlnOne`:**
```
R2.*2 | aes''8(\p\<^\markup \italic "solo" bes''8 g''4 f''4 | es''2\> des''4)\! |
R2.*2 | f''8(\< g''8 es''4 d''4\! | es''2) es'8(\mp^\markup \italic "tutti, espr., sul D" f'8 |
```
The concertmaster ends the cell (E♭5, 22 b1–2) and plays the pickup with the section. No pitch, rhythm or dynamic changes. The viola answers and the Vln II louré (c017, c018) stay.

### 2. Bars 70 b3–74: Vln II "sul G" for the lower octave of the A′ theme, "ord." at 75 (applied)

**Problem.** Theme A is heard three times. The horn plays it at 7–14, the violins sul D (p, "warm and subdued") at 23–30, and the violins in octaves at 71–82. In the reprise only the dynamics are new: up to bar 75 the octaves are p, which is the same colour as 23 one octave higher. The great Romantic reprises re-colour the tune. Tchaikovsky, the example Adler prints, sends the violins to the G string, which is what turns a returning theme into an intense one.

**Evidence.** Adler PDF 74 describes the G string as "the thickest and most sonorous of the four… as one goes into its higher positions, the sound becomes very intense". The example is Tchaikovsky 5/ii mm. 111–19, the violins sul G in a slow movement (Ex. III-5, which I checked on the image: the line rises to about E5). Adler PDF 73 says a string should be marked only "for the desired timbre", which is the case here. Vln II's 70 b3–74 span is E♭4–E♭5, within the range of the Tchaikovsky example. F5 and B♭5 (75–76) are too high for the G string, so "ord." goes at 75, where the crescendo to the ff starts. Vln I's upper octave (A♭5–E♭6) stays on the A and E strings (PDF 75), so the reprise has a bright top over an intense lower octave. This is the opposite of the subdued sul D colour at 23, which c016 and c018 protect.

**Fix (applied):**
- `sections/retr.ily`, `retrVlnTwo`, bar 70: `r2 es'8(\p^\markup \italic "sul G" f'8 |`
- `sections/aTwo.ily`, `aTwoVlnTwo`, bar 75: `aes'4.\mp\<^\markup \italic "ord." c''8) f''4~ |`

Doublings, notes and texture in 71–82 are unchanged (do-not-touch respected).

### 3. Bars 37, 45, 53: give theme B's head its own rhythm, `2 8 8 4` instead of `4. 8 4 4` (applied)

**Problem.** Theme A opens `4. 8 4~`, the b-phrase opens `4. 8 4` (15), and theme B opens `4. 8 4 4` (37, 45, 53). The movement's three melodic ideas start with the same rhythmic cell. Theme B is also built on neighbour notes, so after the movement a listener keeps theme A and confuses B with it. In Dvořák 9/ii, the model, the main theme (bar 7, dotted eighth and sixteenth) and the middle theme (bar 46, triplet turn and tied syncopation) differ in rhythm from their first beat. That rhythmic difference is the main reason the middle theme is remembered as a second tune.

**Evidence.** The reading does not discuss rhythm in a melody. The finding rests on the corpus comparison above and on the score itself. It does connect to Adler's examples of singing string lines that start on a held note: Brahms 3/iii (Ex. III-7) and Tristan (Ex. III-59) both begin with a sustained tone before they move.

**Fix (applied in the scratch copy), `sections/bSec.ily`:** same pitches, new rhythm. The head now starts on a held F5 (D♭5 at 45), and bars 1–2 become the mirror rhythms `2 8 8 4 | 4 8 8 2`.
```
% bSecFluteOne, 37
f''2(\p^\markup \italic "dolce cantabile" ges''8 f''8 es''4 | des''4 c''8 des''8 es''2) | ...
% bSecVlnOne 45 and bSecVc 45 (tenor clef)
des''2(\p es''8 des''8 c''4) | ...        des'2(\p^"arco" es'8 des'8 c'4) | ...
% bSecVlnOne and bSecVlnTwo, 53
f''2(\p\<^\markup \italic "sul A" ges''8 f''8 es''4) | ...
```
The neighbour G♭ (E♭ at 45) now falls on beat 3 as an accented neighbour over the tonic. The second phrase (41, 57) keeps its `4 4 4. 8` rise, so the dotted figure remains in the theme, and the augmented head at 61 is not affected. Composer's choice: if you want to keep the 37 head as it was, apply at least 53, where the violins have it under the animando.

### 4. Bars 58, 61–62, 76, 79: the melodic high point is reached before the climaxes it should crown (not applied)

**Problem.** The movement's top pitch, B♭6, comes at 58 (Vln I and Fl 1, an eighth-note upper neighbour under the crescendo), at 76 (a quarter-note appoggiatura, mp→f), and at 79 (held, ff). Two consequences:

(a) The B section's ff arrival at 61–62 tops out at D♭6 and F6, a sixth below what the line reached three bars before at mf. The pitch high point and the dynamic high point do not coincide.

(b) The A′ peak at 79 repeats the B♭6 of 76, so it arrives as the second statement of a note the ear already has. The reading supports this concern: Adler PDF 72 and 75 say the top E-string register is "difficult to control" and "strident" when forced. A note in that register keeps its effect only if it is saved for one place.

**Fix (option, for the composer).** (b) is protected (theme A melody, B♭ appoggiatura, octave doubling 75–82), so I do not propose touching 76–79. For (a), divide Vln I at 61–62 so that the ff arrival reaches the top and prefigures the B♭–A♭ of 79–80:
```
% bSecVlnOne 61-62 (replaces the non-div. double stop of c024; its notes stay in the lower half)
<< { aes'''1 | bes'''2 aes'''2 } \\ { <f'' des'''>1 | f'''2 fes'''2 } >>  % "div."
```
A♭6 is the fifth of D♭. B♭6 is the third of G♭(maj7) and A♭6 the third of F♭, which becomes G♯ for the E6 at 63. Adler PDF 76: passages too hard for one player "are quite simple played divisi". This partly undoes cycle 24's double stop, so I list it as an option only.

### 5. Theme A is never sung by the cellos or by violas with cellos (observation, not applied)

**Problem.** Adler calls the cello's D string "the most musically captivating string… warm and lyrical" (PDF 99). Among viola uses he prints the viola–cello coupling of Beethoven 5/ii, a slow-movement theme marked p dolce (Ex. III-39, PDF 91). In this movement the cellos have a fine counter-melody (23–30, 71–78) and double other lines (45–47, 57–60), but they never have theme A. Every statement of the tune lies between A♭3 and B♭6 in horn or violins.

**Fix.** I do not see a place to do it without breaking a protected texture (7–14 horn, 23–30 plain statement, 31–36 bass-clarinet moment, 71–82 A′, coda exchange). I record it for the composer's overall view. If the coda is ever reopened, a cello statement of the head (A♭3–C4–E♭4 on the D string) would be the place to consider.
