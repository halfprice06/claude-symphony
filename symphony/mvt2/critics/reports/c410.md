## Findings

Ranked by how much it would matter if the point were wrong. Findings 1 and 3–4 are verification and need no change; Finding 2 is a text correction for coherence/mvt2.md.

### 1. Both joins still match the other movements at pitch (bars 1–2, 89–92). No change.

**Reading.** PDF 587: in a common-tone modulation "the common tone will appear in an exposed position, usually in the soprano", and with two major triads it "will also contain a chromatic inflection". Points for Review 2 (PDF 603) names C major → A♭ major as the typical case.

**Evidence.** Movement I v421 ends (coda.ily lines 32, 61, 75, 88, 96) with Hn 3 C4, Vln I C5, Vln II G4, violas E4 and cellos C3 under fermatas with \laissezVibrer, and prints "attacca". v410 bar 1 takes exactly these five notes with \repeatTie at ppp; the common tone C5 stays on top, and the violas' E4→E♭4 at bar 2 is the inflection. So this is the reading's own example, with its two features. At the other end, the last chord keeps C5 on top (cyclic rule) and A♭4 in Vln II. The scherzo v400 opens with Hn 1 G♯4 alone, pp, under a fermata (scherzo.md line 7), so the common tone is exposed in the next movement, as Widmung re-enters its held A♭ as G♯ alone at p after the ritard. (Ex. 32-3). Cl 2's E♭4 is the D♯ the scherzo needs (PDF 597, Haydn's D♯/E♭). The four theme records all pass, so nothing either neighbour quotes from II has moved.

**Fix.** None.

### 3. The remote areas and the coda still tie the movement to the motto, so its place between I and III holds (37, 47–50, 62–65, 85–89). No change.

**Reading.** PDF 600–601: key areas "project over a long span of time those pitch relationships that are most characteristic of the piece". PDF 602: without its motivic link a remote area "would lack any organic connection". Review 5 (PDF 603): the usual way to keep unity is to tie remote areas to the motivic design.

**Evidence.** Each chromatic area carries a piece of movement I: F♯ minor (47–50) carries the S head once, in the cor anglais; A minor (63–64) carries the P head once, in Tbn 1; the D♭ section enlarges the motto tail's passing D♭ (8, 24, 33), and the coda closes it with D♭–C on top (Cl 1 87–89). Bar 87 is movement I's Neapolitan sixth read as IV6 of A♭, and its F–F♭–E♭ line is the major-third step (A♭ → E) that the scherzo takes next. So the movement looks back to I in its middle and forward to III at its end, which is the right role for a slow movement in a cyclic symphony. The grep found the S-head rhythm only at 47–49 and no C-major material after bar 1, and the motto tail appears at 8, 24, 32–34, 67–69, 72 and 85–89, as recorded.

**Fix.** None.

### 4. ♭VI at symphony level reaches its goal by equal division, not by a cadential V; keep it that way. No change.

**Reading.** PDF 593: "The usual goal of ♭VI is a cadential V". PDF 599–600: equal divisions of the octave can replace that motion, as in the Appassionata (A♭–E–C–A♭) and, in Tchaikovsky 4/i, at some cost to "a large-scale tonic-dominant relationship".

**Evidence.** Movement II is ♭VI of C; the scherzo is E (III♯) and the finale C. The symphony therefore uses the Appassionata's major-third chain rather than ♭VI → V. Inside movement II the usual route is kept (♭VI → ♭II → V at 62–65), so the cycle's unusual plan is offset by a regular one inside the movement. The finale's opening German sixth on A♭ (mvt2.md line 165) then gives the large-scale ♭VI its augmented-sixth resolution (PDF 593: "A most natural way of leading ♭VI to V is to transform it into an augmented 6th chord"). That is why the last chord must stay a plain A♭ triad, with no added sixth or seventh.

**Fix.** None. Do not colour the last chord.
